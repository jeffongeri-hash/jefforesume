Salivary & Gastric Secretion

Digestion's opening moves — and the three-phase gastric secretory sequence that starts before food ever reaches the stomach.

Salivary Secretion

Produced by three paired major glands (parotid — serous/enzymatic, submandibular — mixed, sublingual — mucous-predominant) under near-exclusive autonomic control — unusually, both sympathetic and parasympathetic stimulation increase salivary flow (parasympathetic produces copious watery secretion; sympathetic produces a smaller, more protein-rich secretion) rather than opposing each other as they typically do elsewhere in the gut.

ComponentFunction
Salivary amylaseBegins starch digestion (limited physiologic significance given brief oral transit time, but re-activated transiently in the stomach before gastric acid inactivates it)
Lingual lipaseBegins minor triglyceride digestion, more functionally relevant in neonates than adults
BicarbonateNeutralizes oral/esophageal acid exposure — part of the natural defense against reflux-related esophageal injury
Mucins, IgA, lysozymeLubrication and antimicrobial defense

Salivary secretion is unusual among GI secretions for being hypotonic relative to plasma — ductal cells reabsorb Na⁺/Cl⁻ more than they secrete K⁺/HCO₃⁻, and because ductal water permeability is low, the fluid isn't fully re-equilibrated to isotonicity the way most other GI secretions are.

This three-phase sequencing is why vagotomy (historically used for refractory peptic ulcer disease before modern acid suppression made it largely obsolete) reduced acid output substantially — the cephalic phase, and much of the gastric phase's vagal amplification, depends entirely on intact vagal transmission.

The Mucus-Bicarbonate BarrierWhy the Stomach Doesn't Digest Itself

Surface mucous cells secrete a viscous, adherent mucus layer with an embedded, unstirred bicarbonate gradient — luminal pH can be 1–2 while the epithelial surface directly beneath the mucus layer stays near neutral. This barrier depends heavily on prostaglandin E2 (COX-1-derived) for both mucus and bicarbonate secretion and for maintaining adequate mucosal blood flow — the direct mechanistic link to NSAID-induced peptic ulceration: non-selective COX inhibition removes this protective prostaglandin signal, thinning the barrier and leaving the epithelium exposed to standard luminal acid that it would otherwise tolerate without injury. This is why NSAID-induced ulcers reflect a barrier failure more than an acid-output problem, and why co-prescribed PPIs (reducing the acid load the weakened barrier must withstand) and misoprostol (a prostaglandin E1 analog directly replacing the lost protective signal) both work, via different points in the same mechanism.

Pepsinogen & Intrinsic FactorThe Chief Cell's Two Jobs

Pepsinogen

Secreted as an inactive zymogen by chief cells — activated to pepsin only on contact with acid (autocatalytic, then pepsin itself activates more pepsinogen) — a protective sequencing that keeps the enzyme inactive until it reaches the acidic environment it needs both to activate and to function.

Intrinsic factor

Secreted by parietal cells (the same cell making acid — see GI Atlas Vol. 1 Stomach section for the acid-secretion convergence), essential for B12 absorption in the terminal ileum (covered in depth in this volume's Vitamin & Mineral Absorption section). This shared cellular origin is exactly why parietal cell autoimmune destruction (atrophic gastritis/pernicious anemia — Vol. 1) knocks out acid secretion and B12 absorption simultaneously.

Vol. 2 · Region 2 · Exocrine Pancreas

Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion

Two cell types with two completely different jobs, one CFTR-powered bicarbonate pump, and a zymogen activation cascade that has to stay switched off until the exact right moment.

Two Cell Types

Acinar cells

Secrete digestive enzymes (as inactive zymogens) and a small volume of NaCl-rich fluid. Driven primarily by CCK (see GI Atlas Vol. 1, Small Intestine — Hormones section) acting on CCK1 receptors, with cholinergic/vagal input providing additional drive.

Duct cells

Secrete the bicarbonate-rich fluid that carries the enzymes out and neutralizes gastric acid. Driven primarily by secretin (Vol. 1) acting via cAMP — though duct cells comprise only ~5% of pancreatic mass, they're responsible for essentially all the fluid volume in pancreatic juice.

This division mirrors the parietal-cell/chief-cell split introduced in the Salivary & Gastric Secretion section — different cells producing the "vehicle" (fluid/bicarbonate) versus the "cargo" (active substance) of a secretion, a recurring architectural pattern in GI exocrine glands.

CFTR is fundamentally a chloride channel, but in the distal pancreatic duct — where luminal chloride is already low — it functions in tight coupling with SLC26 exchangers as a functional bicarbonate conduit, secreting bicarbonate directly and/or providing the chloride that SLC26 exchanges for luminal bicarbonate. Secretin drives this entire cascade via cAMP-mediated CFTR activation — a direct, mechanistic link between the hormone covered in Volume 1 and the channel covered here.

Why the alkaline fluid matters beyond neutralizing acid
The bicarbonate-rich fluid isn't just protective against acid — it's the hydraulic and pH mechanism that flushes zymogens out of the ductal tree and maintains the alkaline environment digestive enzymes need to function once activated in the duodenum. This is why CFTR dysfunction in cystic fibrosis causes pancreatic damage even though the defect is in fluid/electrolyte transport, not enzyme production directly — inadequately hydrated, viscous secretions plug the ducts, causing proximal acinar atrophy and fibrosis from obstruction rather than from any primary enzyme synthesis defect.

Enteropeptidase (formerly "enterokinase"), anchored to the duodenal brush border, is the deliberate single trigger point for the entire cascade — trypsin, once formed, activates chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase, and even more trypsinogen, so the whole enzyme complement switches on together, but only once secretions reach the duodenum, never inside the pancreas itself.

Hereditary PancreatitisPRSS1 (cationic trypsinogen) gain-of-function
Autosomal dominant

Mutations that either increase trypsinogen's autoactivation rate or impair trypsin's normal self-degradation safety mechanism allow premature intrapancreatic trypsin activation — the zymogen cascade above firing inside the gland instead of the duodenum, causing recurrent acute pancreatitis from childhood and markedly increased lifetime pancreatic cancer risk. A clean demonstration of what happens when the "stay off until the duodenum" rule fails at its very first step.

Disease Correlations

Cystic Fibrosis — Pancreatic ManifestationsCFTR · 7q31
Autosomal recessive

~85% of CF patients have exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (severe CFTR genotypes) — thick, poorly hydrated secretions obstruct ducts, causing progressive acinar atrophy and fibrosis in utero or early in life. Diagnosed functionally via low fecal elastase-1. The remaining ~15% with milder/residual-function genotypes retain enough duct function to avoid overt insufficiency but remain at elevated pancreatitis risk from the same underlying bicarbonate-secretion defect operating at lower severity — and CF-related diabetes (from progressive endocrine islet damage secondary to the same fibrotic process) is a distinct, common late complication.

Chronic Pancreatitis — Exocrine InsufficiencyAcquired — most commonly alcohol-related
Acquired

Progressive acinar loss eventually drops enzyme output below the threshold needed for adequate digestion (roughly <10% of normal enzyme output before steatorrhea becomes clinically apparent — the pancreas has substantial functional reserve, which is why exocrine insufficiency is often a late finding in chronic pancreatitis). Treated with pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, which must be acid-protected (enteric-coated) to survive gastric transit before releasing enzymes in the duodenum — directly exploiting the same pH-triggered activation logic as endogenous zymogen activation.

Biliary Secretion & Enterohepatic Circulation

Bile acids are recycled with remarkable efficiency — roughly 95% of each cycle's worth is reclaimed and reused, which is exactly why interrupting that recycling is a viable drug strategy.

Bile Acid Synthesis

Synthesized in hepatocytes from cholesterol via the rate-limiting enzyme CYP7A1 (cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase) — the primary bile acids (cholic acid, chenodeoxycholic acid) are then conjugated with glycine or taurine (improving water solubility and preventing passive reabsorption too early in the intestine, keeping them functional through the full length of small bowel where fat digestion occurs) before secretion into bile canaliculi.

~95% of the bile acid pool is reclaimed each cycle via active transport at the terminal ileum (the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter, ASBT) — the body cycles its relatively small total bile acid pool (~2–4g) through this loop several times per meal rather than synthesizing fresh bile acids each time, since de novo synthesis is comparatively slow and metabolically costly. Only the ~5% lost to fecal excretion needs fresh synthesis to maintain pool size — and that small obligate loss is actually the primary route by which the body eliminates excess cholesterol, since bile acids are cholesterol-derived.

BilirubinThe Other Biliary Cargo

Unconjugated bilirubin (from heme breakdown, bound to albumin in circulation) is taken up by hepatocytes and conjugated with glucuronic acid via UGT1A1 — conjugation is what makes bilirubin water-soluble enough to excrete in bile. In the distal gut, bacterial enzymes deconjugate and reduce a portion of biliary bilirubin to urobilinogen, some of which is reabsorbed (a bilirubin-specific enterohepatic circulation, parallel to but distinct from the bile acid cycle above) and excreted renally, while most continues to stercobilin, giving stool its characteristic color — the mechanistic reason biliary obstruction (blocking bilirubin's entry into the gut entirely) produces pale, clay-colored stool.

Disease Correlations

Bile Acid MalabsorptionTerminal ileal disease/resection (Crohn's, short bowel), or primary ASBT dysfunction
Acquired (common) or rare congenital

Loss of the terminal ileal reabsorption step (see GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Hormones section for the same segment's role in GLP-1/GLP-2/PYY production) sends excess bile acids into the colon, where they directly stimulate colonic secretion and motility — a genuine, underrecognized cause of chronic secretory diarrhea, especially post-cholecystectomy or in Crohn's disease involving the terminal ileum. Treated with bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine, colesevelam) — which bind bile acids in the lumen, directly removing the diarrhea-triggering substance rather than treating the diarrhea symptomatically.

Gilbert SyndromeUGT1A1 (reduced-activity promoter variant)
Autosomal recessive (variably expressed)

Mildly reduced UGT1A1 conjugation activity (~30% of normal) causes intermittent, mild unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia — typically triggered by fasting, illness, or stress. Benign, requiring no treatment; important mainly as a common (~5–10% of the population) cause of otherwise-unexplained isolated indirect hyperbilirubinemia that shouldn't trigger an extensive hepatobiliary workup once recognized.

Cholesterol GallstonesMultifactorial — bile acid/lecithin/cholesterol ratio imbalance
Multifactorial

Bile acids and lecithin (phospholipid) solubilize cholesterol in bile by forming mixed micelles — when cholesterol secretion exceeds the solubilizing capacity of the available bile acid/lecithin pool (from obesity, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, or reduced bile acid pool size), cholesterol precipitates and crystallizes, seeding stone formation. This is a direct application of the same bile acid physiology covered above: gallstone risk is fundamentally a ratio problem between the pool this section describes and the cholesterol it's asked to carry.

Vol. 2 · Region 3 · Small Intestine — Carbohydrate & Protein

Carbohydrate & Protein Absorption

Three named transporters move essentially all dietary sugar across the brush border — and the same sodium-glucose cotransport principle underlies oral rehydration therapy.

Carbohydrate DigestionBreaking Polysaccharides Down to Monosaccharides

Salivary and pancreatic amylase break starch down to disaccharides/oligosaccharides (maltose, maltotriose, α-limit dextrins) — but absorption requires the final step down to monosaccharides, carried out by brush border enzymes anchored directly on the enterocyte apical membrane: lactase (lactose → glucose + galactose), sucrase-isomaltase (sucrose → glucose + fructose; also handles the α-limit dextrins from amylase digestion), and maltase-glucoamylase (maltose → glucose).

The Three Transporters

Oral rehydration therapy is this mechanism, deployed clinically
SGLT1's Na⁺-glucose cotransport remains intact even during severe secretory diarrhea (cholera being the classic teaching example) where nearly every other absorptive/secretory process is deranged — this is precisely why oral rehydration solution is formulated with a specific glucose:sodium ratio rather than water and salt alone: the glucose is there to drive sodium (and the water that follows it osmotically) absorption via SGLT1, not for caloric content. WHO-formulated reduced-osmolarity ORS (~245 mOsm/L, glucose:sodium roughly 1:1 molar ratio) is the current standard, refined from earlier, higher-osmolarity formulations to reduce stool output while maintaining equivalent rehydration efficacy.

Protein Absorption

Pancreatic proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidases — see this volume's Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion section for their activation cascade) break dietary protein down to free amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides. Unlike carbohydrates, protein absorption doesn't require complete digestion to single amino acids first — the brush border PepT1 transporter absorbs di- and tripeptides directly (using a H⁺ gradient rather than Na⁺), which are then hydrolyzed to free amino acids intracellularly by cytoplasmic peptidases. Free amino acids are absorbed via multiple Na⁺-dependent amino acid transporters with overlapping substrate specificity (neutral, acidic, basic amino acid-preferring systems) — the redundancy across several transporter families is a meaningful safeguard, since loss of any single transporter rarely causes complete malabsorption of its substrate class.

Disease Correlations

Lactose IntoleranceLactase (LCT) — age-related decline (primary) or acquired (secondary)
Common — majority of the world's adult population has some degree of primary lactase decline

Undigested lactose reaches the colon intact, where bacterial fermentation produces gas (bloating, flatulence) and osmotically active fermentation products draw water into the lumen (osmotic diarrhea). Secondary lactase deficiency — transient, following any process that damages the brush border (viral gastroenteritis, celiac disease, prolonged NPO status) — reflects lactase's position as the brush border enzyme with the most superficial location and slowest regeneration, making it disproportionately vulnerable to any mucosal insult compared to sucrase-isomaltase or maltase-glucoamylase.

Congenital Sucrase-Isomaltase DeficiencySI gene
Autosomal recessive

Presents in infancy specifically with sucrose/starch introduction (breast milk lactose is tolerated normally, since lactase is a separate enzyme) — chronic osmotic diarrhea and failure to thrive triggered by the specific dietary transition that introduces the enzyme's substrate.

Hartnup DiseaseSLC6A19 (neutral amino acid transporter)
Autosomal recessive

Loss of the neutral amino acid transporter (including tryptophan) at both the intestinal brush border and renal tubule causes combined malabsorption and urinary wasting of neutral amino acids. Tryptophan loss specifically reduces niacin synthesis substrate (the same tryptophan-to-niacin pathway from carcinoid syndrome in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Colon section) — producing a pellagra-like presentation (photosensitive rash, ataxia) via transporter loss rather than tumor-driven tryptophan diversion, a genuinely useful parallel to keep in mind alongside the carcinoid mechanism.

Vol. 2 · Region 3 (cont.) · Small Intestine — Lipid Handling

Fat Absorption & Fat-Soluble Vitamins

The most logistically complex absorptive process in the gut — requiring bile acids, pancreatic lipase, and a dedicated intracellular reassembly line all working in the correct sequence.

The Full Sequence

  1. Emulsification — bile acids (this volume's Biliary Secretion section) break large fat globules into smaller droplets, increasing surface area for enzymatic access.
  2. Enzymatic digestion — pancreatic lipase (requiring colipase, also pancreatic-secreted, to displace bile acids from the droplet surface and anchor lipase there) hydrolyzes triglycerides to monoglycerides and free fatty acids.
  3. Micelle formation — bile acids solubilize these digestion products (plus cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins) into mixed micelles, water-soluble enough to cross the unstirred water layer and reach the brush border — the same bile acids from Section 3, now doing a second, distinct job beyond their role in emulsification.
  4. Uptake — monoglycerides and fatty acids diffuse from the micelle across the enterocyte apical membrane (passive for the most part, with some transporter-mediated contribution); the bile acids themselves are left behind in the lumen to form new micelles repeatedly, only being reabsorbed much further downstream at the terminal ileum (Section 3).
  5. Intracellular reassembly — inside the enterocyte, free fatty acids and monoglycerides are re-esterified back into triglycerides, then packaged with cholesterol and apolipoproteins into chylomicrons.
  6. Lymphatic export — chylomicrons are too large for capillaries and are instead exported via intestinal lacteals into the lymphatic system, entering systemic circulation via the thoracic duct — the one major absorptive pathway in the gut that bypasses the portal vein entirely.

Fat-Soluble VitaminsRiding the Same Micelle

Vitamins A, D, E, and K require this exact same bile-acid-dependent micellar solubilization to be absorbed — any process that disrupts bile acid delivery (biliary obstruction, bile acid malabsorption from this volume's Biliary Secretion section) or pancreatic lipase output (chronic pancreatitis, CF — this volume's Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion section) causes combined fat-soluble vitamin deficiency alongside steatorrhea, not an isolated problem. Vitamin K deficiency specifically (impaired coagulation factor synthesis) is often the first clinically apparent sign in an otherwise occult fat malabsorption process.

Disease Correlations

AbetalipoproteinemiaMTTP (microsomal triglyceride transfer protein)
Autosomal recessive

MTTP is required to load triglyceride onto apoB inside the enterocyte during chylomicron assembly — without it, absorbed fat can enter the cell but cannot be packaged for export, accumulating as lipid droplets within enterocytes (visible on biopsy) while chylomicrons never reach circulation. Presents with severe fat malabsorption, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency (particularly vitamin E, causing a progressive spinocerebellar neurodegeneration if untreated), and acanthocytosis (abnormally shaped red cells from altered membrane lipid composition).

Steatorrhea — Localizing the CauseMultiple possible sites of failure along the sequence above

Because fat absorption requires so many sequential steps, steatorrhea itself doesn't localize the problem — pancreatic enzyme deficiency (Section 2), bile acid deficiency/biliary obstruction (Section 3), and primary mucosal disease (celiac disease — this volume's Vitamin & Mineral Absorption section) can all produce an identical clinical and stool-fat picture. Distinguishing them requires targeted testing (fecal elastase for pancreatic function, imaging/labs for biliary obstruction, duodenal biopsy for mucosal disease) rather than the presence of steatorrhea alone.

Vol. 2 · Region 3 (cont.) · Small Intestine — Micronutrients

Vitamin & Mineral Absorption

B12's absorption pathway is the single most multi-step, failure-prone process in this entire volume — and iron absorption is gated by a hormone most people have never heard of.

Vitamin B12A Four-Step Relay, Any Link of Which Can Fail

  1. Release from food — gastric acid and pepsin free B12 from dietary protein (impaired by achlorhydria, PPI use, atrophic gastritis).
  2. R-protein (haptocorrin) binding — salivary R-protein binds free B12 in the acidic stomach, protecting it from acid degradation.
  3. Intrinsic factor handoff — pancreatic proteases degrade R-protein in the alkaline duodenum, releasing B12 to bind intrinsic factor (secreted by gastric parietal cells — this volume's Salivary & Gastric Secretion section) instead — impaired by exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (Section 2) or by intrinsic factor deficiency itself (pernicious anemia, GI Atlas Vol. 1 Stomach section).
  4. Terminal ileal receptor-mediated uptake — the B12-intrinsic factor complex binds cubam (cubilin/amnionless receptor complex) specifically in the terminal ileum — impaired by ileal resection, Crohn's disease, or the rare congenital cubam defect (Imerslund-Gräsbeck syndrome).
Why B12 deficiency takes years to manifest
The liver stores several years' worth of B12 (unusually large reserve compared to almost every other vitamin) — this is why B12 deficiency from any of the four failure points above presents so much later than deficiencies of other vitamins with minimal body stores, and why a single normal B12 level doesn't fully exclude early malabsorption if body stores haven't yet been depleted.

Once absorbed, B12 circulates bound to transcobalamin II for cellular delivery — a separate transport protein from the haptocorrin/intrinsic factor machinery that gets it across the gut wall in the first place.

Hepcidin, produced by the liver in response to rising iron stores and inflammation, binds and degrades ferroportin — the only known cellular iron exporter, present on duodenal enterocytes and macrophages alike — directly throttling how much absorbed iron actually reaches the bloodstream versus staying trapped inside the enterocyte to be sloughed off with normal cell turnover. This is the master regulatory brake explaining why iron absorption is deliberately limited even in the face of adequate dietary intake, and why chronic inflammation (via inflammation-driven hepcidin elevation) causes the functional iron restriction seen in anemia of chronic disease despite normal or even elevated total body iron stores.

Disease Correlations

Celiac DiseaseHLA-DQ2/DQ8 association; gluten (gliadin peptide)-triggered
Autoimmune, strong genetic predisposition

Gliadin peptides, deamidated by tissue transglutaminase, trigger an HLA-DQ2/DQ8-restricted T-cell response causing villous atrophy — flattening the absorptive surface responsible for essentially every nutrient covered in this volume, though iron, folate, and calcium (all absorbed predominantly in the proximal small bowel, exactly where celiac damage is most severe) are disproportionately affected compared to B12 (absorbed much further downstream in the relatively spared terminal ileum). Diagnosed via anti-tissue transglutaminase IgA (with total IgA to exclude selective IgA deficiency causing a false-negative), confirmed by duodenal biopsy; treated with strict gluten exclusion, which allows villous recovery over months.

HFE mutations impair the normal signal that should raise hepcidin in response to rising iron stores — hepcidin stays inappropriately low, ferroportin remains active, and iron absorption continues unchecked despite iron overload, progressively depositing in liver, heart, pancreas, and joints. A direct failure of the hepcidin-ferroportin brake described above, in the opposite direction from anemia of chronic disease's inflammation-driven excess.

X-linked recessive

Impaired intestinal copper export (the copper analog of ferroportin's iron-export role) traps copper within enterocytes instead of delivering it to the circulation — severe systemic copper deficiency presenting in infancy with connective tissue abnormalities (copper is a required cofactor for lysyl oxidase, needed for collagen/elastin crosslinking) and progressive neurodegeneration (copper-dependent enzymes in the CNS).

Vol. 2 · Region 4 · Colon — Water & Electrolytes

Colonic Water & Electrolyte Absorption

By the time chyme reaches the colon, roughly 90% of the fluid load has already been absorbed upstream — the colon's job is fine-tuning the last small fraction, and the secretory/osmotic diarrhea distinction hinges entirely on how that fine-tuning fails.

The Numbers

Of roughly 9L of fluid entering the GI tract daily (2L ingested + ~7L of salivary/gastric/pancreatic/biliary/intestinal secretions — nearly every section of this volume contributing its own share), the small intestine absorbs ~7–8L, leaving only ~1–2L reaching the colon, which absorbs all but ~100–200mL destined for stool. This means the colon operates as a final, high-efficiency concentrating step rather than the primary site of fluid absorption — most volume-based diarrhea reflects an upstream (small intestinal) process overwhelming this back-end capacity, not a colonic problem per se.

MechanismElectrogenic Sodium Absorption

Colonic absorption relies heavily on electrogenic Na⁺ absorption via apical ENaC channels (the same channel family covered in the endocrine atlas's RAAS/aldosterone discussion) — aldosterone directly upregulates colonic ENaC expression, meaning colonic sodium/water absorption is under genuine endocrine control, not just passive concentration-gradient physics. Water follows the absorbed sodium osmotically, without a dedicated water channel doing most of the work (unlike the renal collecting duct's aquaporin-driven system).

Short-Chain Fatty AcidsA Second, Bacteria-Dependent Absorptive Pathway

Colonic bacteria ferment unabsorbed carbohydrate (dietary fiber, resistant starch, and — as covered in this volume's Carbohydrate & Protein Absorption section — any lactose that escaped small intestinal digestion) into short-chain fatty acids (acetate, propionate, butyrate). These are absorbed by colonocytes and serve two roles: a direct energy source for the colonic epithelium itself (butyrate specifically is the colonocyte's preferred fuel) and an additional avenue for colonic sodium/water absorption independent of the ENaC pathway above. This is part of why a colon that's lost its normal bacterial fermentation capacity (post-antibiotic dysbiosis, for instance) can develop impaired fluid absorption distinct from any electrolyte-transporter defect.

Why this framework is worth internalizing as a default lens
Nearly every disease correlation introduced across this entire volume — and several from GI Atlas Vol. 1 — ultimately expresses itself clinically as one of these two diarrhea patterns. Learning to sort a new presentation into "persists with fasting" (secretory — look for a hormone-secreting tumor, bile acid malabsorption, or a toxin-mediated secretory process) versus "resolves with fasting" (osmotic — look for a malabsorbed sugar or an unabsorbed osmotic laxative) is a genuinely transferable diagnostic habit built directly from the physiology in this file.

Cholera ToxinThe Classic Secretory Mechanism

Vibrio cholerae toxin ADP-ribosylates the Gs alpha subunit in colonic and small intestinal enterocytes, permanently activating adenylyl cyclase — the resulting sustained cAMP elevation locks open the same CFTR chloride channel introduced in this volume's Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion section (CFTR is expressed throughout the GI epithelium, not just the pancreas), driving massive, unregulated chloride and water secretion. This is the toxin-mediated extreme of the secretory diarrhea mechanism — a hijacked version of the exact same channel and second-messenger system used physiologically for normal secretin-driven pancreatic bicarbonate secretion, redirected toward catastrophic fluid loss instead of controlled digestive function.

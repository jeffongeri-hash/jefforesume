Diabetes in Pregnancy — Full Management

Vol. 1 covered GDM screening. This is everything after the diagnosis: targets, when to add pharmacotherapy, which agent, and how pregestational diabetes changes the calculus.

Step 1 — Medical Nutrition Therapy

First-line for every GDM diagnosis: carbohydrate-controlled nutrition therapy plus 30 minutes/day of moderate exercise. This controls glucose adequately in roughly 60–70% of women with GDM within 1–2 weeks. If fasting or postprandial targets are missed on self-monitoring with reasonable consistency during that window, move to pharmacotherapy.

Step 2 — Pharmacotherapy: Insulin vs. Oral Agents Tier A

Where ACOG and SMFM genuinely disagree

ACOG (Practice Bulletin 190, reaffirmed) and the ADA designate insulin as first-line pharmacotherapy — it doesn't cross the placenta, and it has the longest safety track record. SMFM's separate statement considers metformin a reasonable first-line alternative given comparable glycemic efficacy in trials. This is a genuine, acknowledged difference in expert interpretation of the same evidence, not a case of one body being "wrong" — know both positions and your own institution's default.

AgentKey evidence points
InsulinDoes not cross the placenta; ~100 years of safety experience; most flexible titration; downside is injections and cost/access
MetforminCrosses the placenta; associated with less maternal weight gain and less neonatal hypoglycemia than insulin in trials; monotherapy fails in 14–46% of patients, requiring insulin supplementation; long-term offspring metabolic/neurodevelopmental data still limited
GlyburideCrosses the placenta more than previously assumed; not found non-inferior to insulin on a composite of neonatal hypoglycemia, macrosomia, and hyperbilirubinemia in head-to-head data; ACOG/ADA state it should not be first-choice pharmacotherapy; ~23% monotherapy failure rate

Starting Insulin — A Practical Regimen

  • Isolated fasting hyperglycemia: bedtime intermediate-acting (NPH) or long-acting basal insulin, starting low (e.g., 0.2 units/kg) and titrating every few days against fasting values.
  • Isolated postprandial hyperglycemia: rapid-acting insulin before the offending meal(s), dosed to the specific postprandial pattern.
  • Both: basal-bolus regimen. Total daily dose requirements typically rise as pregnancy progresses due to increasing insulin resistance — expect to titrate upward through the third trimester, not just at initiation.

Pregestational Diabetes — What's Different

  • Preconception counseling is the single highest-yield intervention: periconceptional glycemic control correlates directly with congenital anomaly risk, since major organogenesis (weeks 3–8) often precedes the first prenatal visit.
  • Baseline retinopathy and nephropathy screening; retinopathy can progress during pregnancy and needs dilated exam each trimester in established disease.
  • Tighter targets and closer monitoring than GDM given higher baseline risk; earlier and more frequent antenatal surveillance.
  • Higher baseline risk of preeclampsia, macrosomia, and stillbirth compared with GDM — this population gets the earliest and most intensive antenatal testing protocols (Vol. 1, Module 3 concepts, applied earlier and more often).

Obesity in Pregnancy — Full Management

Vol. 1 covered the risk profile and weight-gain targets. Here: what changes practically in how you manage the pregnancy.

Compounding Risk, Not Just Additive

Obesity doesn't just add to GDM and preeclampsia risk independently — it interacts with them. A patient with obesity and GDM has materially higher macrosomia and cesarean risk than either factor alone, which is why obesity-plus-GDM patients typically get the most aggressive glycemic management and earliest surveillance in Module 1's framework.

Practical Management Adjustments

  • Anesthesia planning: early antenatal anesthesia consultation is worth arranging proactively — difficult neuraxial access and airway considerations are better addressed before an emergent intrapartum need arises than during one.
  • VTE prophylaxis dosing: standard fixed-dose LMWH prophylaxis under-doses higher body weights; weight-based dosing (or higher fixed doses at higher BMI tiers) is increasingly standard rather than a flat dose for everyone.
  • Oxytocin dosing: emerging data suggest higher oxytocin doses may be needed to achieve adequate uterine contractility in patients with obesity, plausibly related to reduced myometrial contractile response — worth knowing if labor augmentation seems to be underperforming at standard doses.
  • Ultrasound limitations: anatomic survey sensitivity for fetal structural anomalies decreases with increasing body habitus — this is a real imaging limitation, not just a documentation caveat, and is worth disclosing directly when counseling on scan results.
  • Wound complications: higher post-cesarean wound infection and separation risk — meticulous closure technique and, in some protocols, subcutaneous closure/negative pressure dressings for higher BMI tiers.

Prior Bariatric Surgery

A frequently missed detail Tier B

Patients with a history of malabsorptive bariatric surgery (Roux-en-Y gastric bypass in particular) may not tolerate a standard oral glucose tolerance test — the glucose load can trigger dumping syndrome (flushing, tachycardia, GI symptoms) that's uncomfortable and can produce misleading results. Alternative GDM screening approaches (home glucose monitoring profiles rather than a bolus OGTT) are reasonable in this population — worth asking about bariatric history specifically at intake rather than assuming a negative answer if unasked. Nutritional deficiency screening (B12, iron, folate, fat-soluble vitamins) is also warranted given malabsorption risk.

Don't wait for culture results to start empiric therapy in a symptomatic patient — treat empirically, then adjust to sensitivities. Obtain a test-of-cure culture 1–2 weeks after completing treatment.

Recurrent UTI — Suppressive Prophylaxis

After 2 or more episodes of cystitis/ASB, or after a single episode of pyelonephritis, start daily suppressive prophylaxis for the remainder of pregnancy: nitrofurantoin 100mg or 50mg nightly, or cephalexin 250–500mg nightly (stop cephalexin ~4 weeks before delivery per some protocols; stop nitrofurantoin as term approaches per the standard near-term contraindication above).

Pyelonephritis — Inpatient Management

Pregnancy-specific severity: pyelonephritis in pregnancy carries a meaningfully higher risk of progressing to sepsis and ARDS than in non-pregnant patients — this is an inpatient, IV-antibiotic diagnosis, not an outpatient one.

  • Empiric IV options: ceftriaxone, cefepime, ampicillin + gentamicin, or aztreonam (penicillin allergy) — guided by local resistance patterns.
  • Transition to oral therapy once afebrile and clinically improved for 24–48 hours, completing a total 10–14 day course.
  • Suppressive prophylaxis for the remainder of pregnancy after any pyelonephritis episode (above).
Don't forget the GBS connection

Any GBS bacteriuria detected at any point in pregnancy — regardless of colony count — requires treatment at diagnosis and intrapartum GBS antibiotic prophylaxis in labor, independent of the 36–37 week culture result from Vol. 1.

Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy

Vol. 1 covered how to recognize a hypertensive emergency. This is ongoing management — chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, and the trial that overturned decades of "don't treat mild hypertension" teaching.

Classification

  • Chronic hypertension — predates pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks
  • Gestational hypertension — new-onset after 20 weeks, without proteinuria or severe features
  • Preeclampsia — new hypertension after 20 weeks plus proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction (full criteria in Vol. 1, Module 6)
  • Chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia — new proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction developing on top of pre-existing chronic hypertension

The CHAP Trial — Chronic Hypertension and Pregnancy Tier A

Tita et al. (NEJM, 2022) randomized 2,408 women with mild chronic hypertension (<160/110) before 23 weeks to active treatment (BP goal <140/90) versus control (treatment withheld until severe range, ≥160/105).

30.2% vs 37.0%
Primary composite outcome (severe preeclampsia, medically indicated preterm birth <35wk, abruption, fetal/neonatal death) — active vs control
11.2% vs 10.4%
Small-for-gestational-age — essentially no difference, addressing the old fear
24.4% vs 31.1%
Any preeclampsia — active vs control
Why this overturned decades of practice

The old concern — that tighter BP control would reduce placental perfusion and cause growth restriction — was the entire rationale for "letting mild chronic hypertension ride" until it became severe. CHAP showed treating to <140/90 improved outcomes without increasing SGA. ACOG and SMFM now recommend 140/90 as both the initiation threshold and treatment goal for chronic hypertension in pregnancy — a direct practice change from this single trial.

Typical approach: start one first-line agent, titrate to at least mid-to-high range dosing before adding a second agent from a different class, rather than combining two agents at low doses early.

Absolutely contraindicated

ACE inhibitors and ARBs — teratogenic, associated with fetal renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, and skull ossification defects, particularly with 2nd/3rd trimester exposure. Stop and switch before conception when possible in a patient with chronic hypertension planning pregnancy.

Acute Severe Hypertension (≥160/110) — Emergency Treatment

1
IV labetalol 20mg, doubling every 10 min (20→40→80→80mg) up to a max cumulative dose, or IV hydralazine 5–10mg every 20 min.
2
Oral immediate-release nifedipine 10mg is an effective, fast-acting oral alternative when IV access is delayed — repeat in 20 min if needed.
3
Goal: BP <160/110 within 30–60 minutes to reduce stroke risk — severe hypertension is a genuine acute stabilization priority, not a "wait for the next scheduled reading" situation.

Cardiac Disease & Peripartum Cardiomyopathy

Cardiac conditions and cardiomyopathy together account for roughly a fifth of U.S. pregnancy-related deaths (Vol. 1). The core challenge: normal pregnancy physiology (Vol. 1, Module 1) mimics early heart failure symptom-for-symptom.

Peripartum Cardiomyopathy (PPCM)

LVEF <45%
Diagnostic threshold, new-onset, no other identified cause
1 in 900–4,000
U.S. incidence — and rising, plausibly from increasing maternal age and multifetal pregnancy via ART
4×
Higher incidence in Black women vs. white women — a disparity that persists independent of socioeconomic factors

Onset window: classically the last month of pregnancy through 5 months postpartum, though earlier and later presentations are increasingly recognized. It's a diagnosis of exclusion — no cardiac history, no other identifiable cause of the systolic dysfunction.

The Diagnostic Challenge — Red Flags vs. Normal Pregnancy Discomfort

Normal 3rd-trimester complaintRed flag suggesting PPCM instead
Mild dyspnea on significant exertionDyspnea at rest, or disproportionate to activity level
Positional discomfort lying flatTrue orthopnea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea — waking gasping for air
Mild dependent ankle edemaRapid weight gain with edema, or edema disproportionate to gestational stage
FatigueNew cough, hemoptysis, chest pain, palpitations, or syncope — none of these are "normal pregnancy"

Any combination of these red flags warrants an echocardiogram and NT-proBNP — a low threshold for cardiac workup in this population costs little and catches a condition that's otherwise missed until decompensation.

Management & Prognosis

  • Multidisciplinary cardio-obstetric team management — obstetrics, cardiology, MFM, anesthesia — is the standard of care, not a nice-to-have.
  • Guideline-directed heart failure therapy adapted for pregnancy/lactation safety (ACE inhibitors/ARBs are avoided antepartum for the same fetal reasons as Module 4, but are generally compatible postpartum and with breastfeeding — a genuine antepartum/postpartum distinction worth knowing).
  • Recovery is genuinely possible and not rare: roughly 50–70% of patients recover LVEF to >50% within 6–12 months. Even patients presenting with a very low initial EF can fully recover — initial severity doesn't reliably predict the ceiling of recovery, which matters for how you counsel a newly diagnosed patient.
  • Mode of delivery is generally guided by obstetric indications, not cardiac status alone, in a hemodynamically stable patient — vaginal delivery with a well-managed second stage (to minimize the hemodynamic load of prolonged pushing) is often preferred over routine cesarean.
  • Recurrence risk in a future pregnancy is directly tied to degree of LVEF recovery — patients with incomplete recovery face substantially higher recurrence and worse outcomes with subsequent pregnancy, making pre-conception cardiology counseling essential before any future pregnancy.

Perinatal Mental Health & Substance Use Disorder

The single largest underlying-cause category in the Vol. 1 maternal mortality data. Screening and appropriate treatment are genuinely protective interventions here — this module leans into both.

Universal Screening — ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 4

  • Screen everyone receiving prepregnancy, prenatal, or postpartum care for depression and anxiety using a standardized, validated tool — at the initial prenatal visit, again later in pregnancy, and again postpartum.
  • Validated instruments: Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) — 10 items, validated across pregnancy and postpartum, score ≥10–13 (threshold varies by validation study) flags for further assessment — or PHQ-9.
  • Screen for bipolar disorder before starting pharmacotherapy for depression/anxiety if not previously assessed — starting an antidepressant alone in unrecognized bipolar disorder risks precipitating mania.
  • Any affirmative answer to a self-harm or suicide item requires immediate assessment of acuity and severity, with risk-tailored management arranged before the patient leaves — this is not deferred to a follow-up appointment.
If a patient screens positive for suicidal ideation

Assess acuity directly (intent, plan, access to means) rather than avoiding the topic — direct questions do not increase risk and reliably improve your ability to triage appropriately. Arrange same-day psychiatric evaluation for anyone with an active plan or intent; for lower-acuity passive ideation, ensure close follow-up, safety planning, and a warm handoff to behavioral health rather than a referral slip alone. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline is an appropriate resource to provide directly to any patient in this situation.

Pharmacotherapy for Perinatal Depression Tier A

The most significant recent shift: earlier guidance reviewed medication safety data without firmly recommending pharmacotherapy; current guidance actively recommends it as an effective, appropriate first-line option — a real-world RCT found SSRI pharmacotherapy achieved EPDS score improvement below threshold in 45% of patients at 4 weeks versus 20% with supportive "listening visits" alone.

  • Sertraline is generally preferred first-line — most extensive safety data, favorable breastfeeding profile, high remission rates. Escitalopram is a reasonable alternative for patients with no prior antidepressant history.
  • Paroxetine carries a signal for fetal cardiac malformation with first-trimester exposure in some studies — generally avoided as an initial choice in pregnancy, though the absolute risk increase is small and this is not an indication to stop it abruptly in someone already stable on it without a real discussion of relative risk.
  • The risk/benefit framing that matters for counseling: untreated perinatal depression itself carries real risks — to bonding, to obstetric engagement, to the pregnancy, and to the mother directly — so "avoiding medication" is not a risk-free default option; it's a different risk profile, not a safer one.
  • New as of October 2025: ACOG issued specific clinical practice guidance on zuranolone and brexanolone, the newer rapid-acting neurosteroid-based treatments specifically approved for postpartum depression — worth reviewing directly given how recent this guidance is.

Substance Use Disorder — Opioid Use Disorder in Pregnancy Tier A

The single most important principle in this section

For opioid use disorder in pregnancy, medication for opioid use disorder (buprenorphine or methadone) is the recommended treatment — not medically supervised withdrawal. Withdrawal is associated with relapse rates ranging from 59% to over 90%, and relapse carries far higher maternal and fetal risk than continued, medically managed opioid agonist therapy. This reverses an intuitive but harmful instinct ("get her off opioids entirely") that remains common outside addiction-informed obstetric care.

  • Both agents are ACOG-endorsed, safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and should be continued postpartum — the postpartum period is a documented higher-risk window for return to use given sleep deprivation, new caregiving demands, and (unfortunately) custody-related fear that can itself discourage disclosure and engagement.
  • Co-management with an addiction medicine-experienced provider is standard; appropriate 42 CFR Part 2-compliant consent is required to share information between obstetric and addiction treatment providers.
  • Universal screening (not just for patients who "look like" they use substances) plus brief intervention and referral to treatment (SBIRT) improves both maternal and infant outcomes — and a positive screen or drug test alone does not equal a diagnosis or mandate a particular response; approach and consent matter for keeping patients engaged in care rather than driving them away from it.
  • Depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, and PTSD are all more common in patients with OUD — screen for co-occurring conditions rather than treating OUD in isolation.

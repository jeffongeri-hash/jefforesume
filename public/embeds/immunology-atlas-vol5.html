01 · Solid Organ Transplant

Rejection: Four Mechanisms, Four Timelines

The timeline alone narrows the mechanism before a single lab result comes back.

“Three years out from a kidney transplant with a slowly rising creatinine — is this even rejection, or something else entirely?”

Figure 1. Four rejection mechanisms, roughly ordered by how soon after transplant each becomes possible.
TypeMechanismED-relevant presentation
HyperacutePre-formed donor-specific antibodies bind graft endothelium immediately on reperfusionNow rare given routine pre-transplant crossmatching; if it occurs, it's recognized in the OR, not the ED
Acute cellularT-cell mediated direct attack on graft tissue — the most common rejection typeOften subclinical, found on protocol biopsy; when symptomatic: graft tenderness/swelling (kidney), organ dysfunction, low-grade fever
Acute antibody-mediatedDe novo donor-specific antibodies develop post-transplantSimilar presentation to cellular rejection but generally more treatment-resistant; often found on biopsy with C4d staining
ChronicProgressive fibrosis and vasculopathy from cumulative immune injury over timeSlowly rising creatinine (kidney), progressive dyspnea/reduced ejection fraction (heart), or insidious organ dysfunction over months to years; largely irreversible
The ED's actual job with suspected rejection is rarely to diagnose it definitively — that requires biopsy and specialist interpretation. The job is to recognize the pattern (unexplained graft-organ dysfunction in a transplant recipient), rule out the mimics that are actually treatable emergently (infection, drug toxicity, obstruction/vascular complication), and get the transplant team involved quickly.
Verify before use: rejection presentation varies significantly by organ (kidney, liver, heart, lung each have different typical signs) — this table is a general framework, not organ-specific detail.
02 · Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplant

Graft-versus-Host Disease

The mirror image of rejection — here, the transplanted immune system is the one doing the attacking.

After allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT), the transplanted donor immune cells can recognize the recipient's tissue as foreign and attack it — the reverse direction of solid organ rejection. Acute GVHD classically targets three organs — skin, liver, and the GI tract — and while it was traditionally defined as occurring within the first 100 days post-transplant, the modern view recognizes meaningful overlap in timing with chronic GVHD. Chronic GVHD behaves more like a multi-organ autoimmune/ sclerodermatous disease and carries its own distinct, broader set of manifestations.

Acute GVHD staging (MAGIC criteria)

Figure 2. Acute GVHD is staged independently in each of three organs; the overall grade takes the worst of the three.
The clinical picture to recognize: a maculopapular rash (often starting on palms/soles/ears before spreading), high-volume watery or bloody diarrhea, and rising bilirubin in a patient who had an allogeneic stem cell transplant, typically in the first weeks to months post-transplant. Any one of these findings in that context should raise GVHD, and the degree of each (per the staging above) drives the overall severity grade and treatment intensity.

Chronic GVHD — a different disease pattern

Chronic GVHD manifests less like an acute inflammatory attack and more like an acquired multi-organ autoimmune syndrome: sclerodermatous skin changes, dry eyes/mouth (sicca syndrome), bronchiolitis obliterans in the lungs, and liver involvement resembling chronic cholestasis. It carries its own long-term morbidity independent of the original transplant indication, and its management (extended immunosuppression, often for years) is a major driver of the chronic infection risk discussed in Volume II.

Verify before use: acute GVHD staging criteria have been revised over time (Glucksberg → IBMTR → MAGIC) and different transplant centers may still reference different systems — confirm which staging system your institution/transplant service uses.

Calcineurin Inhibitors (Tacrolimus, Cyclosporine)

The backbone of most regimens — and the class most likely to be the reason a stable transplant patient suddenly isn't.

ToxicityDetail
NephrotoxicityDose-related, often reversible early; harder to distinguish from rejection without a level and, sometimes, a biopsy
NeurotoxicityRanges from tremor to PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome) — see below
MetabolicHyperkalemia, hypertension, hyperglycemia (more pronounced with tacrolimus)

PRES — the calcineurin inhibitor emergency worth recognizing by name

Figure 4. Recognizing calcineurin-inhibitor-associated PRES.
Case pattern

Two weeks after a kidney transplant on a tacrolimus-based regimen, a patient develops headache, blurred vision, and a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure. Blood pressure is elevated but not dramatically so. MRI shows vasogenic edema concentrated in the posterior (occipito-parietal) white matter. This is the classic presentation of calcineurin-inhibitor-associated PRES — note that it can occur as early as the first 1–2 weeks post-transplant, doesn't require severe hypertension to occur, and is generally reversible with blood pressure control and reducing, holding, or switching the causative agent (often tacrolimus to cyclosporine, " "or a dose reduction).

Myth: “normal or only mildly elevated blood pressure makes PRES unlikely.” Hypertension is common in CNI-associated PRES but not universal — don't let a reassuring blood pressure talk you out of the diagnosis in a transplant patient with new headache, seizure, visual change, or altered mental status.

Diarrhea in a transplant patient is a genuine three-way differential: mycophenolate toxicity, GVHD (in the HSCT population), and infection (including CMV colitis) can all present similarly — the workup (and the urgency) differs substantially depending on which one it is, so this symptom alone shouldn't be assumed to be a drug side effect without at least considering the other two.
Verify before use: specific target trough levels, dose adjustments, and toxicity management belong to the transplant team — this section is oriented toward ED recognition of toxicity patterns, not dosing.
04 · Practical Synthesis

The ED Approach: Rejection, Infection, or Toxicity?

Every new symptom in a transplant patient is one of three things until proven otherwise — and the three have very different next steps.

“New fever and malaise in a transplant patient — rejection, infection, or a drug problem, and where do I even start?”

PossibilityFavors thisInitial ED move
RejectionOrgan-specific dysfunction (rising creatinine, falling ejection fraction, rising LFTs) without a clear infectious or toxic explanationTrend the organ-specific marker, involve transplant team, avoid assuming without ruling out the other two first
InfectionFever, localizing symptoms, timeline consistent with the post-transplant infection risk window (see Volume II)Cultures before antibiotics when feasible, but don't delay empiric coverage in a sick-appearing patient — the same urgency principles from Volume II apply
Drug toxicitySymptom pattern matches a known toxicity of the patient's specific regimen (see tables above); recent dose change or new interacting medicationCheck a drug level if applicable (tacrolimus, cyclosporine), review the med list for new interacting drugs (azoles, macrolides especially)
Practical synthesis

Get a precise transplant history fast: organ, time since transplant, current immunosuppression regimen and any recent changes, and baseline organ function (a baseline creatinine or ejection fraction changes everything about how to interpret today's number). Cross-reference the timeline framework from Volume II — how long ago the transplant happened narrows the infectious differential meaningfully. Involve the transplant team early; they carry both the patient's specific history and the standing relationship to guide a rejection-vs-infection-vs-toxicity call that's genuinely difficult to make from a single ED visit. When in doubt and the patient looks unwell, treat empirically for infection while the rest of the workup proceeds — undertreating a real infection " "in an immunosuppressed patient is the higher-consequence error of the three possibilities.

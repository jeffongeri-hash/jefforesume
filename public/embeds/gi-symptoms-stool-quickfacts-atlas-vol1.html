The Bristol Stool Chart & Stool Characteristics

A 7-point scale that's really a transit-time proxy in disguise — every type maps directly onto how long stool sat in the colon before it was passed.

The Chart

TypeDescriptionTransit time / meaning
1Separate hard lumps, like nuts — hard to passSlowest transit — stool has spent the longest in the colon, maximal water reabsorption
2Sausage-shaped but lumpySlow transit, mild constipation
3Sausage shape with cracks on the surfaceNormal — the firmer end of the healthy range
4Smooth, soft sausage or snakeIdeal — the reference "normal" stool
5Soft blobs with clear-cut edgesLacking fiber, or the faster end of normal transit
6Mushy consistency with ragged edgesFaster transit, mild diarrhea
7Watery, no solid pieces, entirely liquidFastest transit — minimal time for colonic water reabsorption (GI Atlas Vol. 2)
Why this chart works mechanistically
Every step from Type 1 to Type 7 tracks directly with how much time colonic water-reabsorption machinery (GI Atlas Vol. 2's colonic water/electrolyte section) had to act on the stool before it was passed — slow colonic transit means more time for water to be pulled out, producing harder, more fragmented stool (Types 1–2); fast transit means less water reabsorption time, producing looser or entirely liquid stool (Types 6–7). The chart is really a bedside proxy for transit time that doesn't require an actual transit study.

Clinical Use

Types 1–2, persistent: suggests slow-transit constipation or dyssynergic defecation (GI Atlas Vol. 3) — worth distinguishing which, since treatment differs completely Types 6–7, persistent: diarrhea — triggers the classification framework in this atlas's Diarrhea section Used as a standardized outcome measure in IBS trials and clinical practice (IBS-C vs. IBS-D subtyping, GI Atlas Vol. 3, is partly defined using this scale) A single abnormal reading matters less than a sustained pattern — normal stool form varies day to day even in healthy people

Beyond FormOther Normal Stool Characteristics

CharacteristicNormalWorth noting
FrequencyAnywhere from 3x/day to 3x/week is considered within normal rangeFrequency alone, without a change from the individual's own baseline, is a poor marker of pathology
OdorPresent but not extreme — from bacterial fermentation byproducts (GI Atlas Vol. 2)Markedly foul, greasy odor suggests fat malabsorption (this atlas's diarrhea section)
Floating vs. sinkingEither can be normal, largely reflecting gas contentConsistently floating, greasy, hard-to-flush stool suggests steatorrhea — worth distinguishing from simple gas-related floating

Stool Color Differential

Normal brown color depends on an intact bilirubin pathway (GI Atlas Vol. 2) — nearly every color deviation traces back to a specific, identifiable break in that pathway or a specific ingested substance.

Why Stool Is Brown At All

Normal brown color comes from stercobilin, the end product of bacterial bilirubin metabolism in the gut (GI Atlas Vol. 2's Biliary Secretion section) — bacteria deconjugate and reduce biliary bilirubin through urobilinogen down to stercobilin. Any disruption to this pathway, or any sufficiently pigmented ingested substance passing through unabsorbed, changes stool color in a predictable, mechanistically traceable way.

The Differential

ColorMechanismCauses
Pale / clay-colored (acholic)Bilirubin never reaches the gut at all — biliary obstruction (GI Atlas Vol. 5's cholecystectomy section) prevents stercobilin from ever formingCholedocholithiasis, pancreatic head mass compressing the common bile duct (GI Atlas Vol. 5's Whipple section), cholangiocarcinoma, severe hepatocellular disease impairing bilirubin conjugation/excretion
Black, tarry, foul-smelling (melena)Blood degraded by gastric acid and gut bacteria over a long transit distance — requires roughly 50–100mL of blood and several hours of transit time to develop the characteristic tar-like appearanceUpper GI bleeding (this atlas's dedicated Upper GI Bleeding section) — the transit distance/time requirement is exactly why melena localizes a bleed to the upper GI tract or proximal small bowel rather than the colon
Black, non-tarry, odorlessIngested pigment passing through without any blood-degradation process at allIron supplementation, bismuth subsalicylate (GI Atlas Vol. 4) — a genuinely important distinction from true melena, since these substances turn stool black through direct pigmentation rather than blood breakdown, and a guaiac/FIT test can help distinguish true melena from this benign mimic when the history is ambiguous
Bright red (hematochezia)Blood that hasn't been degraded — either a lower GI source, or an upper GI source bleeding briskly enough that transit time was too short for melena to developCovered in depth in this atlas's Blood in Stool section
GreenBiliverdin (the precursor bilirubin is reduced from) hasn't been fully converted before transit completes — rapid transit doesn't give bacteria enough time to finish the full stercobilin conversion pathwayAny cause of accelerated transit (this atlas's Diarrhea Classification section), green leafy vegetable or dye-heavy food intake, iron supplementation in some cases
Yellow, greasy, foul-smellingFat malabsorption — undigested fat itself, plus altered bacterial fermentation from unabsorbed nutrients reaching the colonExocrine pancreatic insufficiency (GI Atlas Vol. 2), celiac disease (GI Atlas Vol. 2), bile acid deficiency/biliary obstruction — the full malabsorptive diarrhea differential in this atlas's next section
White/barium-coloredRecent barium contrast studyShould resolve within days; a benign, easily explained finding when the history is known

The One Combination Worth Memorizing

Pale stool + dark urine + jaundice
This triad specifically indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinemia with an obstructive/cholestatic pattern — bilirubin that made it through hepatic conjugation but can't reach the gut lumen backs up into blood (jaundice) and spills into urine (dark urine, since conjugated bilirubin is water-soluble and renally excreted, unlike unconjugated bilirubin), while its absence from the gut leaves stool pale. This combination should prompt urgent evaluation for biliary obstruction rather than being treated as three separate, unrelated findings.

Blood in Stool — Types, Localization & Differential

Combine the blood's appearance with the stool's consistency and you get a genuinely useful bedside differential — hard stool with blood tells a different story than loose stool with blood.

Three Types of GI Bleeding, by Appearance

TypeAppearanceTypical source
MelenaBlack, tarry, foul-smellingUpper GI (esophagus, stomach, duodenum) or proximal small bowel — see this atlas's Stool Color Differential section for the mechanism
HematocheziaBright or dark red blood, often mixed with or coating stoolClassically lower GI (colon, rectum, anus), but a brisk upper GI bleed can present this way too if transit is fast enough — GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas's Lower & Occult GI Bleeding section covers distinguishing the two
Occult bloodNot visible — detected only by FIT/guaiac testingAny source, any rate — the diagnostic entry point for unexplained iron deficiency anemia (also covered in the GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas)

The Combination That MattersBlood + Stool Consistency

Pairing the blood's appearance with the stool's consistency (Bristol type, this atlas's first section) narrows the differential meaningfully — this is a genuinely practical bedside heuristic, not just a physiology curiosity.

PatternMost likely causeWhy
Hard stool (Bristol 1–2) + bright red blood, often on the surface or on toilet paper, with painAnal fissureHard, constipated stool mechanically tears the anal mucosa on passage — the pain (often described as sharp, tearing, lasting after defecation) plus the blood being on rather than mixed into the stool both point to a local, distal source rather than a more proximal one

Normal-formed stool + blood mixed throughout (not just on the surface)More proximal colonic source — diverticular bleeding, angiodysplasia, or a mass lesionBlood genuinely mixed into rather than coating the stool suggests it entered the stool stream higher up, with more transit time/mixing than a distal anorectal source would allow
Bloody diarrhea, chronic/recurrent, with weight lossIBD (GI Atlas Vol. 4) or colorectal malignancyChronicity and systemic features (weight loss, night symptoms) shift the differential away from the typically acute/self-limited causes toward something requiring endoscopic evaluation regardless of how the bleeding itself appears
This heuristic narrows the differential — it doesn't replace exam or endoscopy
A patient can have more than one process at once (hemorrhoids and an unrelated proximal source, for instance) — these patterns are a reasonable way to generate and prioritize a differential at the bedside, not a substitute for direct visualization (anoscopy, colonoscopy) when the diagnosis genuinely matters, particularly in anyone with risk factors for malignancy or when bleeding doesn't fit the benign pattern cleanly.

Red Flags That Override Any Reassuring Pattern

Age >50 with new bleeding and no prior colonoscopy Weight loss, night sweats, or other systemic symptoms Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD Iron deficiency anemia (this atlas's Stool Color Differential section touches the mechanism; the GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas covers the full workup) Change in bowel habit accompanying the bleeding, rather than bleeding alone

Diarrhea Classification & Pathophysiology

Four mechanisms, each with its own defining bedside test — this framework has been referenced throughout the whole atlas series and is finally assembled here in one place.

The Four-Way Framework

TypeMechanismFasting testStool osmotic gap
SecretoryActive secretion of fluid/electrolytes exceeds absorptive capacity, or absorption itself is actively impaired (GI Atlas Vol. 2)Persists — the driving mechanism doesn't depend on food intakeLow (<50 mOsm/kg)
OsmoticUnabsorbed osmotically active solute in the lumen draws water in passivelyResolves — removing the causative solute (by fasting) fixes itHigh (>100 mOsm/kg) — unmeasured osmoles present
InflammatoryMucosal damage/inflammation disrupts normal absorption and drives active secretion, often with blood/mucusVariable — often persists given the ongoing mucosal processVariable
Malabsorptive (fatty)Failure to digest/absorb specific nutrients (usually fat) — the malabsorbed nutrient itself acts osmoticallyOften improves with fasting or with removing the specific malabsorbed nutrientHigh, with a positive fecal fat stain

SecretoryCauses, Assembled From Across This Atlas Series

VIPoma (GI Atlas Vol. 1) — the textbook example Carcinoid syndrome (GI Atlas Vol. 1) Cholera toxin — permanently activates CFTR/cAMP (GI Atlas Vol. 2) Bile acid malabsorption (GI Atlas Vol. 2/4) High-output ileostomy (GI Atlas Vol. 5) Congenital chloride/sodium diarrheas (rare)

Osmotic

Lactose intolerance (GI Atlas Vol. 2) — the classic example Other carbohydrate malabsorption (fructose, sorbitol/sugar alcohols) Osmotic laxatives — PEG, lactulose, magnesium salts (GI Atlas Vol. 4) Celiac disease can have an osmotic component from unabsorbed carbohydrate, alongside its malabsorptive features

Inflammatory

IBD — Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis (GI Atlas Vol. 4) Infectious colitis — Shigella, Salmonella, Campylobacter, C. difficile (GI Atlas Vol. 4/5b) Ischemic colitis (GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas) Microscopic colitis (lymphocytic/collagenous) — a notable exception where colonoscopy appears grossly normal despite genuine inflammatory diarrhea, diagnosed only on biopsy

Fecal calprotectin and fecal lactoferrin are the key bedside-accessible markers distinguishing inflammatory from non-inflammatory diarrhea — elevated levels reflect neutrophilic mucosal inflammation and support pursuing endoscopic evaluation, while normal levels make significant inflammatory disease considerably less likely without ruling it out entirely.

MalabsorptiveSteatorrhea Specifically

Exocrine pancreatic insufficiency — chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis (GI Atlas Vol. 2) Celiac disease (GI Atlas Vol. 2) Bile acid deficiency / biliary obstruction (GI Atlas Vol. 2/5) Short bowel syndrome (GI Atlas Vol. 1/5b) SIBO (GI Atlas Vol. 3) — bacterial bile acid deconjugation impairs micelle formation

The stool itself often gives this one away before any test does: foul-smelling, greasy, difficult to flush, sometimes visibly floating (this atlas's Bristol Stool Chart section) — a fecal fat stain (Sudan III) or quantitative 72-hour fecal fat collection confirms it when the clinical picture is ambiguous.

Practical Bedside Approach

  1. Acute (<2 weeks) and otherwise well: usually infectious, self-limited, supportive care.
  2. Bloody, febrile, or systemically unwell: think inflammatory — stool studies (culture, C. diff, calprotectin) and consider empiric evaluation for IBD if chronic/recurrent.
  3. Chronic (>4 weeks), watery, persists with fasting: think secretory — hormone-driven causes deserve consideration once infection is excluded.
  4. Chronic, related to specific foods, improves with fasting: think osmotic — a food/symptom diary and consideration of lactose/fructose intolerance is reasonable before extensive testing.
  5. Greasy, foul, floating, with weight loss: think malabsorptive — pursue the specific pancreatic/mucosal/biliary cause rather than treating the diarrhea symptomatically.

Constipation — Combination Pharmacology Strategies

GI Atlas Vol. 4 covered each laxative class individually — this section is about how they're actually stacked together in practice, since real-world constipation management is rarely a single-agent affair.

The Standard Stepwise Approach

  1. Fiber first (psyllium, methylcellulose — GI Atlas Vol. 4) — the most physiologic starting point, but requires adequate concurrent water intake and can worsen bloating in slow-transit constipation or significant dysmotility (GI Atlas Vol. 3) if added alone without addressing the underlying transit problem.
  2. Add an osmotic agent (polyethylene glycol — "MiraLAX" is the common brand name patients recognize) if fiber alone is inadequate — this is the single most common two-drug combination in real-world constipation management, and deliberately so: fiber increases stool bulk and softness mechanically, while PEG independently draws water into the lumen osmotically. The two mechanisms are complementary rather than redundant, which is exactly why they're commonly used together rather than one being simply "upgraded" to the other.
  3. Add a stimulant (senna, bisacodyl) for breakthrough or more resistant constipation, generally used intermittently/as-needed on top of a fiber+osmotic base rather than as chronic daily monotherapy.
  4. Escalate to a secretagogue (lubiprostone, linaclotide, plecanatide — GI Atlas Vol. 4) for refractory constipation or IBS-C specifically, particularly when a chloride-channel-driven mechanism might address pain as well as stool frequency.
  5. Consider prucalopride (5-HT4 agonist, GI Atlas Vol. 4) for chronic idiopathic constipation refractory to the above, directly targeting motility rather than just stool water content.

Why Fiber + PEG Specifically Makes Mechanistic Sense

Two independent mechanisms, not overlapping ones
Fiber works by adding non-digestible bulk that mechanically stimulates peristalsis via distension (GI Atlas Vol. 4) — a largely mechanical effect. PEG works by remaining non-absorbable in the lumen and osmotically drawing water in — a purely osmotic effect, independent of bulk. Because the two mechanisms don't compete for the same physiologic lever, combining them is genuinely additive rather than simply doubling up on one approach — this is part of why this specific combination is so commonly reached for as a first-line pairing rather than choosing one or the other.

Matching the Combination to the Cause

Practical Combination Pitfalls

Fiber without adequate fluid intake can worsen constipation rather than help it — mechanical bulk without enough water to soften it Chronic stimulant laxative monotherapy (without an osmotic/fiber base) tends to require escalating doses over time in some patients — better used intermittently on top of a maintenance regimen than as the sole daily agent Magnesium-based osmotic agents require caution in renal impairment (GI Atlas Vol. 4) — PEG is generally preferred in this population for the same osmotic effect with less systemic absorption risk Escalating pharmacologic combinations without ever considering dyssynergic defecation (this section's table above) is a common, avoidable reason for "refractory" constipation that isn't actually refractory to the right treatment

GI Quantitative Quick-Facts

The actual numbers behind commonly repeated GI trivia — including a widely-cited figure that a peer-reviewed remeasurement has since corrected.

LengthCorrecting the "16 Miles" Claim

The GI tract is not 16 miles long — not close
16 miles would be over 84,000 feet. The actual total length of the adult human GI tract, mouth to anus, is approximately 16–18 feet (5 meters) by careful modern measurement — some commonly cited lay sources round this up to 25–30 feet using older cadaveric measurements (which tend to overestimate length, since post-mortem loss of muscle tone lets the bowel stretch out more than it sits in a living person). Either way, this is nowhere near 16 miles — that figure appears to be a genuine popular myth with no anatomic basis, possibly a garbled conflation with the intestinal surface area discussion below.
SegmentLength
Esophagus~10 inches (25 cm)
StomachVariable with distension; roughly 10 inches (25 cm) unfilled
Small intestine (duodenum + jejunum + ileum)~20–22 feet (6–7 meters) — the longest single segment, though shorter than commonly quoted maximalist figures
Large intestine (colon + rectum)~5 feet (1.5 meters)
Total, mouth to anus~16–18 feet (5 meters) by careful modern measurement; individual variation is considerable

Surface AreaThe "Tennis Court" Claim Also Needs Correcting

A genuinely useful example of a textbook figure being revised
For decades, textbooks (and this atlas series' own earlier volumes, following that same convention) have cited the small intestine's absorptive surface area as ~250–300 m² — "the size of a tennis court" — a figure based on relatively crude morphometric assumptions. A more careful 2014 remeasurement study, directly examining biopsy morphometry rather than relying on older assumptions, recalculated the total digestive tract mucosal surface area at approximately 32 m² — closer to half a badminton court than a tennis court. Villi and microvilli together still amplify the small intestine's surface area by roughly 60–120-fold over a simple tube, and this remains a genuinely enormous amplification — the corrected absolute number is simply smaller than the commonly repeated figure, not the underlying biological principle.

StageTypical duration

Why These Numbers Matter Clinically, Not Just as Trivia

The 98%+ fluid reabsorption figure directly explains why even a modest percentage reduction in absorptive efficiency (a bit of malabsorption, mild inflammation, a shortened bowel) can produce a dramatic absolute increase in stool output — the margin between normal and diarrheal output is thin relative to the total volume being processed. Gastric emptying times set the diagnostic threshold for gastroparesis (GI Atlas Vol. 3); colonic transit time is literally what the Bristol Stool Chart (this atlas's first section) is a bedside proxy for. Even the corrected, more modest surface-area and length figures above still describe a remarkably efficient organ system — accuracy doesn't make the digestive tract any less impressive, it just makes the impressive part the right one.

05 / Heart Failure & Structural/Inflammatory Disease

Heart Failure

A neurohormonal vicious cycle, restaged by the 2026 Second Universal Definition of Heart Failure — same four stages, refreshed EF-based categories, and a new Stage 0.

Pathophysiology: The Neurohormonal Vicious Cycle

Every compensatory mechanism the body deploys to maintain perfusion in early HF — sympathetic activation, RAAS, ADH — is the same mechanism that drives progressive myocardial damage when sustained chronically. This is the central irony of heart failure, and it's exactly why guideline-directed therapy works by blocking these pathways rather than just supporting pump function.

Falling cardiac output triggers sympathetic, RAAS, and ADH activation — adaptive acutely, maladaptive chronically, driving remodeling that further reduces contractile function and re-triggers the same cascade.
Pearl

This is the mechanistic logic behind the four pillars of GDMT — each interrupts one arm of the cycle: beta-blockers blunt sympathetic drive, ACEi/ARB/ARNI blunt RAAS, MRAs blunt aldosterone's fibrotic effects specifically, and SGLT2i work through a still-incompletely-understood mix of natriuresis, metabolic, and direct myocardial effects. None of them "just" lower numbers — they're all disease-modifying.

Staging & Classification — 2026 Second Universal Definition

The AHA/ACC/ESC/WHF/HFSA/HFA/JHFS consensus document reaffirms the familiar four-stage framework, adds a Stage 0, and reorganizes the EF-based subtypes into three clinically-actionable groups rather than rigid numeric cutoffs.

Top: Stage A–D progression (Stage 0 = no risk factors, not shown, precedes Stage A). Middle: EF-based categories per the 2026 statement. Bottom: NYHA functional class, which layers onto Stage C/D to describe symptom severity.
What's new in 2026

The updated statement steps back from treating LVEF cutoffs as rigid diagnostic boundaries — sex, age, ethnicity, and imaging modality all introduce variability that a single numeric threshold doesn't capture well. Practically, HFrEF/HFpEF/HFimpEF remain useful shorthand, but the emphasis shifts toward Stage B (pre-HF) as the highest-value intervention window, since most affected people sit in Stages 0–B rather than symptomatic C/D. confirm no interim update

Recent pharmacotherapy addition

Finerenone (a nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist) received FDA approval in July 2025 for reducing cardiovascular death and HF hospitalization/urgent visits in patients with LVEF ≥40% — extending MRA benefit into the HFmrEF/HFpEF space based on the FINEARTS-HF trial. verify current label/indication before prescribing

Bedside Differentiators

Acute decompensated HF

Look for the congestion phenotype: JVD, orthopnea, rales, S3, peripheral edema. BNP/NT-proBNP is most useful for its negative predictive value — a low value argues strongly against HF as the cause of dyspnea.

HFpEF is not "just diastolic dysfunction"

Coronary microvascular dysfunction is present in the large majority of contemporary HFpEF cohorts and contributes directly to exercise intolerance and elevated filling pressures — this is a systemic, multi-organ syndrome (renal, metabolic, vascular), not a purely mechanical filling problem.

06 / Heart Failure & Structural/Inflammatory Disease

Cardiomyopathies

Three structurally distinct diseases that all eventually present as heart failure — differentiated by chamber geometry, the dominant type of dysfunction, and etiology.

Dilated vs. Hypertrophic vs. Restrictive

The three classic phenotypes diverge early: dilated cardiomyopathy is a systolic pump failure story, hypertrophic is a diastolic/obstructive story, and restrictive is an infiltrative stiffness story. Recognizing which one you're looking at changes the entire diagnostic and management pathway.

Chamber geometry, dominant dysfunction, and characteristic causes for each phenotype, plus the classic bedside trap: distinguishing restrictive cardiomyopathy from constrictive pericarditis.
HCM and sudden cardiac death

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young competitive athletes — typically from ventricular arrhythmia triggered by the disorganized myocyte architecture (myocardial disarray) characteristic of the disease, sometimes precipitated by dynamic LVOT obstruction during exertion. A harsh systolic murmur that gets louder with Valsalva or standing (decreased preload increases obstruction) — the opposite of most other murmurs — should raise immediate suspicion.

2024 HCM Guideline update

Mavacamten, a cardiac myosin inhibitor, is now incorporated as a treatment option for symptomatic obstructive HCM when beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers provide inadequate control — positioned as a step before septal reduction therapy (surgical myectomy or alcohol septal ablation) rather than only after those interventions fail.

Restrictive Cardiomyopathy vs. Constrictive Pericarditis — Don't Conflate These

Both present with signs of right-heart congestion and diastolic dysfunction, but they are mechanistically opposite problems (myocardial vs. pericardial) with very different treatments — one is medically managed infiltrative disease, the other is often surgically curable.

Amyloidosis — the most commonly missed infiltrative cause

Cardiac amyloidosis (AL or ATTR) hides behind a restrictive phenotype with disproportionately thick walls for the degree of hypertension present, low-voltage ECG despite echocardiographic "hypertrophy" (a classic voltage-mass mismatch clue), and often carpal tunnel syndrome or spinal stenosis years before the cardiac diagnosis. ATTR amyloidosis in particular is increasingly recognized in older adults with HFpEF and deserves a lower threshold for pyrophosphate scintigraphy given effective disease-modifying therapy now exists.

07 / Heart Failure & Structural/Inflammatory Disease

Valvular Heart Disease

Four lesions, two mechanical categories — stenotic (pressure overload) and regurgitant (volume overload) — each producing a distinct remodeling pattern and a distinct murmur.

Pressure vs. Volume Overload Physiology

The ventricle responds differently depending on whether it's fighting a fixed obstruction or handling extra backflow volume — concentric hypertrophy in the former, eccentric hypertrophy (dilation) in the latter. That single distinction explains most of the exam findings and natural history differences below.

Aortic and mitral stenosis (top) drive pressure-overload concentric hypertrophy; aortic and mitral regurgitation (middle) drive volume-overload eccentric hypertrophy. Bottom: the acute valvular emergency pattern not to miss.
Aortic stenosis — the triad that predicts mortality

Angina, syncope, and dyspnea/heart failure mark progressively worse prognosis in that order once severe AS becomes symptomatic — average survival without valve replacement drops to roughly 5 years with angina, 3 years with syncope, and 2 years with heart failure symptoms. Symptom onset is the trigger for intervention regardless of how "compensated" the patient looks. verify these survival figures against current literature before quoting

As transcatheter approaches (TAVI, transcatheter edge-to-edge mitral repair) have matured with lower procedural risk, intervention thresholds have progressively shifted earlier in the disease course, and shared decision-making around valve type and procedural approach is now a formal part of the guideline framework.

Mitral stenosis and AFib

Chronic LA pressure/volume overload from MS makes atrial fibrillation extremely common — and because rheumatic MS carries a disproportionate thromboembolic risk even beyond typical AFib stroke-risk scores, anticoagulation decisions in this population shouldn't rely on CHA₂DS₂-VASc alone.

Chronic vs. acute regurgitation

Chronic AR/MR allow time for compensatory chamber dilation — patients can be remarkably asymptomatic for years. Acute regurgitation (endocarditis destroying a leaflet, papillary muscle rupture post-MI, aortic dissection extending into the valve) gives the ventricle no time to adapt: expect flash pulmonary edema and rapid decompensation even with a "smaller" regurgitant lesion than the chronic case.

Pericarditis → Tamponade

From straightforward viral pericarditis to a hemodynamic emergency — the pivot point is the rate of fluid accumulation, not just the total volume.

Progression & Tamponade Physiology

A slowly accumulating liter of pericardial fluid can be entirely asymptomatic; 150 mL accumulating over minutes (post-catheterization perforation, aortic dissection rupturing into the pericardium) can be fatal. Compliance, not volume, is the operative variable.

Top: the progression from inflammation to tamponade. Bottom: the mechanism (ventricular interdependence and pressure equalization), Beck's triad, pulsus paradoxus, and the echo findings that confirm the diagnosis.
Don't rely on Beck's triad

All three findings (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds) occur together in only a minority of true tamponade cases — sensitivity is poor. Maintain suspicion based on the clinical scenario (recent cardiac procedure, malignancy, uremia, aortic dissection, chest trauma) and confirm with bedside echocardiography rather than waiting for the full triad to declare itself.

Diffuse ST-elevation without reciprocal depression, plus PR depression, is the key pattern separating pericarditis from STEMI — but large or focal effusions can still coexist with concerning presentations, so this is a pattern-recognition aid, not a rule-out test on its own.

First-line therapy

NSAIDs plus colchicine is standard for acute idiopathic/viral pericarditis — colchicine specifically reduces recurrence risk, which is the more common long-term problem than the index episode itself. Reserve corticosteroids for refractory cases or specific etiologies (autoimmune, uremic) where NSAIDs are contraindicated or ineffective, since early steroid use is associated with higher recurrence rates.

Effusion accumulation and pericardial reserve

The normal pericardium has limited acute stretch capacity — rapid accumulation of as little as 150–200 mL can produce tamponade physiology, while chronic effusions of a liter or more may cause minimal hemodynamic disturbance if the pericardium has had time to stretch. This is the single most important concept for interpreting "how big is the effusion" on imaging. verify specific volume figures before quoting

Myocarditis & Endocarditis

Two very different infectious/inflammatory processes that share a talent for mimicking other diagnoses — and a 2023 update to how endocarditis gets diagnosed.

Infective Endocarditis: From NBTE to Vegetation

Endocarditis rarely starts on a pristine valve. It's a two-hit process — endothelial injury creates a sterile thrombus, and only then does bacteremia give it teeth.

Top: vegetation formation from endothelial injury through established infection. Middle: the four complication categories. Bottom: the 2023 Duke-ISCVID criteria structure, now with a third (surgical) major-criterion domain.
What changed in 2023

The Duke-ISCVID update expanded the list of "typical" causative organisms, added nuclear/PET imaging findings as major imaging criteria, introduced direct surgical/intraoperative inspection as an entirely new major-criterion domain, and removed the older strict requirements around blood culture timing and separate venipuncture sites — all aimed at increasing sensitivity without sacrificing specificity, since the 2000 Modified Duke criteria were missing a meaningful fraction of clinically-adjudicated cases.

Culture-Negative Endocarditis — Think Beyond Standard Cultures

Prior antibiotic exposure

The most common cause of "culture-negative" IE in practice is simply antibiotics given before cultures were drawn — always ask about recent antibiotic use before assuming a fastidious organism.

HACEK & fastidious organisms

HACEK organisms, Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella, and Tropheryma whipplei require specialized serology, PCR, or extended culture — a negative standard blood culture panel doesn't rule out IE in the right clinical context.

Myocarditis

Myocarditis is the great mimic of both ACS and dilated cardiomyopathy — chest pain with troponin elevation but normal (or non-obstructive) coronaries should always prompt the question "could this be inflammatory rather than ischemic?"

Etiology

Viral causes (coxsackievirus, parvovirus B19, adenovirus, and — less commonly now — SARS-CoV-2) predominate in most series. Also consider immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis in oncology patients — a rare but often fulminant and high-mortality complication that demands urgent recognition.

Diagnosis

Cardiac MRI with the Lake Louise criteria (edema, hyperemia, and necrosis/fibrosis patterns) is the diagnostic imaging standard; endomyocardial biopsy remains the definitive test but is reserved for fulminant presentations, suspected giant cell myocarditis, or cases where the diagnosis changes management (e.g., suspected immunosuppression-responsive etiology).

Fulminant myocarditis

A subset of patients present with rapid hemodynamic collapse, malignant arrhythmias, or cardiogenic shock within days of a viral prodrome — this fulminant phenotype requires aggressive hemodynamic support (often mechanical circulatory support) but paradoxically carries a better long-term prognosis if the patient survives the acute phase, compared to the more indolent non-fulminant form that more often progresses to chronic dilated cardiomyopathy.

Pulmonary HTN → Cor Pulmonale

Five WHO groups, one redefined hemodynamic threshold, and the direct pathophysiologic bridge from elevated pulmonary pressure to right heart failure.

Classification & the 2022 Hemodynamic Redefinition

Pulmonary hypertension is a hemodynamic finding with many possible upstream causes — the WHO group framework organizes those causes by shared mechanism and treatment approach, which matters enormously since therapy for one group can actively harm patients in another.

Top: the five WHO groups. Middle: the 2022 ESC/ERS hemodynamic redefinition (mPAP threshold lowered from ≥25 to >20 mmHg, with a new PVR criterion for pre-capillary disease). Bottom: the progression from chronic RV pressure overload to overt right heart failure.
The most important clinical trap

Pulmonary arterial hypertension–specific therapies (endothelin receptor antagonists, PDE5 inhibitors, prostacyclin pathway agents) are for Group 1 disease. Using them in Group 2 (left heart disease) can worsen pulmonary edema by increasing pulmonary blood flow into an already congested left atrium — always establish the group before reaching for PAH-specific therapy, and remember Group 2 is the most commonly encountered cause of PH in general clinical practice.

What Changed in the 2022 Redefinition

The mPAP threshold dropped from the longstanding ≥25 mmHg (in place since the first World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension in 1975) to >20 mmHg, based on normative data showing that even modestly elevated pressures carry prognostic significance. Critically, pre-capillary PH now also requires a pulmonary vascular resistance >2 Wood units — this addition helps separate genuine pulmonary vascular disease from pressure elevation driven simply by high cardiac output or borderline left heart filling pressures, preventing over-diagnosis from the lower mPAP cutoff alone.

Cor Pulmonale: Why the RV Fails Differently Than the LV

The right ventricle evolved as a low-pressure, high-compliance pump — thin-walled and volume-tolerant, but poorly equipped to handle sustained pressure overload. This structural mismatch is exactly why chronic pulmonary hypertension of any cause eventually produces right heart failure, often well before analogous chronic pressure overload would decompensate the LV.

Ventricular interdependence, again

The same septal-bowing mechanism seen in tamponade appears here: RV pressure/volume overload pushes the interventricular septum leftward (the "D-shaped septum" on echo), impairing LV filling and reducing cardiac output through a second, indirect mechanism beyond the RV failure itself.

Acute vs. chronic cor pulmonale

Acute cor pulmonale (massive PE, ARDS) gives the RV no time to hypertrophy — expect rapid RV dilation and shock physiology. Chronic cor pulmonale (COPD, ILD, longstanding PAH) allows compensatory RV hypertrophy, so decompensation, when it finally occurs, often follows a slower, more insidious congestive course (peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites) rather than sudden collapse.

CTEPH — the potentially curable group

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (Group 4) is unique among the five groups in that a subset of patients are candidates for pulmonary thromboendarterectomy — a surgical procedure that can be curative rather than merely palliative. Any patient with unexplained pulmonary hypertension and a history of VTE (even remote or unrecognized) warrants a ventilation-perfusion scan to evaluate for this possibility before assuming idiopathic PAH.

01 · Vascular Access

Routinely Maintaining Intravenous Access in Hospitalized Patients

Kennis B, Gerstenberger JP, Hayes L. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routinely maintaining intravenous access in hospitalized patients. J Hosp Med. 2026;21:197–199. doi: 10.1002/jhm.70191 · published online Oct 6, 2025
An 80-year-old man admitted with viral gastroenteritis and AKI has a PIVC placed for fluids. His diarrhea resolves, he's tolerating oral intake, and he's now just waiting on disposition — but the line stays in "just in case," and he says it's uncomfortable.

Original rationale

PIVCs are the cornerstone of acute hospital care — rapid access for fluids, meds, and emergencies. Placing one "just in case" costs little and removes a barrier if the patient suddenly decompensates.

Why it persists

Convenience and risk-aversion: nobody wants to be the reason a crashing patient has no access. Removing a working line takes an active decision; leaving it in is the passive default.

What the evidence actually shows

  • ~1 in 3 PIVCs placed in the ED are never used before removal.
  • ~1 in 4 PIVCs in hospitalized patients are "idle" — maintained without active use.
  • PIVCs, despite a lower per-catheter infection risk than central lines, are so common that they account for up to one-third of S. aureus catheter-related bacteremia.
  • More than 1 in 3 PIVCs fail before treatment completion, and roughly 10% show malfunction on inspection — the "just in case" line often isn't reliably patent when actually needed.
  • Noninfectious complications (pain, phlebitis) occur in 10–20% of patients with a PIVC in place.
  • A growing share of drugs traditionally given IV (opioids, many antibiotics) are equally effective given orally, undercutting the rationale for keeping access "for meds."

When it's still reasonable

Patients at meaningfully elevated risk of acute decompensation, anyone with an anticipated contrast study or procedure requiring access, and any situation where IV route remains clinically preferred (not just habitual).

Bottom line

Reassess idle PIVCs daily like any other line or catheter. If it's not being used and isn't anticipated to be needed imminently, pull it — intraosseous access is a reliable fallback if true emergent access is needed later.

02 · Bedside Manner

Standing at the Bedside

Gray A, Hall AM, Feldman LS. Things We Do for No Reason™: Standing at the bedside. J Hosp Med. 2026;21(7):801–803. doi: 10.1002/jhm.70293

Original rationale

Rounding is time-pressured. Standing feels efficient — you're already positioned to move to the next task, and sitting can feel like it invites a longer conversation than the schedule allows.

Why it persists

Pure habit and workflow culture. Nobody is taught to sit; standing is simply the unexamined default carried over from residency, and rooms aren't always set up with an obvious chair.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Getting to eye level is a core, well-studied component of etiquette-based medicine.
  • Sitting at the bedside, compared with standing, improves physician–patient communication and patient perception of the encounter.
  • Critically, sitting does not lead to additional time spent in the patient's room — the perceived efficiency cost of standing is not real; patients simply perceive sitting visits as longer even when they aren't.

Bottom line

Sit or get to eye level when circumstances allow. It's a free intervention — no measurable time cost — with a real communication benefit.

03 · Underuse / Missed Workup

Failing to Consider Primary Aldosteronism in the Initial Evaluation of Hypertension, Hypertensive Urgency, and Hypertensive Emergency

Shih MC, Lavorgna TR, Dogra P, Hirner CN, Payne K. Things We Do for No Reason™: Failing to consider primary aldosteronism in the initial evaluation of hypertension, hypertensive urgency, and hypertensive emergency. J Hosp Med. 2025. doi: 10.1002/jhm.70035 · published online Mar 20, 2025
This entry is one of the series' "underuse" pieces — TWDFNR periodically flags things we fail to do, not just things we overdo.
A woman in her mid-30s presents with dyspnea, palpitations, and chest pressure; BP peaks at 190/139. Labs show hypokalemia (3.2 mEq/L), hypomagnesemia (1.7 mg/dL), and proteinuria/hematuria. She's treated as hypertensive emergency with IV/oral metoprolol and improves — but the underlying cause is never screened for.

Original rationale

Inpatient hypertension management is triage-focused: get the pressure down safely. Etiology is treated as an outpatient PCP problem, especially on a short admission.

Why it persists

Time pressure of the inpatient stay, and clues like hypokalemia get attributed to diuretics, vomiting, or poor intake rather than triggering a secondary-cause workup.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Primary aldosteronism (PA) is considered the most common identifiable cause of secondary hypertension.
  • Classic clues — hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, resistant or new severe hypertension, especially in a younger patient — should prompt consideration of PA, not just be treated as incidental.
  • Critically, many antihypertensives that get started reflexively during the admission (ACEi/ARB, MRAs, beta-blockers) alter the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone axis — once started, they can suppress or distort the aldosterone/renin ratio and make PA much harder to diagnose for months afterward.

Bottom line

In new or resistant hypertension with suggestive features, consider a plasma aldosterone/renin ratio before starting agents that affect the RAAS — don't reflexively treat the number and defer the workup, since starting treatment first can close the diagnostic window.

04 · Documentation

Routine Use of "Denies" and Other Stigmatizing Language in Medical Documentation

Caton JB, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routine use of "denies" and other stigmatizing language in medical documentation. J Hosp Med. 2025;20(6):623–627. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13527 · published online Oct 14, 2024

Original rationale

"Denies" is terse, traditional H&P shorthand — a fast way to document a negative in the review of systems or social history.

Why it persists

Hardwired into EMR templates and smart-phrases; it's the path of least resistance, and most clinicians have never been prompted to reconsider it.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Language in the medical record has a documented "distancing function" — word choice subtly shapes how subsequent readers (clinicians) perceive the patient, independent of the underlying facts.
  • "Denies" carries a connotation of concealment or dishonesty that neutral phrasing ("reports no…," "no history of…") does not — this matters most in sensitive domains like substance use, abuse, and psychiatric symptoms.
  • Stigmatizing documentation language has been shown to bias downstream clinicians' attitudes toward a patient after reading the chart, independent of the clinical facts presented.

Bottom line

Swap "denies" for neutral, descriptive phrasing, especially in social history and substance-use/psychiatric documentation. It costs nothing and removes an unintended implication of dishonesty from the chart.

05 · Imaging / Renal

Avoiding Intravenous Iodinated Contrast Material in Patients With Acute Kidney Injury or Chronic Kidney Disease

Coleman M, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Avoiding intravenous iodinated contrast material in patients with acute kidney injury or chronic kidney disease. J Hosp Med. 2025;20:1108–1111. doi: 10.1002/jhm.70063

Original rationale

Older observational data (largely from the high-osmolar contrast era) linked IV contrast to "contrast-induced nephropathy" (CIN), teaching generations of clinicians to withhold contrast whenever creatinine was elevated.

Why it persists

The reflex is deeply engrained — "elevated creatinine = no contrast" — reinforced by radiology gatekeeping and medicolegal caution, even as the contrast agents themselves have changed substantially.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Modern low-osmolar and iso-osmolar IV contrast carries a substantially lower nephrotoxic signal than the historical literature suggested — much of the original "CIN" association reflected confounding (patients who need contrast are often sicker at baseline), not true causation.
  • Large controlled comparisons find diagnostic IV contrast CT is not significantly associated with clinically meaningful AKI in most populations once confounders are accounted for ("neurons over nephrons" reasoning is now standard in time-sensitive imaging like stroke protocols).
  • The 2020 ACR/National Kidney Foundation consensus statement (Davenport et al., Radiology 2020;294(3):660–668) explicitly supports more liberal use of IV contrast in patients with reduced renal function.
  • Delaying or avoiding clinically indicated contrast imaging (PE, dissection, stroke, occult infection/malignancy) carries its own well-documented diagnostic-delay harm — one that, in most patients, outweighs the now much smaller theoretical renal risk.

When caution is still warranted

Dialysis-dependent patients without residual renal function, and patients with severe AKI/CKD where the imaging is elective rather than diagnostically necessary — individualize rather than blanket-avoid.

Bottom line

Don't withhold clinically necessary contrast CT for AKI/CKD alone in most patients. Use the lowest effective contrast volume and reserve true avoidance for the highest-risk renal populations.

Verify locally: institutional contrast/renal protocols and eGFR thresholds vary — confirm against your radiology department's current policy before applying broadly.
06 · Cardiac / Pharmacy

Checking QTc on Hospitalized Adult Patients Before Intravenous Ondansetron Administration

Kaushik R, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Checking QTc on hospitalized adult patients before intravenous ondansetron administration. J Hosp Med. 2024–2025. PMID: 39149835

Original rationale

A 2012 FDA warning flagged dose-dependent QT prolongation with ondansetron, prompting the withdrawal of the 32 mg single IV dose and widespread caution language.

Why it persists

The warning got generalized far beyond the dose that prompted it — order sets now default to requiring a baseline ECG before any IV ondansetron, regardless of the dose actually being given or the patient's baseline risk.

What the evidence actually shows

  • The FDA action targeted the high-dose (32 mg) single infusion — standard intermittent dosing (4–8 mg IV) carries a much smaller QT signal.
  • Clinically significant QTc prolongation or torsades with routine low-dose IV ondansetron is rare in patients without other major risk factors present.
  • Reflexive baseline ECG-before-every-dose has low diagnostic yield at that dose range and delays antiemetic treatment while adding cost and nursing/pharmacy workload.

When it's still reasonable

Patients with a known baseline-prolonged QTc, concurrent use of other QT-prolonging drugs, significant electrolyte derangement (hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia), or structural heart disease — screen selectively, not universally.

Bottom line

Reserve QTc screening before IV ondansetron for patients with identifiable risk factors rather than applying it as a blanket rule to every dose in every patient.

07 · Addiction Medicine / Hepatology

Avoiding Naltrexone for Alcohol Use Disorder in Liver Disease

Kee DP, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Avoiding naltrexone for alcohol use disorder in liver disease. J Hosp Med. 2025;20(7):768–771. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13569

Original rationale

Naltrexone's label carries a hepatotoxicity warning, and the drug is hepatically metabolized — an intuitive reason to avoid it in a patient whose liver is already compromised.

Why it persists

Blanket avoidance across any degree of liver disease, driven by unfamiliarity outside addiction medicine and by the label warning being applied without reference to the dose that actually generated it.

What the evidence actually shows

  • The original hepatotoxicity signal came largely from studies using supratherapeutic doses (up to 300 mg/day — roughly 6× the standard AUD dose).
  • Standard therapeutic dosing (oral 50 mg daily, or extended-release IM 380 mg monthly) has not shown consistent clinically significant hepatotoxicity in patients treated for AUD, including many with underlying liver disease.
  • Untreated AUD itself carries substantial risk of progression to cirrhosis and decompensation — withholding effective pharmacotherapy is not a "safe" default, it trades one risk for a larger one.

When caution is still warranted

Acute hepatitis or acute hepatic failure — reasonable to hold or defer here. Baseline and periodic LFT monitoring is a sensible precaution in compensated disease, not a reason to withhold treatment outright.

Bottom line

Naltrexone at standard AUD dosing is not contraindicated in most compensated liver disease. Don't let the label's supratherapeutic-dose data drive a blanket avoidance that leaves AUD untreated.

Verify before prescribing: confirm current liver function, exclude acute hepatitis/failure, and check your state's/institution's naltrexone initiation protocol.
08 · Pulmonary / Labs

Arterial Blood Gas Testing to Screen for Hypercarbic Respiratory Failure

Lacy ME, Saa L, Bruss Z, Noronha L. Things we do for no reason™: arterial blood gas testing to screen for hypercarbic respiratory failure. J Hosp Med. 2025;20(9):1002–1004. doi: 10.1002/jhm.70039

Original rationale

Arterial sampling is the traditional gold standard for pCO2, so it became the reflexive first test whenever hypercarbia is a concern (respiratory distress, altered mental status, COPD exacerbation).

Why it persists

Habit, and a perception that venous values are "less trustworthy" than arterial ones — even when the clinical question is simply screening for significant hypercarbia rather than precise oxygenation data.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Venous blood gas (VBG) pCO2 correlates reasonably well with arterial pCO2 in hemodynamically stable patients, with a fairly consistent offset (venous typically runs several mmHg higher).
  • A normal VBG pCO2 in a stable patient reasonably excludes clinically significant hypercarbia, avoiding an unnecessary arterial puncture.
  • Arterial puncture carries its own real costs — pain, hematoma, and rarely pseudoaneurysm — that a venous sample avoids entirely.

When ABG is still the right test

Hemodynamically unstable patients, situations needing precise oxygenation data (PaO2, A-a gradient), or when a VBG result is abnormal or discordant with the clinical picture.

Bottom line

Use VBG first-line to screen for hypercarbia in stable patients; reserve arterial sampling for confirming an abnormal VBG, instability, or when oxygenation data specifically is needed.

Infertility — Definitions & Workup

ASRM defines infertility as a disease, not merely a delay — a framing that matters for insurance advocacy and for how the diagnosis is discussed with patients.

When to Evaluate

SituationTiming
Female partner <35, no known risk factorsAfter 12 months unprotected intercourse
Female partner ≥35After 6 months (expedited)
Female partner >40Immediate evaluation and treatment
Known risk factor present (below)Immediate — don't wait out the clock

Known risk factors warranting immediate evaluation regardless of duration: oligo/amenorrhea, known or suspected tubal/uterine/peritoneal disease, stage III–IV endometriosis, known or suspected male-factor subfertility, or a genetic condition known to affect fertility.

Start Both Partners at the Same Time

The number every non-REI provider underestimates

Male factor contributes to 40–50% of infertile heterosexual couples' cases — sole or contributing. ASRM/ACOG guidance is explicit: evaluation of both partners should begin concurrently, not sequentially after the female workup turns up nothing. A women's health specialist can reasonably obtain the male partner's history and order a semen analysis directly, rather than waiting on a referral to start that half of the workup.

Female Factor — Core Workup

  • Ovulatory function: menstrual history is often sufficient; mid-luteal progesterone (>3 ng/mL confirms ovulation) or urinary LH ovulation predictor kits when history is ambiguous.
  • Ovarian reserve: anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and antral follicle count on ultrasound are now the preferred tests — more stable across the cycle than day-3 FSH/estradiol, which remain acceptable alternatives. Ovarian reserve testing predicts response to stimulation, not natural fertility or the ability to conceive spontaneously — an important distinction when counseling patients on what a "low" AMH does and doesn't mean.
  • Tubal patency & uterine cavity: hysterosalpingogram (HSG) remains the classic first-line test for tubal patency and gross cavity assessment; saline infusion sonohysterogram is a good alternative/adjunct for cavity detail (polyps, submucosal fibroids, septum).
  • Additional, as indicated: TSH and prolactin if cycle irregularity or galactorrhea; expanded workup for suspected endometriosis or recurrent pregnancy loss follows separate, more involved algorithms.

Male Factor — Core Workup

  • Semen analysis (WHO reference criteria) is the cornerstone — volume, concentration, motility, morphology. Two abnormal analyses at least 2–4 weeks apart before drawing firm conclusions, given real intra-individual variability.
  • Common identifiable causes: varicocele (the single most common surgically correctable cause), obstructive and non-obstructive azoospermia, hormonal causes (hypogonadotropic hypogonadism), and lifestyle/exposure factors (heat, smoking, anabolic steroid use).
  • Refer to a specialist in male reproductive medicine for any abnormal semen analysis, especially azoospermia or severe oligospermia — karyotype and Y-chromosome microdeletion testing are relevant in specific severe presentations.
Unexplained infertility

Diagnosed in up to 30% of infertile couples — defined as normal ovulation, tubal patency, and semen analysis with no identified cause. This isn't a workup failure; it's a real, common diagnostic category with its own treatment pathway (typically starting with ovulation induction + IUI before escalating to IVF).

Assisted Reproductive Technology

From first-line oral ovulation induction through IVF, donor gametes, and fertility preservation.

Ovulation Induction — Letrozole Has Displaced Clomiphene Tier A

The PPCOS II trial (Legro et al., NEJM) randomized 750 women with PCOS and ovulatory dysfunction to letrozole vs. clomiphene citrate for up to 5 cycles.

61.7% vs 48.3%
Cumulative ovulation rate — letrozole vs clomiphene
27.5% vs 19.1%
Live birth rate — letrozole vs clomiphene
3% vs 7%
Twin pregnancy rate — letrozole was lower, not higher

Both ASRM and WHO now recommend letrozole as first-line oral ovulation induction for PCOS-related anovulatory infertility, reversing decades of clomiphene-first practice. The mechanism-based worry that letrozole (an aromatase inhibitor) might carry teratogenic risk has not borne out — congenital anomaly rates were statistically similar between arms and consistent with general-population baseline rates in the trial data. Clomiphene's disadvantage is largely peripheral: its anti-estrogenic effect on the endometrium and cervical mucus creates a gap between ovulation rate and actual conception rate that letrozole doesn't share.

Escalation Pathway

1
Oral ovulation induction (letrozole first-line, clomiphene alternative) ± timed intercourse or IUI — lowest cost, lowest multiple-gestation risk.
2
Gonadotropin ovulation induction (injectable FSH/LH) — more effective per cycle but a substantially higher multiple-gestation rate (~14% in observational data, including higher-order multiples) and higher cost.
3
IVF — for tubal factor, severe male factor, failed ovulation induction/IUI cycles, advanced maternal age, or endometriosis-associated infertility. Multiple-birth rate is lower than gonadotropin cycles when single embryo transfer is used, which is now standard practice for good-prognosis patients specifically to avoid multiple gestation.

IVF — What the Process Actually Involves

  • Controlled ovarian stimulation (injectable gonadotropins, ~8–14 days) to recruit multiple follicles, monitored by serial ultrasound and estradiol.
  • Retrieval — transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte aspiration under sedation.
  • Fertilization — conventional insemination, or ICSI (intracytoplasmic sperm injection — a single sperm injected directly into each egg) for severe male factor or prior fertilization failure.
  • Culture & transfer — embryo(s) cultured to day 3 (cleavage stage) or day 5 (blastocyst); transferred fresh or after cryopreservation (frozen embryo transfer, now the majority approach at many centers, partly to allow uterine recovery from stimulation hormones and partly to allow genetic testing turnaround).
  • PGT (preimplantation genetic testing) — PGT-A screens for aneuploidy; PGT-M tests for a specific known monogenic condition. An add-on with genuine utility in specific situations (advanced maternal age, known carrier status, recurrent loss) rather than a default for every cycle.

IVF Success — Age Is the Dominant Variable

Per SART national data (cumulative live birth rate, own oocytes): <35 years ~56%, 35–37 ~41%, 38–40 ~27%, declining sharply thereafter to roughly 10% at 43 and 5% at 45, plateauing near the low single digits beyond that. This is the single most important number for setting expectations — clinic-specific marketing statistics vary with patient selection, but the age-related decline itself is remarkably consistent across the field.

Donor Gametes, Gestational Carriers & Fertility Preservation

  • Donor oocytes — the most effective way to overcome age-related ovarian reserve decline, since embryo/pregnancy outcomes track the egg source's age, not the recipient's.
  • Donor sperm — for severe/untreatable male factor, absence of a male partner, or genetic carrier avoidance.
  • Gestational carrier (surrogacy) — for absolute uterine factor, recurrent implantation failure, or medical contraindication to carrying a pregnancy; legally complex and state-dependent in the U.S.
  • Fertility preservation (oocyte/embryo cryopreservation): the medically urgent indication is pre-gonadotoxic therapy (chemotherapy/radiation) — ideally referred immediately at diagnosis, before treatment starts. Elective ("social") fertility preservation for age-related planning is increasingly common and, per the earlier SART growth data, one of the fastest-growing ART categories nationally — success still depends heavily on the age at which oocytes were frozen, not the age at eventual use.
03

Preconception Optimization

The highest-leverage window in obstetrics — most of it closes before the first prenatal visit even happens (Vol. 1, Module 1).

Chronic Disease Optimization Before Conception

  • Diabetes: periconceptional glycemic control correlates directly with congenital anomaly risk (Vol. 3, Module 1) — this is the single clearest example of preconception care changing outcomes that a first-trimester visit cannot undo.
  • Hypertension: switch off ACE inhibitors/ARBs before conception (Vol. 3, Module 4) rather than discovering the pregnancy on a contraindicated medication.
  • Thyroid disease: optimize TSH before conception in known thyroid disease — untreated hypothyroidism in early pregnancy is linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.
  • Teratogenic medication review: isotretinoin (mandatory washout period and pregnancy prevention program), valproate (neural tube defects and neurodevelopmental effects — avoid unless no alternative controls a serious condition), warfarin (switch to a pregnancy-compatible anticoagulant preconception when possible).
  • Vaccination status: confirm rubella and varicella immunity and update any needed vaccines before conception, since live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are contraindicated once pregnant.

Nutrition & Weight

Folic acid (Vol. 1) and pre-pregnancy BMI optimization (Vol. 1/Vol. 3) both function best started preconception, for the same reason: the exposure windows that matter (neural tube closure, early organogenesis) precede pregnancy recognition.

Genetic Carrier Screening

Expanded carrier screening panels (cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, and dozens to hundreds of additional conditions depending on panel breadth) are increasingly offered preconception rather than at the first prenatal visit — preconception timing preserves the full range of reproductive options (including donor gamete decisions) if both partners are found to be carriers of the same recessive condition, which isn't equally true once already pregnant.

Paternal Preconception Factors — Frequently Skipped Entirely

Sperm are not exempt from aging biology

De novo single-nucleotide mutations accumulate in sperm at roughly 1–3 additional mutations per year of paternal age, via ongoing spermatogonial cell division. Advanced paternal age (commonly defined ≥40–45) is epidemiologically associated with increased risk of autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and several single-gene "sentinel" conditions in offspring, plus reduced sperm quality, higher DNA fragmentation, and lower ART success rates.

Keep this appropriately calibrated

For autism and schizophrenia specifically, rigorous modeling shows the epidemiologic association with paternal age is larger than de novo mutation accumulation alone can explain — meaning some of the observed association likely reflects confounding factors (e.g., subclinical parental traits influencing both age at fatherhood and offspring risk) rather than mutation burden alone. Useful for counseling context: real biological signal exists, but "older father" is not a clean, fully mechanistic risk factor the way maternal age is for aneuploidy.

  • Modifiable paternal factors with real evidence behind them: smoking cessation, alcohol moderation, obesity/weight optimization, and avoiding sustained scrotal heat exposure (frequent hot tub/sauna use, laptop-on-lap habits) — all measurably affect semen parameters.
  • Paternal preconception health is a legitimate, evidence-based target for counseling — not just a maternal responsibility, and a natural point to actively involve a male partner in preconception planning rather than treating it as solely "her" workup.

Epigenetics & the DOHaD Hypothesis

The real molecular biology behind "the prenatal environment shapes lifelong health" — and, by extension, exactly why some popular prenatal advice (Vol. 1's Mozart-effect discussion) has no mechanistic basis while other advice has a well-documented one.

The Mechanisms, Briefly

  • DNA methylation — addition of methyl groups to CpG sites in DNA, typically silencing gene expression at that region without altering the underlying sequence. This is the most-studied human epigenetic mark and the one with the clearest prenatal-exposure data (below).
  • Histone modification — acetylation, methylation, and other chemical marks on the histone proteins DNA wraps around, altering how tightly chromatin is packed and therefore how accessible genes are for transcription.
  • Non-coding RNA — regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally; an active area of research with less mature human prenatal-exposure data than DNA methylation.

The DOHaD Hypothesis & the Dutch Hunger Winter

The Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis — building on David Barker's original observations — holds that the prenatal environment programs offspring physiology in ways that persist across the lifespan, and that this programming becomes harmful specifically when it's mismatched to the environment the offspring actually grows up in (a fetus "predicting" scarcity that never arrives, for example).

The best human natural experiment we have

The Dutch Hunger Winter (a severe, sharply time-bounded famine during German occupation, winter 1944–45) let researchers compare people exposed to acute prenatal starvation at precisely known gestational windows against unexposed siblings. Heijmans et al. (PNAS, 2008) found that individuals exposed to famine specifically during the periconceptional period had measurably less DNA methylation at the imprinted IGF2 gene — six decades later, in adulthood, compared to their own same-sex, unexposed siblings.

Later genome-scale follow-up work (Tobi et al., Nature Communications) extended this: famine-associated differential methylation clustered at gene-regulatory regions tied to growth and metabolic pathways — directly consistent with the epidemiologic finding that Dutch Hunger Winter survivors have elevated adult rates of glucose intolerance, obesity, and cardiovascular disease.

The Mechanistic Link to Folic Acid — Not Just NTD Prevention

Folate, B12, and choline all feed one-carbon metabolism, the biochemical pathway that supplies methyl groups for DNA methylation genome-wide. This gives the Vol. 1/Vol. 4 folic acid recommendation a second, independent rationale beyond neural tube closure: periconceptional methyl-donor nutrient status plausibly shapes epigenetic programming more broadly, not only the specific NTD-prevention pathway most providers learned it for.

Maternal Stress & the HPA Axis

This is the actual mechanism behind the Vol. 1 advice that a calmer, better-supported pregnancy matters — not a vague wellness platitude. The placental enzyme 11β-HSD2 normally inactivates the majority of maternal cortisol before it reaches fetal circulation, acting as a protective barrier. Under conditions of significant, sustained maternal stress, this barrier is incompletely protective, and human and animal data associate elevated prenatal maternal cortisol exposure with altered offspring HPA-axis reactivity and stress-response patterning later in life. This is the legitimate epigenetic pathway underlying "reduce maternal stress" as prenatal advice — worth knowing specifically so it can be explained with an actual mechanism rather than asserted as folk wisdom.

Transgenerational Effects — Where to Be Appropriately Careful

Evidence strength drops sharply past the F1 generation

Direct in-utero (F1) epigenetic programming — the fetus exposed in its mother's womb — has real, replicated human evidence behind it (above). True transgenerational inheritance (effects appearing in the F2 grandchild generation, who was never directly exposed, or beyond) is well-documented in animal models (the classic agouti mouse coat-color/methylation experiments) but has much thinner, more contested human evidence. Some Dutch Hunger Winter follow-up work has explored F2 outcomes with mixed findings. Be precise with this distinction when discussing epigenetics with patients — "what you eat during pregnancy can affect your child" has strong human evidence; "it can affect your grandchildren" is a much shakier claim to make with confidence in humans specifically.

Practical Translation — What's Actually Proven

The evidence-based list Tier A
  • Adequate methyl-donor nutrition (folate/B12/choline) — mechanistically tied to DNA methylation, not just NTD prevention
  • Avoiding known teratogens, heavy alcohol, and smoking — direct epigenetic and mutagenic mechanisms, not just structural anomaly risk
  • Glycemic control in diabetic pregnancy (Vol. 3) — offspring epigenetic programming toward later obesity/diabetes risk is an active, evidence-supported research area, adding a mechanistic layer to why tight control matters beyond the immediate pregnancy
  • Chronic maternal stress reduction — via the HPA-axis/cortisol pathway above, this is real biology, not just wellness advice
Circling back to Vol. 1's Mozart-effect discussion

None of the mechanisms above provide any plausible pathway for passive in-utero music exposure to alter offspring gene expression or epigenetic programming — there's no equivalent to a "methyl donor" or "cortisol pathway" for classical music. This is precisely why it belongs in the folklore category rather than alongside nutrition, toxin avoidance, and stress reduction, which all have identifiable molecular mechanisms behind them.

Cost of Fertility Treatment

Unlike pregnancy/delivery care (Vol. 1), fertility treatment is not a federally protected essential health benefit — coverage is a patchwork that depends heavily on state and employer.

$20–25K+
Typical single IVF cycle, all-in (base fee + medications + monitoring)
$40–60K+
Realistic total for most patients, who need more than one cycle
21–25
States with some form of fertility insurance mandate — coverage detail varies enormously by state

Why Coverage Is So Inconsistent

  • No federal IVF mandate — unlike maternity coverage under the ACA, fertility treatment is not a required essential health benefit nationally.
  • Self-funded employer plans (which cover the majority of Americans with employer-sponsored insurance) are exempt from state mandates entirely under federal ERISA law — meaning even patients in a "mandate state" may have zero fertility coverage if their employer self-funds.
  • Even in mandate states, requirements often specify a minimum documented infertility duration, prior failed treatment attempts, or diagnosis-specific criteria before coverage applies — coverage-on-paper and coverage-in-practice are not the same thing.
  • Employer-sponsored fertility benefit programs (separate from standard health insurance) have become one of the more meaningful gap-fillers in recent years, particularly at larger employers.

Practical guidance worth giving patients
  • Ask every clinic for an itemized cost sheet before starting — quoted "base" prices routinely understate the realistic all-in cost.
  • Confirm directly with HR whether an employer plan is self-funded before assuming a state mandate applies.
  • Multi-cycle discount/refund programs exist at many clinics and function as a hedge against needing more than one cycle — worth asking about specifically rather than assuming standard per-cycle pricing is the only option.

Partner's Role in the Fertility Journey

Infertility is a couple's diagnosis even when the workup finds a single-partner cause — and the emotional toll is well-documented on both sides, not just the partner undergoing procedures.

Show Up for the Workup, Literally

Module 1 was explicit: evaluation of both partners should start concurrently. If you're the male partner, getting a semen analysis done promptly — rather than treating it as an optional or lower-priority step — meaningfully shortens the overall diagnostic timeline for the couple, and normalizes shared responsibility for what's happening rather than positioning it as "her" workup that you're peripherally supporting.

The Emotional Weight Is Real and Often Underestimated

What actually helps
  • Attend appointments when you can, especially retrieval and transfer days in an IVF cycle — the hormonal and physical toll of stimulation falls disproportionately on one partner, and presence matters concretely, not just symbolically.
  • Learn the injection schedule and logistics if your partner is self-injecting stimulation medications — being a genuine second set of hands (or at least a second set of eyes on timing) reduces a meaningful source of daily stress during a cycle.
  • Expect — and don't personalize — real strain on intimacy and mood during treatment; timed intercourse, injection schedules, and repeated disappointment cycles take a documented toll on relationships, and that strain is a normal feature of the process, not a sign something is wrong between you.
  • If male factor is identified, actively resist any instinct toward shame or withdrawal — this is precisely the situation where showing up engaged (Module 1) matters most, since male-factor diagnoses can otherwise become something a partner quietly checks out of rather than actively works through together.

Preconception Is a Shared Project, Not Just Her Prep List

Everything in Module 3's paternal preconception section — smoking cessation, alcohol moderation, weight optimization, heat exposure — is something a partner can act on directly and in parallel with a female partner's preconception care, not something to leave entirely on her plate while treating your own health habits as unrelated to the outcome.

Airspace Disease / Pneumonia

10 findings

1. Lobar consolidation (bacterial pneumonia pattern)

Concerning

Airspace filling confined to a single lobe, the classic pattern of typical bacterial pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous consolidation with air bronchograms, respecting fissural boundaries, most often affecting a single lobe.

Differential
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (classic cause)
  • Post-obstructive pneumonia (if a central endobronchial lesion is present)
  • Organizing pneumonia
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to likely pathogens; follow-up imaging in 6-8 weeks if risk factors for malignancy to confirm resolution.

2. Bronchopneumonia (patchy/multifocal)

Concerning

Patchy, multifocal airspace disease centered on the airways rather than a single lobe.

Imaging Characteristics

Multifocal, patchy areas of consolidation and centrilobular nodularity ('tree-in-bud') not respecting lobar boundaries.

Differential
  • Bacterial bronchopneumonia (Staph, Klebsiella, gram-negatives)
  • Aspiration
  • Atypical infection
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to likely pathogens and clinical setting (community vs. hospital-acquired).

3. Aspiration pneumonia

Concerning

Pneumonia from inhalation of oropharyngeal or gastric contents, with a characteristic dependent distribution.

Imaging Characteristics

Consolidation in the dependent lung segments (posterior upper lobes/superior lower lobes if aspirated supine; basal segments if upright), often bilateral.

Differential
  • Aspiration pneumonitis (chemical, may improve without antibiotics)
  • Aspiration pneumonia (bacterial superinfection)
Next Step

Correlate with aspiration risk factors (dysphagia, altered mental status); antibiotics if bacterial infection suspected.

4. Atypical/viral pneumonia pattern

Concerning

Pneumonia with a predominantly interstitial/ground-glass pattern rather than dense consolidation.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral, often peripheral ground-glass opacities with or without interlobular septal thickening; may progress to consolidation in severe cases.

Differential
  • Viral pneumonia (influenza, COVID-19, RSV)
  • Mycoplasma/Chlamydophila pneumonia
  • Early organizing pneumonia
Next Step

Correlate with viral testing; supportive care and targeted antivirals/antibiotics as indicated.

5. Round pneumonia

Benign

Spherical area of consolidation mimicking a mass, classically seen in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, rounded area of consolidation, most often in a lower lobe, without the irregular margins typical of a neoplasm.

Differential
  • Bacterial pneumonia (round morphology, especially pediatric)
  • Pulmonary mass (key differential in adults, warrants follow-up)
Next Step

Follow-up imaging to confirm resolution after treatment, particularly in adults where malignancy must be excluded.

6. Necrotizing / cavitary pneumonia

Concerning

Severe pneumonia with tissue necrosis and cavity formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Consolidation with multiple small lucencies or a discrete cavity, sometimes with an air-fluid level; associated with virulent organisms.

Differential
  • Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA)
  • Klebsiella pneumoniae
  • Anaerobic infection
  • Fungal infection
Next Step

Broad-spectrum antibiotics; monitor for abscess formation and empyema.

7. Lung abscess

Concerning

Localized area of lung parenchymal necrosis and cavitation containing pus.

Imaging Characteristics

Thick-walled cavity with an air-fluid level, often with surrounding consolidation, typically in a dependent segment if aspiration-related.

Differential
  • Anaerobic/aspiration-related abscess (most common)
  • Cavitary malignancy (thicker, more irregular wall)
  • Infected bulla
Next Step

Prolonged antibiotics; drainage rarely needed unless not responding to medical therapy.

8. Septic pulmonary emboli

Concerning

Multiple peripheral infected emboli seeding the lungs, usually from a distant infected source (endocarditis, infected line, IV drug use).

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple peripheral nodules, often with cavitation, sometimes showing a 'feeding vessel sign' (a pulmonary vessel leading directly into the nodule).

Differential
  • Right-sided endocarditis
  • Infected central line/hardware
  • Suppurative thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome)
Next Step

Blood cultures, echocardiography to evaluate for endocarditis, and source control.

9. Organizing pneumonia pattern

Variable

Pattern of airspace consolidation from organizing exudate, either cryptogenic or secondary to another cause.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral or peribronchovascular consolidation, often migratory on serial imaging; the 'reversed halo' (atoll) sign, a ring of consolidation surrounding ground-glass, is a classic but not universal feature.

Differential
  • Cryptogenic organizing pneumonia
  • Infection
  • Drug reaction
  • Connective tissue disease-associated
Next Step

Correlate with clinical course and medication history; often responds to corticosteroids after infection is excluded.

10. Aspiration pneumonitis (chemical)

Variable

Chemical injury to the lung from aspirated gastric contents, distinct from bacterial aspiration pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Ground-glass opacity or consolidation in dependent segments, often improving rapidly (24-48 hours) without antibiotics if truly chemical rather than infectious.

Differential
  • Aspiration pneumonitis (self-limited, may not need antibiotics)
  • Aspiration pneumonia (bacterial, needs antibiotics)
Next Step

Supportive care initially; add antibiotics if no improvement or if bacterial superinfection is suspected.

Pulmonary Nodules & Masses

12 findings

1. Solitary pulmonary nodule, low-risk

Benign

Small pulmonary nodule with imaging features suggesting a benign etiology.

Imaging Characteristics

Small (<6 mm in low-risk patients), smooth, well-defined nodule, sometimes with a benign calcification pattern (central, popcorn, or diffuse) or stable on prior imaging over 2+ years.

Differential
  • Granuloma (most common benign cause)
  • Intrapulmonary lymph node (typically subpleural, oval, lower lobe)
Next Step

Follow Fleischner Society guidelines for surveillance based on size and risk factors.

2. Solitary pulmonary nodule, indeterminate

Variable

Nodule without clear benign or malignant features, requiring risk-stratified follow-up.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodule 6-8 mm or larger without a benign calcification pattern, smooth to mildly lobulated margins, and no comparison imaging available.

Differential
  • Granuloma
  • Early primary lung malignancy
  • Metastasis (if known primary elsewhere)
Next Step

Fleischner Society guideline-based follow-up CT interval based on size and risk factors; PET-CT for nodules ≥8 mm with intermediate-to-high risk.

3. Solitary pulmonary nodule, high-risk/spiculated

Concerning

Nodule with imaging features concerning for malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Spiculated or lobulated margins, larger size, upper lobe predominance, and/or interval growth on serial imaging.

Differential
  • Primary lung malignancy (most concerning)
  • Less likely: organizing infection/granuloma with atypical margins
Next Step

Pulmonology/thoracic surgery referral for tissue diagnosis (biopsy) and staging workup.

4. Multiple pulmonary nodules, miliary pattern

Concerning

Innumerable tiny nodules diffusely scattered throughout both lungs.

Imaging Characteristics

Numerous, small (1-3 mm), randomly distributed nodules throughout both lungs, described as a 'miliary' (millet seed) pattern.

Differential
  • Miliary tuberculosis
  • Fungal infection (histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis)
  • Hematogenous metastases (thyroid, renal cell, choriocarcinoma classically)
Next Step

Correlate with infectious symptoms and malignancy history; infectious workup (AFB smear/culture) and/or biopsy as indicated.

5. Pulmonary hamartoma

Benign

Most common benign lung tumor, composed of cartilage, fat, and other mesenchymal tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined nodule with characteristic 'popcorn' calcification and/or macroscopic fat attenuation (negative HU) within the lesion.

Differential
  • Chondroid hamartoma (classic popcorn calcification)
  • Lipomatous hamartoma (fat-predominant)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic hamartoma with diagnostic fat/calcification.

6. Calcified granuloma

Benign

Healed prior infectious nodule, extremely common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, densely and often centrally or diffusely calcified nodule, frequently with associated calcified hilar lymph nodes from the same prior infection.

Differential
  • Healed histoplasmosis/tuberculosis (most common)
  • Other prior granulomatous infection
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic calcified granuloma.

7. Part-solid / subsolid nodule

Concerning

Nodule with a mixed ground-glass and solid component, part of the adenocarcinoma spectrum.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodule containing both a ground-glass component and a solid component; the solid component size correlates with invasive potential.

Differential
  • Atypical adenomatous hyperplasia (pure ground-glass, smaller)
  • Adenocarcinoma in situ / minimally invasive adenocarcinoma
  • Invasive adenocarcinoma (larger solid component)
Next Step

Fleischner/Lung-RADS-based surveillance; growth or an enlarging solid component warrants resection consideration.

8. Cavitary pulmonary nodule/mass

Concerning

Nodule or mass containing a gas-filled cavity, with wall thickness helping estimate malignant risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent cavity within a nodule or mass; wall thickness >15 mm favors malignancy, while thin walls (<4 mm) favor benign etiology, though overlap exists.

Differential
  • Cavitary squamous cell carcinoma (thick, irregular wall)
  • Infection (TB, fungal, abscess)
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis
  • Rheumatoid nodule
Next Step

Correlate wall characteristics and clinical context; tissue diagnosis if malignancy suspected.

9. Pulmonary metastases

Concerning

Secondary malignant deposits in the lung, most often hematogenous.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple, often bilateral, well-defined nodules of variable size, typically more numerous in the lower lobes and peripheral/subpleural in distribution.

Differential
  • Hematogenous metastases from a known extrathoracic primary
  • Multifocal primary lung malignancy (less common)
  • Multiple granulomas
Next Step

Correlate with known malignancy history; oncology referral for staging.

10. Carcinoid tumor

Concerning

Neuroendocrine lung tumor, often centrally located and endobronchial.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often endobronchial or peribronchial mass, avidly enhancing given its vascularity; may cause post-obstructive atelectasis/pneumonia.

Differential
  • Endobronchial lung cancer
  • Foreign body (if acute obstruction)
  • Mucus plug
Next Step

Thoracic surgery/oncology referral; typically has a more favorable prognosis than other lung malignancies.

11. Rounded atelectasis

Benign

Folded, atelectatic lung adjacent to pleural thickening, a benign mimic of a pulmonary mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded, peripheral opacity abutting thickened pleura with curvilinear bronchovascular markings swirling into the mass ('comet tail sign'), often in the setting of prior asbestos exposure or pleural disease.

Differential
  • Pulmonary mass/malignancy (key differential requiring recognition of the comet tail sign)
Next Step

Recognize the classic appearance to avoid unnecessary biopsy; correlate with asbestos exposure history and stability on follow-up.

12. Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation

Variable

Abnormal direct connection between a pulmonary artery and vein, bypassing the capillary bed.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined nodule with a visible feeding artery and draining vein connecting it to the pulmonary vasculature, best seen on contrast-enhanced or angiographic imaging.

Differential
  • Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT)-associated (often multiple)
  • Idiopathic/sporadic
Next Step

Interventional radiology referral for embolization given risk of paradoxical embolism/stroke and hemoptysis, particularly for feeding vessels ≥3 mm.

Lung Cancer & Staging

8 findings

1. Primary lung adenocarcinoma

Concerning

Most common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer, often peripheral and arising from the adenocarcinoma-in-situ spectrum.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral, spiculated or lobulated solid or part-solid mass/nodule; may show pleural tagging or a pleural indentation.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (more often central)
  • Metastasis (if known extrathoracic primary)
  • Organizing infection
Next Step

Pulmonology/thoracic oncology referral for biopsy, molecular testing, and staging.

2. Squamous cell carcinoma

Concerning

Non-small cell lung cancer subtype, classically central and associated with cavitation.

Imaging Characteristics

Central, often endobronchial mass, frequently causing post-obstructive atelectasis/pneumonia; cavitation is more common than in adenocarcinoma.

Differential
  • Small cell lung cancer (also central, but typically more bulky nodal disease)
  • Carcinoid tumor
Next Step

Pulmonology/thoracic oncology referral for staging; strong association with smoking.

3. Small cell lung cancer

Concerning

Aggressive, centrally located neuroendocrine lung malignancy with early nodal/metastatic spread.

Imaging Characteristics

Central perihilar mass with bulky mediastinal lymphadenopathy, often disproportionately large relative to the primary tumor; frequently presents with SVC obstruction.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
  • Lymphoma (bulky mediastinal disease)
Next Step

Oncology referral for urgent staging (limited vs. extensive stage) and treatment initiation given rapid growth.

4. Pancoast (superior sulcus) tumor

Concerning

Lung cancer arising at the apex, with a propensity to invade the brachial plexus, chest wall, and sympathetic chain.

Imaging Characteristics

Apical mass with invasion of the chest wall, ribs, or brachial plexus; correlate with clinical Horner syndrome or arm pain/weakness.

Differential
  • Apical pleural thickening/cap (benign mimic)
  • Apical infection (TB, fungal)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology referral; MRI often used to assess brachial plexus/vascular invasion for staging.

5. Lung cancer with malignant pleural effusion/invasion

Concerning

Locally advanced lung cancer with pleural spread, upstaging the tumor to at least T4/M1a.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural effusion, often exudative, with nodular pleural thickening or discrete pleural nodules in a patient with a known or suspected lung primary.

Differential
  • Malignant pleural effusion
  • Reactive parapneumonic effusion (if infection also present)
Next Step

Thoracentesis with cytology for confirmation; affects staging and treatment eligibility.

6. Lung cancer with mediastinal invasion

Concerning

Locally advanced tumor invading mediastinal structures, affecting resectability.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal fat plane between the tumor and mediastinal structures (great vessels, esophagus, trachea, heart), sometimes with frank encasement.

Differential
  • T4 disease by mediastinal invasion criteria
Next Step

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology review for resectability and treatment planning.

7. Lymphangitic carcinomatosis

Concerning

Diffuse tumor infiltration of the pulmonary lymphatics, most often from breast, lung, stomach, or pancreatic primaries.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth or nodular interlobular septal thickening and peribronchovascular thickening, often asymmetric, with preserved lung volumes (distinguishing from fibrosis, which causes volume loss).

Differential
  • Pulmonary edema (usually more symmetric, gravity-dependent)
  • Sarcoidosis (nodular septal thickening, but typically with volume preservation and different distribution)
Next Step

Oncology referral; poor prognostic finding warranting correlation with the underlying malignancy status.

8. Post-obstructive atelectasis/pneumonia

Concerning

Lung collapse or infection distal to an obstructing endobronchial lesion, most often a central tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Lobar or segmental atelectasis/consolidation with a central obstructing mass at the bronchus, sometimes with the 'S sign of Golden' (central mass causing convex bulge in an otherwise concave collapsed lobe border).

Differential
  • Central endobronchial malignancy (most common in older smokers)
  • Mucus plug
  • Foreign body aspiration
  • Broncholith
Next Step

Bronchoscopy to identify and biopsy the obstructing lesion; especially important if the consolidation fails to resolve with treatment.

Pulmonary Embolism & Pulmonary Vascular

8 findings

1. Acute pulmonary embolism, central/saddle

Concerning

Thrombus lodged at the main pulmonary artery bifurcation or extending into both main pulmonary arteries.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect straddling the pulmonary artery bifurcation (saddle embolus) or within a main pulmonary artery on CT pulmonary angiography.

Differential
  • Acute PE (most common)
  • Chronic PE (if the clot appears organized/eccentric with vessel wall involvement)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; assess for right heart strain and hemodynamic instability to determine need for thrombolysis/thrombectomy.

2. Acute pulmonary embolism, segmental/subsegmental

Concerning

Smaller thrombus within a segmental or subsegmental pulmonary artery branch.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within a segmental or subsegmental pulmonary artery, partially or completely occluding the vessel.

Differential
  • Acute PE
  • Motion/breathing artifact (can mimic subsegmental filling defects, a known pitfall)
Next Step

Anticoagulation per clinical risk stratification; correlate clinically given the higher false-positive rate for isolated subsegmental findings.

3. Chronic pulmonary embolism / CTEPH signs

Concerning

Chronic thromboembolic disease causing pulmonary hypertension.

Imaging Characteristics

Eccentric, webbed, or bandlike filling defects adherent to the vessel wall (rather than centrally located acute clot), with vessel wall thickening, mosaic attenuation of the lung parenchyma, and pulmonary artery enlargement.

Differential
  • Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH)
  • Pulmonary artery sarcoma (rare, can mimic chronic PE)
Next Step

Pulmonary hypertension/CTEPH specialist referral; distinct management from acute PE, including consideration of pulmonary thromboendarterectomy.

4. Pulmonary infarct

Variable

Area of lung tissue death from vascular occlusion, a complication of PE.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral, pleural-based, wedge-shaped opacity with the apex pointing toward the hilum ('Hampton hump'), corresponding to the occluded vessel's territory.

Differential
  • Pulmonary infarct from PE (most common cause)
  • Pneumonia (less classically wedge-shaped)
Next Step

Treat the underlying PE; infarcts typically resolve slowly over weeks and may cavitate or leave residual scarring.

5. Right heart strain (CT signs)

Concerning

CT findings suggesting hemodynamically significant PE with right ventricular compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

RV:LV diameter ratio >1.0 on axial images, straightening or bowing of the interventricular septum toward the left ventricle, and reflux of contrast into the IVC/hepatic veins.

Differential
  • Submassive/massive PE with right heart strain
Next Step

Escalate care per PE severity protocols; consider thrombolysis or advanced therapies per institutional pathway and hemodynamic status.

6. Pulmonary artery aneurysm

Concerning

Focal dilation of a pulmonary artery, uncommon but with rupture/dissection risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, fusiform or saccular dilation of the main or branch pulmonary artery.

Differential
  • Idiopathic/congenital
  • Behçet disease (pulmonary artery aneurysm classic association)
  • Mycotic aneurysm (infection)
  • Chronic PE-related dilation
Next Step

Vascular/pulmonology referral; assess for underlying vasculitis if multiple or in a young patient.

7. Pulmonary arteriovenous malformation (vascular)

Variable

See also under Pulmonary Nodules & Masses; listed here for its vascular significance.

Imaging Characteristics

Tangle of abnormal vessels with a feeding artery and draining vein, best characterized on contrast-enhanced CT or catheter angiography.

Differential
  • Hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia-associated
  • Sporadic AVM
Next Step

IR referral for embolization given stroke/abscess risk from paradoxical embolization.

8. Pulmonary hypertension, CT signs

Variable

Elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, suggested by several secondary CT findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Main pulmonary artery diameter >29 mm (or greater than the adjacent ascending aorta), right ventricular hypertrophy/dilation, and septal bowing.

Differential
  • Idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension
  • Secondary to chronic lung disease (Group 3)
  • Secondary to chronic PE (Group 4/CTEPH)
  • Left heart disease (Group 2)
Next Step

Cardiology/pulmonology referral for further workup including echocardiography and right heart catheterization.

Pleura

10 findings

1. Simple pleural effusion

Variable

Free-flowing fluid within the pleural space, with a broad differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, water-attenuation fluid layering dependently in the pleural space, often with associated passive atelectasis of the adjacent lung.

Differential
  • Transudative (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome)
  • Exudative (parapneumonic, malignant, other)
Next Step

Thoracentesis with Light's criteria if new or of unclear etiology.

2. Complex/loculated pleural effusion (empyema)

Concerning

Infected pleural fluid collection, often loculated and requiring drainage.

Imaging Characteristics

Loculated fluid collection with a thickened, enhancing pleural rind ('split pleura sign'), sometimes containing gas locules.

Differential
  • Empyema (infected effusion)
  • Complicated parapneumonic effusion (pre-empyema)
  • Hemothorax (if high attenuation)
Next Step

Chest tube drainage (often with fibrinolytics) or surgical decortication for organized empyema, plus antibiotics.

3. Hemothorax

Concerning

Blood within the pleural space, from trauma, anticoagulation, or malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural fluid with higher attenuation (35-70 HU) than simple fluid, sometimes with a layering hematocrit effect.

Differential
  • Traumatic hemothorax
  • Anticoagulant-related
  • Malignant pleural effusion with hemorrhage
Next Step

Chest tube drainage per trauma protocol; surgical evacuation if retained/large volume.

4. Pneumothorax, simple

Concerning

Air within the pleural space without hemodynamic compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

Visceral pleural line with absent lung markings peripheral to it; best assessed on lung windows.

Differential
  • Primary spontaneous (tall, thin patients)
  • Secondary spontaneous (underlying lung disease)
  • Traumatic/iatrogenic
Next Step

Size and symptom-based management (observation, needle aspiration, or chest tube).

5. Tension pneumothorax

Concerning

Pneumothorax with progressive air accumulation causing mediastinal shift and hemodynamic compromise, a clinical diagnosis requiring immediate treatment.

Imaging Characteristics

Large pneumothorax with contralateral mediastinal shift, ipsilateral diaphragmatic flattening/inversion, and often cardiovascular compromise.

Differential
  • Tension physiology (clinical emergency, treat before imaging confirmation if suspected)
Next Step

Immediate needle or finger thoracostomy/chest tube decompression; do not delay treatment for imaging if clinically suspected.

6. Pleural thickening, benign

Benign

Focal or diffuse thickening of the pleura from a prior inflammatory or infectious process.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, thin (<3 mm), non-circumferential thickening without an associated mass or nodularity.

Differential
  • Prior infection/inflammation (most common)
  • Prior hemothorax/effusion organization
Next Step

No action needed for typical benign thickening; correlate with occupational exposure history.

7. Pleural plaques (asbestos-related)

Benign

Focal fibrous pleural thickening from prior asbestos exposure, a marker of exposure rather than active disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete, often calcified plaques along the parietal pleura, classically involving the diaphragmatic pleura and posterolateral mid-lung zones, typically sparing the costophrenic angles.

Differential
  • Benign asbestos-related pleural plaques (most common)
  • Pleural metastases (if non-calcified and progressive, warrants distinction)
Next Step

No treatment needed; marker of asbestos exposure and a modest risk factor discussion for mesothelioma/lung cancer.

8. Malignant pleural mesothelioma

Concerning

Primary pleural malignancy strongly associated with asbestos exposure.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential, nodular pleural thickening (>1 cm) often encasing the lung and involving the mediastinal pleura, frequently with an associated effusion and volume loss of the affected hemithorax.

Differential
  • Pleural metastases (usually less circumferential/encasing)
  • Chronic empyema with pleural thickening
Next Step

Thoracic oncology referral; pleural biopsy for tissue diagnosis.

9. Pleural metastases

Concerning

Secondary malignant pleural involvement, most often from lung or breast primaries.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular pleural thickening or discrete pleural-based nodules, often with an associated malignant effusion.

Differential
  • Metastatic pleural disease
  • Mesothelioma (more circumferential/encasing)
Next Step

Correlate with known primary; thoracentesis/pleural biopsy for cytologic confirmation.

10. Chylothorax

Variable

Lymphatic (chyle) fluid within the pleural space, from thoracic duct injury or obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural effusion that may show fat-fluid levels or low attenuation from lipid content, though CT attenuation alone cannot definitively diagnose chylothorax.

Differential
  • Post-surgical/traumatic thoracic duct injury
  • Malignant obstruction of the thoracic duct (lymphoma most common)
Next Step

Pleural fluid triglyceride/chylomicron analysis for confirmation; treat the underlying cause.

Mediastinum - Anterior

8 findings

1. Thymoma

Variable

Tumor of thymic epithelial cells, the most common primary anterior mediastinal mass in adults, with a spectrum from benign to invasive.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often lobulated soft-tissue mass in the anterior mediastinum, which may show capsular invasion or pleural spread in more aggressive subtypes.

Differential
  • Thymic hyperplasia
  • Lymphoma
  • Germ cell tumor
Next Step

Correlate with myasthenia gravis symptoms (associated in a substantial subset); thoracic surgery referral for resection.

2. Thymic hyperplasia

Benign

Enlarged but histologically normal thymus, either from rebound growth (post-chemotherapy/stress) or true lymphoid hyperplasia.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, symmetric enlargement of the thymus maintaining its normal bilobed shape, without a discrete mass.

Differential
  • Normal thymus (age-dependent size)
  • Rebound thymic hyperplasia (post-chemotherapy/illness)
  • Thymoma (discrete mass, distinguishing feature)
Next Step

Correlate with recent chemotherapy/illness history; follow-up imaging to confirm regression if rebound hyperplasia suspected.

3. Mediastinal germ cell tumor (teratoma)

Variable

Germ cell tumor arising in the anterior mediastinum, benign (mature teratoma) to malignant.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined mass, often heterogeneous with fat, fluid, soft tissue, and/or calcification/ossification components (mature teratoma); malignant germ cell tumors are more infiltrative and homogeneous.

Differential
  • Mature teratoma (benign, most common)
  • Malignant germ cell tumor (seminoma, non-seminomatous)
Next Step

Tumor markers (AFP, beta-hCG) and thoracic surgery/oncology referral.

4. Mediastinal lymphoma

Concerning

Lymphomatous involvement of the anterior mediastinum, most often Hodgkin or primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Bulky, often lobulated soft-tissue mass that can encase mediastinal vessels without typically invading them (distinguishing feature from invasive carcinoma).

Differential
  • Thymoma
  • Germ cell tumor
  • Metastatic lymphadenopathy conglomerate
Next Step

Oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

5. Substernal/retrosternal thyroid goiter

Variable

Extension of an enlarged thyroid gland into the superior mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass contiguous with the cervical thyroid gland extending into the superior mediastinum, often with coarse or curvilinear calcifications and cystic components; tracheal deviation/compression may be present.

Differential
  • Benign multinodular goiter (most common)
  • Thyroid malignancy (less common, look for invasive features)
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral, particularly if causing airway compression.

6. Morgagni hernia

Benign

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia through the anterior (retrosternal) foramen, often containing omental fat.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior mediastinal/cardiophrenic angle mass, typically containing fat and sometimes bowel, contiguous with the abdominal cavity through a diaphragmatic defect.

Differential
  • Pericardial fat pad (no discrete hernia sac/defect)
  • Pericardial cyst
Next Step

Surgical referral for repair, particularly if symptomatic or containing bowel.

7. Anterior mediastinal lipoma

Benign

Benign fat-containing tumor of the anterior mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, homogeneous fat-attenuation mass without soft-tissue components or enhancement.

Differential
  • Mediastinal lipomatosis (diffuse fat deposition rather than a discrete mass)
  • Liposarcoma (rare; look for soft-tissue components/septal thickening)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic homogeneous lipoma.

8. Thymic cyst

Benign

Congenital or acquired cystic lesion of the thymus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, thin-walled, fluid-attenuation lesion in the expected thymic location without a solid component.

Differential
  • Congenital thymic cyst
  • Acquired thymic cyst (post-inflammatory, post-radiation)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic simple thymic cyst.

Mediastinum - Middle & Posterior

7 findings

1. Bronchogenic cyst

Benign

Congenital foregut duplication cyst, most often near the carina or in the middle mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, round, fluid-attenuation lesion, though attenuation can be higher than simple fluid due to proteinaceous or hemorrhagic content; no enhancement.

Differential
  • Esophageal duplication cyst (more posterior/esophageal-associated)
  • Pericardial cyst (more anterior/cardiophrenic)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic (airway compression) or if diagnosis is uncertain.

2. Esophageal duplication cyst

Benign

Congenital foregut duplication cyst closely associated with the esophageal wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, fluid-attenuation cystic lesion in the posterior mediastinum, intimately associated with the esophageal wall.

Differential
  • Bronchogenic cyst
  • Neurogenic tumor (if solid components present)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic; usually incidental and benign otherwise.

3. Neurogenic tumor (schwannoma/neurofibroma)

Variable

Tumor arising from nerve sheath or ganglion cells, the most common posterior mediastinal mass, typically in the paravertebral region.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, paravertebral soft-tissue mass, sometimes with a 'dumbbell' shape extending through a neural foramen; may show pressure erosion of adjacent ribs/vertebrae from slow growth.

Differential
  • Schwannoma/neurofibroma (benign, most common)
  • Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (larger, more rapid growth)
  • Ganglioneuroma/neuroblastoma (pediatric)
Next Step

Thoracic surgery referral for resection or biopsy if symptomatic or growing.

4. Paraspinal abscess

Concerning

Infection of the paraspinal soft tissues, often extending from vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection adjacent to the spine, often with associated vertebral body/disc space destruction if from contiguous spread.

Differential
  • Vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis with paraspinal extension
  • Tuberculous (Pott) abscess
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage plus antibiotics; MRI spine for full extent of associated osteomyelitis.

5. Extramedullary hematopoiesis

Benign

Compensatory blood cell production outside the bone marrow, seen in chronic severe anemia (thalassemia, myelofibrosis).

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral, lobulated, paravertebral soft-tissue masses, often with fat components, in a patient with a known chronic hemolytic or myeloproliferative disorder.

Differential
  • Paravertebral neurogenic tumors (usually unilateral, no chronic anemia history)
  • Paraspinal lymphoma
Next Step

No treatment needed for the masses themselves; manage the underlying hematologic condition.

6. Mediastinitis

Concerning

Infection of the mediastinal soft tissues, from esophageal perforation, post-surgical complication, or descending neck infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Mediastinal fat stranding/edema, fluid collections, and sometimes gas within the mediastinum; correlate with recent surgery, esophageal instrumentation, or deep neck infection.

Differential
  • Post-sternotomy mediastinitis
  • Esophageal perforation-related (Boerhaave syndrome)
  • Descending necrotizing mediastinitis from oropharyngeal infection
Next Step

Surgical emergency; broad-spectrum antibiotics and surgical debridement/drainage.

7. Pneumomediastinum

Variable

Free air within the mediastinum, from a range of benign to life-threatening causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent streaks of gas outlining mediastinal structures (great vessels, trachea, esophagus), sometimes extending into the neck (subcutaneous emphysema).

Differential
  • Spontaneous pneumomediastinum (Valsalva/vomiting-related, usually benign)
  • Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave, concerning)
  • Tracheobronchial injury
  • Asthma/severe coughing
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; esophagram or CT with oral contrast if esophageal perforation is suspected given the very different management.

Lymphadenopathy

6 findings

1. Reactive mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy

Benign

Mildly enlarged lymph nodes from a reactive process.

Imaging Characteristics

Oval nodes with preserved fatty hilum, generally <1 cm short axis, without conglomeration.

Differential
  • Reactive to infection/inflammation
  • Post-infectious residual
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; typically no isolated workup needed.

2. Malignant mediastinal/hilar lymphadenopathy

Concerning

Enlarged lymph nodes suspicious for metastatic or lymphomatous involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged (>1 cm short axis), rounded nodes with loss of fatty hilum, often multiple stations involved or conglomerated into a mass.

Differential
  • Metastatic lung cancer
  • Lymphoma
  • Metastasis from an extrathoracic primary
Next Step

EBUS or mediastinoscopy for tissue sampling and staging.

3. Sarcoidosis lymphadenopathy

Benign

Granulomatous disease with a classic pattern of symmetric bilateral hilar and right paratracheal lymphadenopathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, bilateral hilar and right paratracheal nodal enlargement ('1-2-3 sign' or Garland triad), often with preserved node architecture.

Differential
  • Sarcoidosis (classic pattern)
  • Lymphoma (less symmetric)
  • Tuberculosis (often asymmetric, may show necrosis)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical presentation; biopsy (often via EBUS) if diagnosis is not established, to exclude lymphoma/infection.

4. Calcified lymph nodes, eggshell pattern

Benign

Lymph nodes with a thin, peripheral rim of calcification.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodes with a thin, curvilinear peripheral calcific rim ('eggshell' calcification).

Differential
  • Silicosis/coal worker's pneumoconiosis (classic association)
  • Sarcoidosis (less common pattern)
  • Prior histoplasmosis/tuberculosis
Next Step

Correlate with occupational exposure history; no acute action needed for the calcification itself.

5. Castleman disease

Variable

Lymphoproliferative disorder, unicentric (localized) or multicentric, that can mimic lymphoma or other nodal disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Unicentric type: solitary, markedly enhancing (hypervascular) mass, often in the mediastinum. Multicentric type: widespread lymphadenopathy.

Differential
  • Lymphoma
  • Metastatic lymphadenopathy
  • Hypervascular metastasis (unicentric type)
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral for biopsy and subtype-specific management.

6. Tuberculous lymphadenitis

Concerning

Mycobacterial infection of lymph nodes, often with characteristic necrotic appearance.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged nodes with central low attenuation (necrosis) and peripheral rim enhancement, often with surrounding inflammatory change; may be unilateral/asymmetric.

Differential
  • Active tuberculosis
  • Nontuberculous mycobacterial infection
  • Necrotic metastatic nodes
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing and respiratory isolation precautions pending workup.

Airways - Trachea & Bronchi

8 findings

1. Tracheal stenosis

Concerning

Narrowing of the tracheal lumen, from prior intubation/tracheostomy, malignancy, or inflammatory disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or long-segment narrowing of the tracheal lumen, often at the site of a prior tracheostomy/cuff or endotracheal tube balloon.

Differential
  • Post-intubation/tracheostomy stricture (most common acquired cause)
  • Tracheal malignancy
  • Relapsing polychondritis
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis
Next Step

Pulmonology/ENT referral for bronchoscopic evaluation and possible intervention (dilation, stenting).

2. Tracheomalacia

Variable

Excessive tracheal wall collapsibility, causing dynamic airway narrowing.

Imaging Characteristics

Marked reduction (typically >50%) in tracheal cross-sectional area on expiratory imaging compared to inspiration.

Differential
  • Idiopathic/age-related
  • Secondary to chronic external compression (goiter, vascular ring)
  • Post-tracheostomy
Next Step

Pulmonology referral; correlate with expiratory symptoms (dyspnea, cough) and consider bronchoscopy for dynamic assessment.

3. Bronchiectasis

Variable

Irreversible bronchial dilation, from a range of infectious, congenital, and inflammatory causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Bronchial internal diameter greater than the adjacent pulmonary artery ('signet ring sign'), lack of normal bronchial tapering, and/or bronchial wall thickening; may show mucus plugging (tree-in-bud pattern) if infected.

Differential
  • Post-infectious bronchiectasis
  • Cystic fibrosis
  • Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis
  • Primary ciliary dyskinesia
  • Traction bronchiectasis from pulmonary fibrosis
Next Step

Pulmonology referral for etiology workup; airway clearance therapy and treatment of exacerbations.

4. Endobronchial tumor / foreign body

Concerning

Obstructing lesion within the bronchial lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue attenuation lesion filling or narrowing the bronchial lumen, often with post-obstructive atelectasis/pneumonia distally.

Differential
  • Endobronchial malignancy (carcinoid, squamous cell carcinoma)
  • Aspirated foreign body (correlate with choking history)
  • Mucus plug (typically lower attenuation, may clear with bronchial hygiene)
Next Step

Bronchoscopy for direct visualization, biopsy, and/or foreign body removal.

5. Broncholithiasis

Variable

Calcified material (often a calcified lymph node) eroding into and obstructing a bronchus.

Imaging Characteristics

Calcified endobronchial or peribronchial density, often associated with adjacent calcified lymph nodes and post-obstructive changes.

Differential
  • Prior granulomatous lymphadenitis with erosion into the airway
Next Step

Bronchoscopy for removal if causing obstruction/recurrent infection or hemoptysis.

6. Tracheobronchomegaly (Mounier-Kuhn syndrome)

Benign

Congenital dilation of the trachea and main bronchi.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly dilated trachea (typically >3 cm) and main bronchi, often with tracheal wall corrugation/diverticula.

Differential
  • Congenital tracheobronchomegaly
  • Chronic dilation from other causes (rare)
Next Step

Correlate with recurrent respiratory infections; pulmonology referral for management of associated bronchiectasis.

7. Saber-sheath trachea

Benign

Coronal narrowing with sagittal widening of the intrathoracic trachea, associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Intrathoracic trachea shows a markedly reduced coronal (side-to-side) diameter relative to its sagittal (front-to-back) diameter; the extrathoracic trachea is normal.

Differential
  • COPD-associated saber-sheath deformity (classic association)
Next Step

No specific treatment for the tracheal shape itself; correlate with underlying COPD management.

8. Bronchial atresia

Benign

Congenital focal obliteration of a segmental or subsegmental bronchus, with the distal lung supplied by collateral ventilation.

Imaging Characteristics

Branching, tubular, mucus-filled structure (mucocele) at the obstructed bronchus with surrounding hyperlucent, air-trapped lung supplied by collateral ventilation.

Differential
  • Congenital bronchial atresia (classic location: left upper lobe apicoposterior segment)
  • Bronchogenic cyst (if mucocele is rounded rather than branching)
Next Step

Usually asymptomatic and incidental; no treatment needed unless recurrent infection occurs.

Interstitial Lung Disease Patterns

12 findings

1. Usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern

Concerning

Radiologic pattern strongly associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, characterized by basal, subpleural fibrosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Basal and subpleural-predominant reticulation with honeycombing (clustered subpleural cystic airspaces) and traction bronchiectasis, typically without significant ground-glass opacity.

Differential
  • Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (classic UIP pattern)
  • Connective tissue disease-associated UIP
  • Chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (can mimic)
Next Step

Pulmonology referral for multidisciplinary diagnosis (may require biopsy if pattern is atypical) and antifibrotic therapy consideration.

2. Nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP) pattern

Concerning

Interstitial pneumonia pattern with more ground-glass and less honeycombing than UIP, often associated with connective tissue disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Basal-predominant ground-glass opacity and reticulation with relative subpleural sparing, and typically absent or minimal honeycombing.

Differential
  • Idiopathic NSIP
  • Connective tissue disease-associated (most common association)
  • Drug-induced lung disease
  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis
Next Step

Rheumatologic workup for underlying connective tissue disease; pulmonology referral for management.

3. Hypersensitivity pneumonitis

Variable

Immune-mediated lung disease from inhaled antigen exposure (birds, mold, others).

Imaging Characteristics

Upper/mid-lung predominant ground-glass opacity with poorly defined centrilobular nodules and mosaic attenuation with air trapping ('headcheese sign' when combined with fibrosis/normal lung).

Differential
  • Acute/subacute hypersensitivity pneumonitis (reversible with antigen avoidance)
  • Chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (fibrotic, can mimic UIP)
Next Step

Identify and remove the causative antigen exposure; pulmonology referral.

4. Sarcoidosis, parenchymal pattern

Variable

Granulomatous disease with characteristic perilymphatic nodular distribution in the lungs.

Imaging Characteristics

Small nodules distributed along the bronchovascular bundles, interlobular septa, and subpleural regions (perilymphatic distribution), often with associated hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Differential
  • Sarcoidosis (classic distribution)
  • Lymphangitic carcinomatosis (also perilymphatic, but usually less nodular/more linear)
  • Silicosis (upper lobe predominant nodules)
Next Step

Pulmonology referral; biopsy (often via EBUS of associated lymph nodes) for confirmation.

5. Pulmonary fibrosis, nonspecific

Concerning

General term for lung scarring identified on imaging without a specific pattern classification.

Imaging Characteristics

Reticulation, architectural distortion, and volume loss, without clearly fitting a specific UIP/NSIP pattern.

Differential
  • Various interstitial lung disease etiologies
  • Post-infectious/post-ARDS fibrosis
Next Step

Pulmonology referral for further characterization and etiology workup.

6. Ground-glass opacity, diffuse

Variable

Hazy increased lung attenuation that does not obscure underlying vessels, with an extremely broad differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Hazy areas of increased attenuation through which bronchovascular markings remain visible (distinguishing from consolidation, which obscures them).

Differential
  • Infection (viral, PJP)
  • Pulmonary edema
  • Hemorrhage
  • Hypersensitivity pneumonitis
  • Early/atypical malignancy (adenocarcinoma spectrum)
Next Step

Correlate closely with clinical context, distribution, and acuity given the broad differential.

7. Crazy paving pattern

Concerning

Ground-glass opacity with superimposed interlobular septal thickening, resembling irregular paving stones.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse ground-glass opacity with superimposed smooth interlobular septal thickening in a polygonal pattern.

Differential
  • Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis (classic association)
  • Severe pulmonary edema
  • Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage
  • Severe infection (viral, PJP)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; bronchoalveolar lavage often needed for definitive diagnosis.

8. Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis

Variable

Rare disease of surfactant accumulation within the alveoli.

Imaging Characteristics

Geographic, sharply demarcated areas of ground-glass opacity with superimposed septal thickening (crazy paving), often with a striking contrast to adjacent normal lung.

Differential
  • Idiopathic (autoimmune, most common)
  • Secondary (hematologic malignancy, dust exposure, infection)
Next Step

Pulmonology referral; whole lung lavage is the mainstay of treatment for symptomatic disease.

9. Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)

Concerning

Rare cystic lung disease affecting women of reproductive age, associated with tuberous sclerosis in some cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, thin-walled cysts of relatively uniform size distributed throughout both lungs without zonal predominance, in otherwise normal-appearing intervening lung.

Differential
  • Pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis (cysts often more bizarre-shaped, upper lobe predominant, in smokers)
  • Birt-Hogg-Dubé syndrome (cysts often basilar/subpleural)
Next Step

Pulmonology referral; screen for tuberous sclerosis and renal angiomyolipomas; mTOR inhibitor therapy consideration.

10. Pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis

Variable

Smoking-related cystic and nodular lung disease, predominantly affecting young adult smokers.

Imaging Characteristics

Upper and mid-lung predominant nodules and irregular/bizarre-shaped cysts, sparing the costophrenic angles, in a current or former smoker.

Differential
  • Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (uniform cysts, no smoking association, no nodules)
  • Emphysema (lacks the nodular component)
Next Step

Smoking cessation counseling (can lead to regression); pulmonology referral.

11. Silicosis / coal worker's pneumoconiosis

Variable

Occupational lung disease from inhalation of silica or coal dust.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, well-defined nodules in the upper lobes, which may coalesce into progressive massive fibrosis (large conglomerate masses) in advanced disease; eggshell calcification of hilar nodes may be present.

Differential
  • Silicosis (mining, sandblasting, quarrying exposure)
  • Coal worker's pneumoconiosis (coal mining exposure)
Next Step

Occupational medicine referral; remove from further exposure and monitor for progressive massive fibrosis and TB risk.

12. Asbestosis

Variable

Interstitial fibrosis from asbestos exposure, distinct from the benign pleural plaques also caused by asbestos.

Imaging Characteristics

Basal and subpleural-predominant fibrosis resembling a UIP pattern, typically accompanied by pleural plaques which support the diagnosis.

Differential
  • Asbestosis (occupational history plus pleural plaques support diagnosis)
  • Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (no pleural plaques/exposure history)
Next Step

Occupational medicine referral; correlate with detailed exposure history.

Emphysema & Airways Obstruction

6 findings

1. Centrilobular emphysema

Variable

Most common emphysema subtype, associated with smoking, predominantly affecting the upper lobes.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small, round areas of low attenuation without visible walls, centered on the secondary pulmonary lobule, upper lobe predominant.

Differential
  • Smoking-related centrilobular emphysema (most common cause)
Next Step

Smoking cessation counseling; pulmonary function testing and pulmonology referral for symptomatic management.

2. Panlobular emphysema

Concerning

Emphysema subtype affecting the entire secondary pulmonary lobule uniformly, classically associated with alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, uniform decrease in lung attenuation with a paucity of visible vessels, often lower lobe predominant (in contrast to centrilobular emphysema).

Differential
  • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (classic association, especially in younger or non-smoking patients with lower lobe predominance)
  • Severe/advanced smoking-related emphysema
Next Step

Alpha-1 antitrypsin level testing, particularly in younger patients or those with a limited smoking history; pulmonology referral.

3. Paraseptal emphysema / bullae

Variable

Emphysema subtype affecting the periphery of the secondary pulmonary lobule, associated with spontaneous pneumothorax risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Subpleural distribution of emphysematous spaces, often along fissures, which can coalesce into larger bullae (>1 cm).

Differential
  • Paraseptal emphysema (may occur in younger patients without significant smoking history)
  • Bullous lung disease
Next Step

Correlate with pneumothorax risk, particularly in young, tall, thin patients; pulmonology referral if extensive.

4. Air trapping (mosaic attenuation)

Variable

Regional differences in lung attenuation from small airways disease, best demonstrated on expiratory imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Geographic areas of decreased attenuation that fail to increase in density on expiratory images (relative to normal lung, which increases in attenuation with exhalation), reflecting air trapping from small airways obstruction.

Differential
  • Small airways disease (bronchiolitis, asthma)
  • Chronic PE (mosaic perfusion pattern, a vascular rather than airways cause)
Next Step

Correlate with pulmonary function testing; pulmonology referral for further workup of the underlying small airways process.

5. Bronchiolitis obliterans (constrictive)

Concerning

Fibrotic small airways disease causing fixed airflow obstruction, seen after transplant, infection, or toxic inhalation.

Imaging Characteristics

Mosaic attenuation with air trapping on expiratory imaging, often with bronchial wall thickening and minimal-to-no ground-glass opacity.

Differential
  • Post-transplant (lung or hematopoietic stem cell) bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome
  • Post-infectious bronchiolitis obliterans
  • Toxic inhalation-related
Next Step

Pulmonology referral; pulmonary function testing to confirm fixed obstruction.

6. Giant bulla

Variable

Large emphysematous airspace (>1 cm, often much larger) that can compress adjacent normal lung.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, thin-walled, avascular lucent space, potentially occupying a significant portion of a hemithorax and compressing adjacent normal-appearing lung.

Differential
  • Bullous emphysema
  • Giant bulla mimicking pneumothorax on plain film (important distinction, as chest tube placement into a bulla can be harmful)
Next Step

Pulmonology/thoracic surgery referral, particularly if causing significant compressive symptoms (bullectomy consideration).

Cardiac & Pericardium

6 findings

1. Pericardial effusion (CT)

Variable

Fluid within the pericardial sac, incidentally noted or specifically assessed on chest CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid-attenuation stripe surrounding the heart within the pericardium; attenuation can suggest simple fluid, hemorrhage, or exudate.

Differential
  • Idiopathic/viral (most common for small effusions)
  • Malignant effusion
  • Uremic/inflammatory
  • Post-surgical/traumatic
Next Step

Echocardiography for hemodynamic assessment; see the cardiology atlas series for detailed pericardial disease workup.

2. Cardiac tamponade physiology (CT signs)

Concerning

Hemodynamically significant pericardial effusion, primarily a clinical/echocardiographic diagnosis with some supportive CT findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Large pericardial effusion with reflux of contrast into the IVC/azygos vein and flattening or bowing of the interventricular septum.

Differential
  • Cardiac tamponade (clinical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent echocardiography and cardiology/cardiothoracic surgery consultation for pericardiocentesis; see the cardiology atlas for full tamponade workup.

3. Cardiomegaly, incidental

Variable

Enlarged cardiac silhouette noted incidentally on chest CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased overall cardiac dimensions relative to the thoracic cavity, without further chamber-specific characterization on a non-gated study.

Differential
  • Cardiomyopathy
  • Valvular disease
  • Pericardial effusion (can mimic true cardiomegaly)
Next Step

Correlate with cardiac history; echocardiography for chamber-specific assessment (see the cardiology atlas series).

4. Coronary artery calcification, incidental

Variable

Calcified atherosclerotic plaque within the coronary arteries, noted incidentally on a non-gated chest CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete calcific densities along the expected course of the coronary arteries.

Differential
  • Coronary atherosclerosis (marker of cardiovascular risk)
Next Step

Correlate with cardiovascular risk factors; consider dedicated coronary calcium scoring or cardiology referral if not previously evaluated.

5. Pericardial thickening / constrictive pericarditis

Concerning

Thickened, often calcified pericardium restricting cardiac filling.

Imaging Characteristics

Pericardial thickening (>4 mm) and/or calcification, sometimes with associated findings of venous congestion (IVC dilation, ascites, pleural effusions).

Differential
  • Constrictive pericarditis (post-viral, post-surgical, post-radiation, tuberculous)
Next Step

Cardiology referral; see the cardiology atlas series for full constrictive physiology workup.

6. Cardiac chamber thrombus, incidental

Concerning

Thrombus within a cardiac chamber, occasionally noted incidentally on a contrast-enhanced chest CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within a cardiac chamber (most often the left atrial appendage or a dilated/akinetic ventricular segment) on contrast-enhanced imaging.

Differential
  • Atrial fibrillation-related left atrial appendage thrombus
  • Ventricular thrombus (post-MI, cardiomyopathy)
Next Step

Anticoagulation and cardiology referral; echocardiography for confirmation and further characterization.

Aorta & Great Vessels

8 findings

1. Thoracic aortic aneurysm

Concerning

Focal dilation of the thoracic aorta, with rupture risk increasing with size.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter exceeding normal limits for the segment (generally >4 cm ascending, >3.5 cm descending, though thresholds vary by reference), measured outer-wall to outer-wall.

Differential
  • Ascending aortic aneurysm (associated with bicuspid valve, connective tissue disease)
  • Descending aortic aneurysm (more often atherosclerotic)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/cardiothoracic surgery referral for surveillance or repair per size-based thresholds.

2. Aortic dissection, Stanford type A

Concerning

Dissection involving the ascending aorta, a surgical emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Intimal flap within the ascending aorta separating true and false lumens; assess for pericardial effusion, aortic regurgitation, and coronary/great vessel involvement.

Differential
  • Type A dissection (requires emergent surgery)
Next Step

Emergent cardiothoracic surgery activation; do not delay for additional imaging.

3. Aortic dissection, Stanford type B

Concerning

Dissection confined to the descending aorta (distal to the left subclavian artery origin), generally managed medically unless complicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Intimal flap within the descending thoracic aorta without ascending aortic involvement; assess for malperfusion of visceral/renal/limb vessels and for contained rupture.

Differential
  • Uncomplicated type B (medical management)
  • Complicated type B (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain/hypertension - requires intervention)
Next Step

Blood pressure/heart rate control; vascular surgery consultation, with intervention reserved for complicated cases.

4. Aortic intramural hematoma

Concerning

Hemorrhage within the aortic media without a discernible intimal tear, part of the acute aortic syndrome spectrum.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic or circumferential high-attenuation thickening of the aortic wall on non-contrast images, without an intimal flap or flow within the hematoma.

Differential
  • Intramural hematoma (can progress to frank dissection or resolve)
  • Severe atherosclerotic wall thickening (differentiate by acuity/attenuation)
Next Step

Managed similarly to dissection based on location (Type A vs. B); cardiothoracic/vascular surgery consultation.

5. Penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer

Concerning

Ulceration of an atherosclerotic plaque penetrating through the aortic intima into the media, part of the acute aortic syndrome spectrum.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, crater-like outpouching of contrast beyond the expected aortic lumen contour, arising from a background of severe atherosclerotic disease, often with adjacent intramural hematoma.

Differential
  • Penetrating ulcer (risk of progression to pseudoaneurysm, dissection, or rupture)
Next Step

Vascular surgery consultation; risk of complications guides need for intervention vs. surveillance.

6. Traumatic aortic injury

Concerning

Aortic wall injury from blunt or penetrating trauma, classically at the aortic isthmus, a life-threatening finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular aortic contour, intimal flap, pseudoaneurysm, or active extravasation, most often at the aortic isthmus just distal to the left subclavian artery origin, often with associated periaortic hematoma.

Differential
  • Traumatic aortic injury (surgical/endovascular emergency)
  • Ductus diverticulum (normal variant that can mimic minor injury, distinguished by smooth contour)
Next Step

Emergent cardiothoracic/vascular surgery consultation for endovascular or open repair.

7. Aortic coarctation

Variable

Congenital narrowing of the aorta, most often just distal to the left subclavian artery origin.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal narrowing of the aortic lumen at the classic juxtaductal location, often with poststenotic dilation and prominent collateral vessels (including rib notching on plain film, when present).

Differential
  • Congenital coarctation (often diagnosed in childhood, but can present in adulthood)
Next Step

Cardiology referral for hemodynamic assessment and consideration of intervention (stenting or surgical repair).

8. SVC obstruction / thrombosis

Concerning

Obstruction of the superior vena cava, from external compression (often malignancy) or thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Narrowing or occlusion of the SVC lumen with prominent collateral venous pathways (chest wall, azygos system) and often an adjacent mass or catheter-related thrombus.

Differential
  • Malignant extrinsic compression (lung cancer, lymphoma - most common)
  • Catheter/pacemaker lead-related thrombosis
  • Fibrosing mediastinitis
Next Step

Oncology workup if malignant compression suspected; anticoagulation and possible stenting for thrombotic obstruction.

Chest Wall & Bones

8 findings

1. Rib fracture

Variable

Fracture of one or more ribs, from trauma or, rarely, pathologic weakening.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of a rib, best assessed on bone windows; assess number of ribs involved, displacement, and for flail segment (multiple contiguous fractures in ≥2 places).

Differential
  • Traumatic fracture (most common)
  • Pathologic fracture (underlying lytic lesion)
  • Stress fracture (repetitive activity, e.g., first rib in overhead athletes)
Next Step

Pain control and pulmonary toilet to prevent atelectasis/pneumonia; assess for associated pneumothorax, hemothorax, or solid organ injury (lower rib fractures).

2. Sternal fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the sternum, typically from direct anterior chest trauma (steering wheel injury classic).

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the sternum, often at the manubriosternal junction, best assessed on sagittal reformats.

Differential
  • Traumatic sternal fracture
Next Step

Assess for associated cardiac contusion/injury given the mechanism; cardiology/trauma surgery correlation.

3. Vertebral compression fracture, thoracic

Variable

Loss of vertebral body height, benign (osteoporotic) or pathologic (malignant).

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped or biconcave loss of vertebral body height; benign fractures preserve posterior elements and marrow signal (if MRI available), while pathologic fractures show a soft-tissue mass or posterior element involvement.

Differential
  • Osteoporotic compression fracture
  • Pathologic fracture from metastasis or myeloma
  • Traumatic acute fracture
Next Step

Correlate with trauma mechanism and malignancy history; MRI if benign vs. pathologic distinction is unclear.

4. Lytic or sclerotic rib/chest wall lesion

Concerning

Focal bone lesion of the ribs or chest wall bones.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of bone destruction (lytic) or increased density (sclerotic) within a rib, sternum, clavicle, or scapula.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common in adults)
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Primary bone tumor
  • Fibrous dysplasia (benign)
Next Step

Correlate with malignancy history; further workup (biopsy, bone scan) if indeterminate or symptomatic.

5. Chest wall soft-tissue mass

Variable

Mass arising from the chest wall soft tissues rather than intrathoracic structures.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue mass centered in the chest wall musculature or subcutaneous tissue, distinct from underlying pleura/lung.

Differential
  • Lipoma (benign, most common)
  • Desmoid tumor
  • Soft-tissue sarcoma (larger, heterogeneous, invasive features)
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Correlate with growth rate and imaging characteristics; biopsy for indeterminate or enlarging masses.

6. Pectus excavatum

Benign

Congenital posterior depression of the sternum, the most common chest wall deformity.

Imaging Characteristics

Posteriorly displaced sternum with reduced anteroposterior chest diameter; cardiac structures may be displaced/compressed in severe cases (assessed by Haller index).

Differential
  • Congenital pectus excavatum
Next Step

Cardiothoracic surgery referral if severe (Haller index-based) or symptomatic (cardiopulmonary compromise).

7. Pectus carinatum

Benign

Congenital anterior protrusion of the sternum.

Imaging Characteristics

Anteriorly protruding sternum and costal cartilages.

Differential
  • Congenital pectus carinatum
Next Step

Usually a cosmetic concern; surgical/bracing referral if desired by the patient.

8. Costochondral / costal cartilage calcification

Benign

Calcification of the costal cartilages, a common age-related finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or stippled calcific densities following the expected course of the costal cartilages.

Differential
  • Normal age-related calcification
Next Step

No action needed; incidental finding.

Diaphragm

5 findings

1. Diaphragmatic hernia, traumatic

Concerning

Herniation of abdominal contents through a diaphragmatic defect from blunt or penetrating trauma, more often left-sided.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the diaphragm with abdominal contents (stomach, bowel, omental fat) herniating into the thorax; the 'collar sign' (waist-like constriction of herniated bowel at the defect) is a classic finding.

Differential
  • Traumatic diaphragmatic rupture (surgical repair needed)
Next Step

Surgical consultation for repair; high index of suspicion needed as this can be missed on initial imaging.

2. Hiatal hernia

Benign

Herniation of the stomach through the esophageal hiatus into the thorax, extremely common.

Imaging Characteristics

Gastric fundus/body located above the diaphragm within the posterior mediastinum, sometimes with an air-fluid level.

Differential
  • Sliding hiatal hernia (most common, GE junction displaced above diaphragm)
  • Paraesophageal hernia (GE junction normal position, fundus herniates alongside, higher volvulus/strangulation risk)
Next Step

Correlate with reflux symptoms; surgical referral for large or paraesophageal hernias given complication risk.

3. Diaphragmatic eventration

Benign

Congenital or acquired thinning and elevation of a portion (or all) of the diaphragm, without a true defect.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse elevation of the diaphragmatic contour with an intact, though thinned, diaphragmatic muscle.

Differential
  • Congenital eventration
  • Phrenic nerve palsy (acquired elevation, though muscle is paralyzed rather than congenitally absent)
Next Step

Usually incidental and asymptomatic; surgical plication considered if causing significant respiratory compromise.

4. Bochdalek hernia

Benign

Congenital posterolateral diaphragmatic defect, the most common congenital diaphragmatic hernia, usually left-sided.

Imaging Characteristics

Posterolateral diaphragmatic defect with herniation of retroperitoneal fat or, in larger defects, bowel/kidney into the thorax.

Differential
  • Small, asymptomatic Bochdalek hernia (common incidental finding in adults)
  • Large congenital diaphragmatic hernia (typically presents in neonates)
Next Step

Usually incidental in adults with small defects; surgical referral if symptomatic or large.

5. Diaphragmatic rupture, acute traumatic

Concerning

Acute tear of the diaphragm from severe blunt or penetrating trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Diaphragmatic discontinuity with herniated abdominal viscera in the thorax, an elevated or indistinct hemidiaphragm, or a nasogastric tube coursing above the expected diaphragmatic level.

Differential
  • Traumatic rupture (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent surgical repair; high clinical suspicion needed given imaging can be subtle acutely.

Esophagus

6 findings

1. Esophageal wall thickening / carcinoma

Concerning

Circumferential or focal esophageal wall thickening, with malignancy an important consideration.

Imaging Characteristics

Wall thickening >5 mm, focal and asymmetric thickening favoring malignancy over the more circumferential, symmetric thickening of esophagitis.

Differential
  • Esophageal carcinoma (squamous cell or adenocarcinoma)
  • Esophagitis (infectious, reflux, pill-induced)
  • Varices (if serpiginous rather than a discrete mass)
Next Step

GI referral for endoscopy and biopsy.

2. Esophageal dilation (achalasia pattern)

Variable

Dilated, fluid- and debris-filled esophagus from impaired peristalsis and lower esophageal sphincter relaxation.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly dilated esophagus with retained fluid/food debris and a smoothly tapered ('bird's beak') narrowing at the gastroesophageal junction.

Differential
  • Achalasia (primary motility disorder)
  • Pseudoachalasia (malignancy at the GE junction mimicking achalasia, important to exclude)
Next Step

GI referral for manometry and endoscopy (to exclude pseudoachalasia from malignancy).

3. Esophageal foreign body

Variable

Impacted swallowed object within the esophagus.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete object visualized within the esophageal lumen, most often impacted at a site of physiologic narrowing (cricopharyngeus, aortic arch level, GE junction) or an underlying stricture.

Differential
  • Food bolus impaction (common, may indicate underlying eosinophilic esophagitis or stricture)
  • Sharp/pointed foreign body (higher perforation risk)
  • Button battery (time-critical emergency)
Next Step

Urgent endoscopic removal, especially for complete obstruction, sharp objects, or batteries.

4. Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome)

Concerning

Full-thickness esophageal rupture, classically from forceful vomiting, a surgical emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Pneumomediastinum, mediastinal fluid/abscess, and often a left pleural effusion; oral contrast extravasation confirms the diagnosis and localizes the leak.

Differential
  • Boerhaave syndrome (forceful vomiting, classically after a large meal/alcohol binge)
  • Iatrogenic perforation (post-endoscopy/dilation)
Next Step

Surgical emergency; emergent surgical consultation and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

5. Esophageal diverticulum

Benign

Outpouching of the esophageal wall, at various levels (Zenker, mid-esophageal traction, epiphrenic).

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, sac-like outpouching from the esophageal lumen; Zenker diverticula arise posteriorly at the pharyngoesophageal junction, epiphrenic diverticula near the GE junction.

Differential
  • Zenker diverticulum (aspiration risk, dysphagia)
  • Traction diverticulum (mid-esophageal, from adjacent nodal disease historically)
  • Epiphrenic diverticulum (associated with motility disorders)
Next Step

GI/surgical referral if symptomatic (dysphagia, aspiration, halitosis).

6. Esophagitis, wall thickening pattern

Variable

Inflammation of the esophagus with a more circumferential, symmetric wall thickening pattern than malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, circumferential esophageal wall thickening, often with mucosal enhancement, typically involving a longer segment than a focal carcinoma.

Differential
  • Reflux esophagitis
  • Infectious esophagitis (Candida, CMV, HSV, especially in immunocompromised patients)
  • Pill-induced esophagitis
  • Eosinophilic esophagitis
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms and immune status; endoscopy with biopsy if infectious etiology or malignancy is a concern.

Trauma

7 findings

1. Pulmonary contusion

Concerning

Lung parenchymal bruising from blunt trauma, typically appearing and worsening within the first 24-48 hours.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-segmental, geographic areas of consolidation/ground-glass opacity adjacent to the site of impact, not respecting lobar/segmental boundaries (distinguishing it from pneumonia or aspiration).

Differential
  • Pulmonary contusion (peaks in severity around 24-72 hours, then improves)
  • Aspiration (if more dependent/gravity-related distribution)
Next Step

Supportive care (pain control, pulmonary toilet); monitor for worsening respiratory status over the first 24-48 hours.

2. Pulmonary laceration / traumatic pneumatocele

Variable

Tear in the lung parenchyma from trauma, often filling with air, blood, or both.

Imaging Characteristics

Round or oval air-, fluid-, or mixed air-fluid-filled cavity within an area of pulmonary contusion, representing the torn lung parenchyma.

Differential
  • Traumatic pneumatocele (air-filled)
  • Traumatic hematoma (blood-filled)
  • Mixed air-fluid pattern
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; monitor for secondary infection or hemoptysis.

3. Traumatic pneumothorax

Concerning

Air within the pleural space from blunt or penetrating chest trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Visceral pleural line with absent peripheral lung markings; assess for tension physiology and associated rib fractures/lung injury.

Differential
  • Traumatic pneumothorax (simple)
  • Tension pneumothorax (hemodynamic compromise)
Next Step

Chest tube placement per trauma protocol; immediate decompression if tension physiology present.

4. Traumatic hemothorax

Concerning

Blood within the pleural space from chest trauma, often from intercostal vessel, lung parenchymal, or great vessel injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural fluid with higher attenuation (35-70 HU) than simple fluid, layering dependently.

Differential
  • Traumatic hemothorax from intercostal vessel or lung injury
  • Traumatic hemothorax from great vessel injury (higher-risk mechanism)
Next Step

Chest tube drainage per trauma protocol; surgical evacuation if large initial output or ongoing significant bleeding.

5. Flail chest

Concerning

Segment of chest wall that moves paradoxically due to multiple contiguous rib fractures, each fractured in at least two places.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple contiguous ribs with fractures at two or more sites each, creating a free-floating chest wall segment; often associated with significant underlying pulmonary contusion.

Differential
  • Flail chest with paradoxical chest wall motion
Next Step

Aggressive pain control (often regional anesthesia), pulmonary toilet, and consideration of surgical rib fixation in select cases; monitor closely for respiratory failure.

6. Tracheobronchial injury

Concerning

Traumatic disruption of the trachea or bronchi, a rare but life-threatening injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Persistent large pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum despite chest tube placement, direct visualization of the airway wall defect, or the 'fallen lung sign' (lung collapsing away from the hilum due to loss of bronchial support).

Differential
  • Tracheobronchial injury (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent thoracic surgery/bronchoscopy for evaluation and repair.

7. Sternoclavicular joint dislocation

Concerning

Dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint, with posterior dislocation carrying risk of great vessel/airway injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Malalignment of the medial clavicle relative to the sternum; posterior dislocation shows the clavicular head displaced behind the sternum, potentially compressing mediastinal structures.

Differential
  • Anterior dislocation (more common, lower risk)
  • Posterior dislocation (higher risk given proximity to great vessels/trachea)
Next Step

Orthopedic/trauma surgery consultation, particularly urgent for posterior dislocations given vascular/airway compression risk.

Postoperative & Devices

6 findings

1. Central venous catheter malposition

Concerning

Central line positioned outside the expected location (cavoatrial junction).

Imaging Characteristics

Catheter tip located in an unexpected location such as the internal jugular vein, contralateral brachiocephalic vein, right atrium/ventricle, or extravascular space.

Differential
  • Correctly positioned catheter (tip at cavoatrial junction)
  • Malpositioned catheter (arrhythmia risk if intracardiac, perforation risk if extravascular)
Next Step

Reposition before use for infusion of vesicant/hypertonic solutions; notify the placing team promptly.

2. Chest tube position

Variable

Indwelling thoracostomy tube, assessed for appropriate intrathoracic position and function.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube with its most proximal side hole within the pleural space, directed toward the area of fluid/air to be evacuated (apically for pneumothorax, dependently for effusion).

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned tube
  • Malpositioned tube (side hole outside the pleural space, or positioned within a fissure)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical drainage/resolution; reposition if malfunctioning.

3. Endotracheal tube malposition

Concerning

ET tube positioned too high, too low (mainstem intubation), or otherwise incorrectly.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube tip ideally 3-5 cm above the carina; malposition includes right mainstem intubation (tip below the carina, often with left lung atelectasis) or a tip too high (risk of accidental extubation).

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned ETT
  • Right mainstem intubation (most common malposition)
Next Step

Immediate repositioning; right mainstem intubation causing contralateral atelectasis needs prompt correction.

4. Post-lobectomy / pneumonectomy changes

Benign

Expected postoperative changes after lung resection surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent lung parenchyma in the resected region with compensatory volume loss, ipsilateral mediastinal shift, and often surgical clips at the resection margin/hilum; post-pneumonectomy space progressively fills with fluid over time.

Differential
  • Expected post-surgical change
  • Post-pneumonectomy syndrome (severe mediastinal shift causing airway compression, uncommon complication)
Next Step

Correlate with surgical history; no action needed for expected postoperative appearance.

5. Sternotomy wires / cardiac surgical hardware

Benign

Expected metallic hardware from prior cardiac or thoracic surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Metallic sternal wires and/or surgical clips along the expected surgical bed; assess for wire fracture or displacement if there is clinical concern for sternal dehiscence.

Differential
  • Normal post-surgical hardware
  • Wire fracture/sternal dehiscence (if clinical concern for wound complication)
Next Step

No action for stable, intact hardware; correlate with exam if sternal instability is suspected.

6. Pacemaker / ICD lead position

Variable

Indwelling cardiac implantable electronic device, assessed for appropriate lead position.

Imaging Characteristics

Lead(s) coursing from the generator through the venous system into the expected right atrial and/or right ventricular position; assess for lead fracture, migration, or perforation (extending beyond the expected cardiac contour).

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned leads
  • Lead migration/malposition
  • Lead perforation (rare but serious, may cause pericardial effusion)
Next Step

Correlate with device interrogation; cardiology/electrophysiology referral if malposition or perforation suspected.

Incidental & Miscellaneous

6 findings

1. Azygos fissure / lobe

Benign

Congenital variant where the azygos vein courses through an accessory fissure, isolating a small portion of the right upper lobe.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear fissural line in the right upper lobe with a teardrop-shaped opacity at its inferior end (the azygos vein itself within its own mesentery-like fold).

Differential
  • Normal anatomic variant, no clinical significance
Next Step

No action needed; recognize as a normal variant.

2. Pericardial cyst

Benign

Congenital benign cystic lesion, most often at the cardiophrenic angle.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, thin-walled, fluid-attenuation lesion, most commonly in the right cardiophrenic angle, without enhancement.

Differential
  • Morgagni hernia (contains fat/bowel rather than simple fluid)
  • Pericardial fat pad (fat attenuation rather than fluid)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic simple pericardial cyst.

3. Fat embolism syndrome

Concerning

Pulmonary and systemic fat embolization, classically after long bone or pelvic fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse ground-glass opacities and/or small nodules, often with a delayed onset (24-72 hours after the inciting fracture), in a patient with recent long bone/pelvic trauma.

Differential
  • Fat embolism syndrome (classic triad: hypoxemia, neurologic changes, petechial rash)
  • ARDS from another cause
  • Pulmonary contusion
Next Step

Supportive respiratory care; correlate with the classic clinical triad and recent fracture history.

4. Foreign body aspiration

Concerning

Aspirated foreign material within the airway, more common in young children but occurs at any age.

Imaging Characteristics

Direct visualization of a radiopaque foreign body within the airway, or indirect signs (air trapping/hyperinflation on the affected side on expiratory imaging, post-obstructive atelectasis/pneumonia).

Differential
  • Radiopaque foreign body (directly visualized)
  • Radiolucent foreign body (inferred from air trapping/atelectasis)
Next Step

Bronchoscopy for removal; correlate with a choking history.

5. Situs anomaly (chest)

Benign

Abnormal left-right organ positioning affecting the thoracic organs.

Imaging Characteristics

Mirror-image or indeterminate positioning of the heart (dextrocardia/mesocardia), bronchial branching pattern, and great vessels relative to normal anatomy.

Differential
  • Situs inversus totalis (complete mirroring, usually benign/incidental if isolated)
  • Situs ambiguus/heterotaxy (associated with complex cardiac anomalies and splenic abnormalities)
Next Step

Usually incidental if isolated; heterotaxy syndromes warrant cardiac and splenic correlation.

6. Pulmonary sequestration

Benign

Congenital lung tissue mass that does not communicate normally with the tracheobronchial tree and receives an anomalous systemic arterial supply.

Imaging Characteristics

Lung mass or consolidation, most often in the lower lobes (left more common), with a characteristic anomalous systemic artery (often from the aorta) supplying the abnormal tissue, best seen on contrast-enhanced imaging.

Differential
  • Intralobar sequestration (shares pleura with normal lung, more common)
  • Extralobar sequestration (own pleural covering, often associated with other congenital anomalies)
Next Step

Thoracic surgery referral for resection, particularly if symptomatic (recurrent infection) or for definitive diagnosis.

Airspace Disease / Pneumonia Patterns

8 findings

1. Lobar consolidation

Concerning

Homogeneous opacification confined to a lobe, the classic chest X-ray pattern of bacterial pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous opacity respecting fissural boundaries with visible air bronchograms; silhouette sign helps localize (e.g., loss of the right heart border with right middle lobe pneumonia).

Differential
  • Bacterial pneumonia (most common)
  • Post-obstructive pneumonia (if a central mass is present)
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to likely pathogens; follow-up film in 6-8 weeks if risk factors for malignancy.

2. Silhouette sign

Variable

Loss of a normally visible anatomic border, used to localize the site of an adjacent opacity.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normally sharp interface between two structures of similar (soft-tissue/fluid) density, such as the right heart border (right middle lobe) or the diaphragm (lower lobe).

Differential
  • Consolidation obscuring the border (localizes the pneumonia to a specific lobe)
  • Pleural effusion
  • Mass
Next Step

Use to localize the disease process to a specific lobe/segment.

3. Interstitial pattern (reticular/reticulonodular)

Variable

Linear or net-like opacities suggesting interstitial rather than airspace disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Fine linear or reticulonodular markings, often bibasilar, without the confluent homogeneous opacity of consolidation.

Differential
  • Pulmonary edema (interstitial phase)
  • Interstitial lung disease/fibrosis
  • Atypical/viral pneumonia
  • Lymphangitic carcinomatosis
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization if the pattern is new or the diagnosis is unclear.

4. Air bronchogram

Variable

Air-filled bronchi made visible against surrounding opacified (fluid/pus-filled) alveoli, confirming an airspace rather than pleural process.

Imaging Characteristics

Branching lucent (air-filled) tubular structures visible within an area of consolidation.

Differential
  • Pneumonia (most common cause)
  • Pulmonary edema
  • Non-obstructive atelectasis (air bronchograms present, unlike obstructive atelectasis)
Next Step

Supports an airspace process; correlate with clinical picture.

5. Round pneumonia (plain film)

Benign

Spherical consolidation mimicking a mass, classically in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, rounded opacity, most often in a lower lobe.

Differential
  • Pediatric bacterial pneumonia (classic)
  • Pulmonary mass (key differential in adults)
Next Step

Follow-up film after treatment to confirm resolution, especially in adults.

6. Multifocal/patchy opacities (bronchopneumonia pattern)

Concerning

Patchy, multifocal airspace opacities not confined to a single lobe.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple, patchy areas of opacity scattered through one or both lungs, often more prominent at the bases.

Differential
  • Bacterial bronchopneumonia
  • Aspiration
  • Multifocal viral/atypical pneumonia
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to clinical setting; further workup if not improving as expected.

7. Cavitary opacity

Concerning

Lucency within an area of consolidation or a mass, suggesting necrosis/abscess formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent area, often with an air-fluid level, within a region of consolidation or a discrete mass.

Differential
  • Lung abscess
  • Cavitary malignancy
  • Necrotizing pneumonia
  • Cavitary infection (TB, fungal)
Next Step

CT for further characterization; correlate with clinical acuity and risk factors.

8. Diffuse bilateral airspace opacities

Concerning

Widespread bilateral opacification, with a broad and often severe differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral, often symmetric, patchy-to-confluent airspace opacities.

Differential
  • Pulmonary edema (cardiogenic)
  • ARDS
  • Multifocal pneumonia
  • Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage
Next Step

Correlate closely with clinical context (volume status, fever, trauma) to narrow the differential; further workup as indicated.

Pulmonary Nodule / Mass

5 findings

1. Solitary pulmonary nodule, plain film

Variable

Rounded opacity ≤3 cm identified on chest X-ray, requiring comparison to prior imaging and risk stratification.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined round or oval opacity; benign calcification patterns (central, popcorn, diffuse) may be visible, though CT is far more sensitive for characterizing calcification and margins.

Differential
  • Granuloma
  • Primary lung malignancy
  • Metastasis
  • Hamartoma
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization if no prior comparison is available or the nodule is new/growing.

2. Pulmonary mass, plain film

Concerning

Opacity >3 cm, statistically more likely to be malignant than a smaller nodule.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined or irregular opacity >3 cm, with irregular/spiculated margins raising greater concern.

Differential
  • Primary lung malignancy (higher likelihood given size)
  • Metastasis
  • Less likely: organizing infection/abscess
Next Step

CT chest and pulmonology referral for tissue diagnosis.

3. Coin lesion, calcified (benign pattern)

Benign

Solitary round opacity with a calcification pattern suggesting a benign etiology.

Imaging Characteristics

Central, popcorn, laminated, or diffuse ("solid") calcification pattern within a well-defined round nodule.

Differential
  • Calcified granuloma (most common)
  • Hamartoma (popcorn pattern)
Next Step

No further workup needed for a classic benign calcification pattern.

4. Multiple pulmonary nodules, plain film

Concerning

More than one discrete nodular opacity, raising concern for metastatic or infectious/granulomatous disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple, variably sized round opacities scattered through one or both lungs.

Differential
  • Pulmonary metastases
  • Multiple granulomas (if calcified, more reassuring)
  • Septic emboli
  • Miliary infection (if innumerable and tiny)
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization; correlate with known malignancy or infectious symptoms.

5. Cavitary nodule/mass, plain film

Concerning

Nodule or mass with an internal lucency suggesting cavitation.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent area within a nodule/mass, sometimes with an air-fluid level; wall thickness is better assessed on CT.

Differential
  • Cavitary malignancy
  • Lung abscess
  • Cavitary infection (TB, fungal)
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis
Next Step

CT chest for wall characterization and further workup.

Pleural Findings

7 findings

1. Pleural effusion, plain film

Variable

Fluid within the pleural space, visible once a sufficient volume has accumulated.

Imaging Characteristics

Blunting of the costophrenic angle on upright film (requires ~200-300 mL); larger effusions show a meniscus-shaped opacity rising laterally; supine films show a hazy density over the affected hemithorax without a discrete meniscus.

Differential
  • Transudative (heart failure, cirrhosis)
  • Exudative (parapneumonic, malignant)
Next Step

Lateral decubitus view to confirm free-flowing fluid and estimate volume; thoracentesis for new/unexplained effusions.

2. Subpulmonic effusion

Variable

Pleural fluid layering beneath the lung rather than in the costophrenic angle, an easily missed pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Apparent elevation of the hemidiaphragm with lateral peaking of its apex (rather than the normal central peak), sometimes with increased separation between the apparent diaphragm and the gastric bubble on the left.

Differential
  • Subpulmonic effusion
  • True diaphragmatic elevation (eventration, phrenic palsy)
Next Step

Lateral decubitus view or ultrasound to confirm and quantify.

3. Loculated pleural effusion / empyema

Concerning

Pleural fluid collection that does not layer freely, suggesting loculation from infection or organization.

Imaging Characteristics

Lenticular (biconvex), fixed opacity that does not change position or shape on decubitus views, unlike free-flowing fluid.

Differential
  • Empyema
  • Organized hemothorax
  • Loculated malignant effusion
Next Step

CT and/or ultrasound for further characterization; drainage typically required for empyema.

4. Pleural thickening / scarring

Benign

Chronic pleural thickening from a prior inflammatory process.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, often blunted costophrenic angle or linear pleural density without an acute meniscus sign, stable on serial films.

Differential
  • Prior effusion/infection with residual scarring
  • Prior hemothorax
Next Step

No action needed if stable; correlate with prior imaging.

5. Pleural plaques (asbestos-related), plain film

Benign

Focal pleural thickening from asbestos exposure, a marker of exposure.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete areas of pleural thickening, sometimes calcified, along the lateral chest wall or diaphragmatic pleura.

Differential
  • Benign asbestos-related pleural plaques
Next Step

No treatment needed; correlate with occupational exposure history.

6. Hydropneumothorax

Concerning

Combination of air and fluid within the pleural space.

Imaging Characteristics

Air-fluid level within the pleural space on an upright film, with lung collapse above the level.

Differential
  • Traumatic/iatrogenic (post-thoracentesis, chest tube)
  • Bronchopleural fistula (infection eroding into the airway)
  • Ruptured esophagus with pleural extension
Next Step

Chest tube drainage; identify and treat the underlying cause.

7. Mesothelioma / diffuse pleural thickening, plain film

Concerning

Circumferential or extensive pleural thickening concerning for malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular, often circumferential pleural thickening with volume loss of the affected hemithorax.

Differential
  • Malignant mesothelioma
  • Pleural metastases
  • Chronic empyema with thickening
Next Step

CT chest and pleural biopsy for tissue diagnosis.

Pneumothorax

5 findings

1. Pneumothorax, plain film

Concerning

Air within the pleural space, visible as an absence of lung markings peripheral to a visceral pleural line.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin, sharp visceral pleural line with absent lung markings peripheral to it; best seen on an upright expiratory film for small pneumothoraces.

Differential
  • Primary spontaneous (tall, thin patients)
  • Secondary spontaneous (underlying lung disease)
  • Traumatic/iatrogenic
Next Step

Size and symptom-based management (observation, aspiration, or chest tube).

2. Tension pneumothorax, plain film

Concerning

Pneumothorax with mediastinal shift and hemodynamic compromise, a clinical diagnosis that should not await imaging confirmation.

Imaging Characteristics

Large pneumothorax with contralateral mediastinal/tracheal shift and ipsilateral diaphragmatic flattening/depression.

Differential
  • Tension physiology (clinical emergency)
Next Step

Immediate needle or finger thoracostomy; treat clinically before imaging if suspected.

3. Deep sulcus sign

Concerning

Subtle sign of pneumothorax on a supine film, where air collects anteromedially/anterobasally rather than at the apex.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormally deep, lucent costophrenic angle on a supine film, without the classic apical lucency seen on upright films.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax on a supine film (important not to miss, especially in trauma/ICU patients)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical status; CT or upright/decubitus film if uncertain.

4. Pneumothorax ex vacuo

Benign

Pneumothorax resulting from lung collapse (e.g., from a mucus plug or malignant obstruction) rather than a primary pleural air leak.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural air associated with a collapsed lobe/lung from an obstructive process, rather than a true pleural defect.

Differential
  • Post-obstructive lung collapse with secondary pneumothorax
Next Step

Address the underlying obstruction (bronchoscopy); chest tube is often less effective since there is no primary air leak.

5. Loculated pneumothorax

Variable

Pneumothorax that does not follow the expected gravitational distribution due to adhesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Air collection in an atypical, fixed location (not apical on an upright film) due to pleural adhesions preventing free movement of air.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax with underlying pleural adhesions (prior surgery, infection)
Next Step

CT for full characterization; may complicate chest tube placement/positioning.

Mediastinal Widening / Contour

6 findings

1. Widened mediastinum, plain film

Concerning

Increased mediastinal width, a nonspecific but important finding, particularly after trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Mediastinal width >8 cm on an upright AP film (technique-dependent), or a mediastinum-to-chest width ratio >0.25.

Differential
  • Traumatic aortic injury (key concern after trauma)
  • Mediastinal hematoma
  • Mediastinal mass/lymphadenopathy
  • Technical/positioning artifact (common cause of false widening on portable AP films)
Next Step

CT angiography of the chest if traumatic aortic injury is a concern; otherwise correlate with clinical context.

2. Anterior mediastinal mass, plain film

Variable

Opacity in the anterior mediastinal compartment on lateral view.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass filling the retrosternal clear space on the lateral film, silhouetting the ascending aorta/heart border on frontal view.

Differential
  • Thymoma
  • Teratoma/germ cell tumor
  • Thyroid goiter (substernal)
  • Lymphoma
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization.

3. Middle mediastinal mass/widening

Variable

Opacity centered in the middle mediastinal compartment.

Imaging Characteristics

Widening or a discrete mass silhouetting the trachea/central vascular structures.

Differential
  • Lymphadenopathy (reactive, malignant, sarcoid)
  • Bronchogenic cyst
  • Vascular abnormality (aneurysm)
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization.

4. Posterior mediastinal mass, plain film

Variable

Opacity centered in the posterior mediastinal/paravertebral compartment.

Imaging Characteristics

Paravertebral soft-tissue opacity, sometimes with associated rib/vertebral erosion from a slow-growing lesion.

Differential
  • Neurogenic tumor
  • Extramedullary hematopoiesis
  • Descending aortic aneurysm
Next Step

CT (or MRI, particularly if a neurogenic tumor is suspected) for further characterization.

5. Tracheal deviation

Variable

Displacement of the trachea from its expected midline position.

Imaging Characteristics

Trachea shifted away from midline, either pushed by a mass/effusion or pulled by volume loss (atelectasis/fibrosis).

Differential
  • Large pleural effusion or tension pneumothorax (pushes trachea away)
  • Atelectasis/post-lobectomy/fibrosis (pulls trachea toward the affected side)
  • Mediastinal mass
Next Step

Correlate with the direction of shift and other findings to determine the underlying cause.

6. Pneumomediastinum, plain film

Variable

Free air within the mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent streaks outlining mediastinal structures, sometimes with the 'continuous diaphragm sign' (air outlining the central diaphragm beneath the heart) and extension into the neck (subcutaneous emphysema).

Differential
  • Spontaneous (Valsalva-related, usually benign)
  • Esophageal perforation (concerning)
  • Tracheobronchial injury
  • Asthma exacerbation
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; further workup (esophagram/CT) if perforation is suspected.

Cardiac Silhouette

6 findings

1. Cardiomegaly, plain film

Variable

Enlarged cardiac silhouette on chest X-ray, assessed by cardiothoracic ratio.

Imaging Characteristics

Cardiothoracic ratio >0.5 on a properly positioned PA film (unreliable on AP/portable films due to magnification).

Differential
  • Cardiomyopathy
  • Valvular disease
  • Pericardial effusion (can mimic true cardiomegaly - 'water bottle' configuration)
Next Step

Correlate with cardiac history; echocardiography for further characterization (see the cardiology atlas series).

2. Pulmonary vascular congestion / edema pattern

Concerning

Chest X-ray findings of elevated pulmonary venous pressure, classically progressing through stages.

Imaging Characteristics

Cephalization (upper lobe venous redistribution) progressing to interstitial edema (Kerley B lines, peribronchial cuffing) and then alveolar edema (bilateral perihilar 'bat wing' opacities) with more severe elevation in pressure.

Differential
  • Cardiogenic pulmonary edema
  • Volume overload (renal failure)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical volume status; treat the underlying cause of elevated cardiac filling pressures.

3. Water bottle heart configuration

Concerning

Symmetric enlargement of the cardiac silhouette resembling a water bottle or flask, suggesting a large pericardial effusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, symmetric enlargement of the cardiac silhouette with sharp margins and rapid interval change in size (compared to true cardiomegaly, which typically enlarges more gradually).

Differential
  • Large pericardial effusion
  • Global cardiomyopathy (less classically symmetric/flask-shaped)
Next Step

Echocardiography for confirmation and assessment of tamponade physiology; see the cardiology atlas for full workup.

4. Left atrial enlargement, plain film signs

Variable

Chest X-ray signs suggesting an enlarged left atrium.

Imaging Characteristics

Straightening of the left heart border, splaying of the carinal angle, and a double density/contour overlying the right heart border.

Differential
  • Mitral valve disease
  • Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction with elevated filling pressures
Next Step

Echocardiography for confirmation (see the cardiology atlas series).

5. Calcified cardiac valve, plain film

Variable

Visible calcification overlying an expected valve location.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear calcific density projecting over the expected location of the aortic or mitral valve, best confirmed with fluoroscopy or CT if uncertain.

Differential
  • Calcific aortic stenosis
  • Mitral annular calcification
Next Step

Echocardiography for functional assessment (see the cardiology atlas series).

6. Pericardial calcification, plain film

Variable

Calcification of the pericardium, associated with constrictive pericarditis.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear calcific density outlining the cardiac silhouette, best seen on the lateral view.

Differential
  • Constrictive pericarditis (classic association, though not all patients with pericardial calcification have constriction)
  • Prior tuberculous or viral pericarditis
Next Step

Cardiology referral; echocardiography for constrictive physiology assessment.

8 findings

1. Endotracheal tube position, plain film

Variable

ET tube position assessed relative to the carina.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube tip ideally positioned 3-5 cm above the carina with the neck in neutral position.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned ETT
  • Right mainstem intubation (tip below carina, often with left-sided atelectasis)
  • Tube positioned too high (risk of accidental extubation)
Next Step

Reposition immediately if malpositioned; reassess after any neck position change.

2. Central venous catheter position, plain film

Variable

CVC tip position assessed relative to the cavoatrial junction.

Imaging Characteristics

Tip ideally at the cavoatrial junction (roughly at the level of the carina to 2 cm below); malposition includes the internal jugular vein, contralateral brachiocephalic vein, or a curled/looped course.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned catheter
  • Malpositioned catheter (arrhythmia or thrombosis risk depending on location)
Next Step

Reposition before use for vesicant infusions if malpositioned.

3. Nasogastric/orogastric tube position, plain film

Variable

NG/OG tube position assessed for appropriate gastric placement.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube coursing in the midline below the diaphragm with the tip in the stomach, ideally past the GE junction with side holes below the diaphragm.

Differential
  • Correctly positioned tube
  • Esophageal coiling
  • Bronchial malposition (tube coursing into a mainstem bronchus, dangerous if feeds are given)
Next Step

Reposition before use if malpositioned; do not use for feeds/medications until confirmed.

4. Chest tube position, plain film

Variable

Thoracostomy tube position assessed for appropriate intrapleural placement.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube with its most proximal side hole (marked by a radiopaque line break) within the expected pleural space, directed apically for air or dependently for fluid.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned tube
  • Malpositioned tube (side hole outside the pleural space or within the fissure/lung parenchyma)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical output/resolution; reposition if malfunctioning.

5. Pacemaker/ICD lead position, plain film

Variable

Cardiac device lead position assessed on chest X-ray.

Imaging Characteristics

Lead(s) coursing from the generator through the venous system to the expected right atrial/ventricular position.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned leads
  • Lead fracture (visible discontinuity)
  • Lead migration/malposition
Next Step

Correlate with device interrogation; cardiology/EP referral if abnormality suspected.

6. Feeding tube malposition, plain film

Concerning

Post-pyloric feeding tube positioned outside the expected gastrointestinal course.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube tip located outside the expected stomach/duodenum location, including a bronchial or pleural course.

Differential
  • Correctly positioned feeding tube
  • Bronchial/pleural malposition (dangerous if feeds are administered)
Next Step

Confirm position before initiating feeds; reposition if malpositioned.

7. Surgical clips/hardware, plain film

Benign

Expected metallic material from prior surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete metallic densities along a surgical bed (sternotomy wires, vascular clips, mediastinal clips).

Differential
  • Normal post-surgical material
Next Step

No action; correlate with surgical history.

8. Retained thoracic foreign body/hardware

Variable

Retained surgical item or unexpected foreign material within the thorax.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete radiopaque object inconsistent with expected surgical hardware, or a radiopaque marker consistent with a retained sponge.

Differential
  • Retained surgical item (gossypiboma)
  • Expected hardware
Next Step

Surgical consultation for removal if a retained item is confirmed.

Bones & Soft Tissue

6 findings

1. Rib fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of one or more ribs, though chest X-ray has limited sensitivity compared to CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of a rib; non-displaced fractures are easily missed on plain film.

Differential
  • Traumatic fracture
  • Pathologic fracture (underlying lesion)
Next Step

CT for further characterization if clinical suspicion is high despite a negative or equivocal plain film; assess for associated pneumothorax/hemothorax.

2. Clavicle fracture

Variable

Fracture of the clavicle, common after falls or direct trauma to the shoulder.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the clavicle, most often at the middle third.

Differential
  • Traumatic fracture (most common)
  • Pathologic fracture (rare)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; assess for associated brachial plexus or vascular injury with severely displaced fractures.

3. Scapular fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the scapula, uncommon and associated with high-energy trauma mechanisms.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the scapular body, neck, or glenoid.

Differential
  • High-energy traumatic fracture (marker of significant force, prompts search for associated injuries)
Next Step

Trauma survey for associated injuries (pulmonary contusion, rib fractures, vascular injury) given the high-energy mechanism typically required.

4. Lytic or sclerotic rib lesion, plain film

Concerning

Focal bone lesion identified within a rib.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of bone destruction (lytic) or increased density (sclerotic) within a rib.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease
  • Multiple myeloma
  • Primary bone tumor
  • Fibrous dysplasia (benign)
Next Step

CT for further characterization; correlate with malignancy history.

5. Subcutaneous emphysema

Variable

Air within the subcutaneous soft tissues of the chest wall/neck.

Imaging Characteristics

Streaky, lucent tracking through the subcutaneous tissues, often extending into the neck and chest wall.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax with tracking air (most common)
  • Pneumomediastinum with tracking air
  • Necrotizing soft-tissue infection (if associated with systemic toxicity, a key differential to exclude)
Next Step

Identify and treat the underlying source (chest tube for pneumothorax); assess for necrotizing infection if clinical picture is concerning.

6. Chest wall soft-tissue mass, plain film

Variable

Soft-tissue opacity projecting over the chest wall rather than the lung.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass with margins that can be seen extending beyond the expected lung contour on tangential views, sometimes with a sharp lateral border and obtuse angle with the chest wall (extrapleural sign).

Differential
  • Chest wall lipoma/soft-tissue mass
  • Pleural-based mass (can mimic)
Next Step

CT chest for further characterization and to confirm extrapulmonary location.

Mediastinal Masses

8 findings

1. Thymoma, MRI characterization

Variable

Thymic epithelial tumor, characterized by MRI when further tissue characterization is needed beyond CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated soft-tissue mass, typically intermediate T1 and T2 signal with signal drop on chemical shift imaging useful to distinguish from thymic hyperplasia (which shows more uniform fat-related drop).

Differential
  • Thymic hyperplasia
  • Lymphoma
  • Germ cell tumor
Next Step

Thoracic surgery referral for resection; correlate with myasthenia gravis symptoms.

2. Thymic cyst, MRI

Benign

Benign cystic thymic lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense, T1-variable (depending on protein/hemorrhagic content) lesion without a solid enhancing component.

Differential
  • Congenital or acquired thymic cyst
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic simple cyst.

3. Mediastinal lymphoma, MRI

Concerning

Lymphomatous mediastinal mass, occasionally characterized by MRI, particularly in patients who cannot receive iodinated contrast.

Imaging Characteristics

Bulky soft-tissue mass with intermediate T1, intermediate-to-high T2 signal, and restricted diffusion; may encase but typically does not invade mediastinal vessels.

Differential
  • Thymoma
  • Germ cell tumor
  • Metastatic lymphadenopathy
Next Step

Oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

4. Neurogenic tumor, MRI characterization

Variable

Posterior mediastinal/paravertebral nerve sheath or ganglion cell tumor, for which MRI is the modality of choice given superior soft-tissue and neural foraminal detail.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined paravertebral mass, often T2-hyperintense, with possible extension through a neural foramen (dumbbell shape); MRI best assesses spinal canal involvement.

Differential
  • Schwannoma/neurofibroma (benign)
  • Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor
  • Ganglioneuroma/neuroblastoma (pediatric)
Next Step

Thoracic surgery/neurosurgery referral, particularly if there is intraspinal extension.

5. Bronchogenic cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital foregut duplication cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly T2-hyperintense lesion; T1 signal is variable depending on proteinaceous/hemorrhagic content, without an enhancing solid component.

Differential
  • Esophageal duplication cyst
  • Pericardial cyst (location-dependent)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic; otherwise no treatment needed.

6. Extramedullary hematopoiesis, MRI

Benign

Compensatory blood cell production outside the marrow, seen in chronic severe anemia.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral, lobulated paravertebral masses with signal characteristics reflecting the degree of fatty versus hematopoietic marrow conversion.

Differential
  • Chronic hemolytic anemia (thalassemia)
  • Myeloproliferative disorder (myelofibrosis)
Next Step

No treatment needed for the masses themselves; manage the underlying hematologic condition.

7. Germ cell tumor, MRI characterization

Variable

Anterior mediastinal germ cell tumor, benign to malignant.

Imaging Characteristics

Mature teratoma shows heterogeneous signal from fat, fluid, and soft-tissue components; malignant germ cell tumors show more homogeneous, infiltrative soft-tissue signal with restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Mature teratoma (benign)
  • Malignant germ cell tumor
Next Step

Tumor markers and thoracic surgery/oncology referral.

8. Paraspinal abscess, MRI

Concerning

Infection of the paraspinal soft tissues, with MRI as the modality of choice given superior detail of associated spinal involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense, rim-enhancing collection adjacent to the spine, often with associated vertebral body/disc T2 hyperintensity and enhancement if from contiguous osteomyelitis/discitis.

Differential
  • Vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis with paraspinal extension
  • Tuberculous (Pott) abscess
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage plus antibiotics; MRI is the study of choice for assessing epidural extension.

Chest Wall & Brachial Plexus

5 findings

1. Brachial plexus tumor/infiltration

Concerning

Mass involving the brachial plexus, from a primary nerve sheath tumor or invasion by an adjacent lung apex tumor (Pancoast).

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal T2-hyperintense signal and/or mass effect involving the brachial plexus roots/trunks, best assessed with dedicated brachial plexus MRI protocol.

Differential
  • Pancoast tumor invasion (correlate with apical lung mass)
  • Primary nerve sheath tumor
  • Radiation-induced brachial plexopathy (more diffuse, symmetric signal change)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology/neurosurgery referral for staging and treatment planning.

2. Chest wall soft-tissue mass, MRI characterization

Variable

Mass arising from the chest wall soft tissues, characterized by MRI for local extent and tissue type.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal characteristics vary by tissue type; fat-containing lesions (lipoma) show characteristic fat suppression, while sarcomas show heterogeneous signal with restricted diffusion and avid enhancement.

Differential
  • Lipoma (benign)
  • Desmoid tumor (locally aggressive but non-metastasizing)
  • Soft-tissue sarcoma
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Biopsy for indeterminate or enlarging masses; surgical oncology referral if malignant features present.

3. Desmoid tumor (chest wall)

Concerning

Locally aggressive but non-metastasizing fibrous tumor, which can occur in the chest wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Infiltrative, often ill-defined mass with variable T2 signal (low-signal bands reflect dense collagen), avid enhancement, and a tendency to infiltrate along fascial planes.

Differential
  • Soft-tissue sarcoma (key differential)
  • Post-surgical scar (in a patient with prior chest wall surgery)
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral; can be locally aggressive and recur even after resection.

4. Radiation-induced chest wall/brachial plexus change

Variable

Post-radiation fibrosis affecting the chest wall or brachial plexus.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, symmetric T2 signal change and fibrotic thickening in the radiation field, without a discrete enhancing mass (distinguishing from tumor recurrence/infiltration).

Differential
  • Radiation fibrosis (benign, expected post-treatment change)
  • Tumor recurrence (discrete mass, restricted diffusion)
Next Step

Correlate with radiation history and field; serial imaging if a discrete new mass is identified.

5. Rib/sternal marrow infiltration

Concerning

Abnormal marrow signal within the ribs or sternum, suggesting infiltrative disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Replacement of normal fatty marrow T1 signal with intermediate-to-low signal, often with corresponding STIR/T2 hyperintensity and enhancement.

Differential
  • Metastatic marrow infiltration
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Osteomyelitis (if infectious, usually more focal with cortical destruction)
Next Step

Correlate with malignancy history; oncology referral and biopsy if indeterminate.

Variable

Fluid within the pericardial sac, characterized by cardiac MRI when further tissue characterization (simple vs. complex/hemorrhagic) is needed.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense (simple) or heterogeneous/T1-variable (hemorrhagic or exudative) fluid surrounding the heart within the pericardium.

  • Idiopathic/viral

Differential
  • Constrictive pericarditis

See the cardiology atlas series for full constrictive physiology workup.

3. Cardiac/paracardiac mass, MRI characterization

Concerning

Mass involving or adjacent to the heart, characterized by MRI for tissue type and extent.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal characteristics and enhancement pattern help distinguish tumor type (myxoma, sarcoma, thrombus, metastasis); see the cardiology atlas series for detailed cardiac mass characterization.

Differential
  • Cardiac myxoma
  • Cardiac sarcoma
  • Thrombus (no enhancement, key distinguishing feature)
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Cardiology/cardiac surgery referral; see the cardiology atlas series for full workup.

4. Myocardial infiltration/mass extension from lung cancer

Concerning

Direct extension of an adjacent lung or mediastinal malignancy into the pericardium or myocardium.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue signal extending from a known thoracic malignancy into the pericardium or myocardial wall, with loss of the normal fat plane.

Differential
  • Direct tumor extension (T4 disease)

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology review; affects staging and resectability.

Great Vessels / Aorta (MRA)

7 findings

1. Thoracic aortic aneurysm, MRA

Concerning

Focal aortic dilation, assessed by MR angiography, particularly useful for serial surveillance without radiation.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter exceeding normal limits for the segment, with assessment of the aortic root, ascending, arch, and descending segments.

Differential
  • Ascending aneurysm (bicuspid valve, connective tissue disease association)
  • Descending aneurysm (atherosclerotic)
Next Step

Vascular/cardiothoracic surgery referral for surveillance or repair per size-based thresholds.

2. Aortic dissection, MRA

Concerning

Intimal flap and true/false lumen assessed by MR angiography, an alternative when CT is contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Intimal flap separating true and false lumens; cine sequences can assess flap motion and branch vessel involvement.

Differential
  • Stanford type A (surgical emergency)
  • Stanford type B (often medical management unless complicated)
Next Step

Emergent surgical evaluation for type A; medical management with surgical backup for uncomplicated type B.

3. Aortic coarctation, MRA

Variable

Congenital aortic narrowing, well characterized by MRA including flow quantification across the stenosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal narrowing at the classic juxtaductal location with poststenotic dilation; phase-contrast sequences can quantify the pressure gradient and collateral flow.

Differential
  • Congenital coarctation
Next Step

Cardiology referral for hemodynamic assessment and treatment planning (stenting vs. surgical repair).

4. Pulmonary artery abnormality, MRA

Variable

Pulmonary arterial abnormalities including aneurysm, stenosis, or anomalous origin, characterized by MRA.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation (aneurysm), narrowing (stenosis), or abnormal origin of a pulmonary artery branch, with flow quantification possible on phase-contrast sequences.

Differential
  • Pulmonary artery aneurysm
  • Congenital pulmonary artery stenosis
  • Anomalous pulmonary venous return (associated finding)
Next Step

Cardiology/cardiothoracic surgery referral depending on the specific abnormality and hemodynamic significance.

5. Vascular ring/sling

Variable

Congenital anomaly of the aortic arch or pulmonary artery branching that encircles or compresses the trachea/esophagus.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal course of the aortic arch or a pulmonary artery branch creating a ring or sling around the trachea and/or esophagus, best delineated with 3D MRA.

Differential
  • Double aortic arch
  • Right aortic arch with aberrant left subclavian artery
  • Pulmonary artery sling
Next Step

Cardiothoracic surgery referral, particularly if causing airway or esophageal compression symptoms.

6. SVC obstruction, MRA/MRV

Concerning

Obstruction of the superior vena cava, assessed by MR venography when CT is contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Narrowing or occlusion of the SVC with prominent collateral venous flow on delayed venous phase imaging.

Differential
  • Malignant extrinsic compression
  • Catheter/lead-related thrombosis
  • Fibrosing mediastinitis
Next Step

Oncology workup if malignant compression suspected; anticoagulation for thrombotic causes.

7. Innominate (brachiocephalic) artery compression of the trachea

Variable

Anterior tracheal compression by an anomalously coursing or pulsatile innominate artery, more often discussed in pediatric populations.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior indentation/narrowing of the trachea corresponding to the course of the innominate artery, best assessed with dynamic imaging correlating pulsation with the airway.

Differential
  • Innominate artery compression syndrome
  • Normal anatomic variant without significant compression
Next Step

Cardiothoracic surgery referral if causing significant respiratory symptoms (rare in adults).

Pleural & Pulmonary

4 findings

1. Pleural effusion, MRI

Variable

Pleural fluid characterized by MRI when CT/ultrasound are insufficient or contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense fluid within the pleural space; signal heterogeneity can suggest hemorrhagic or proteinaceous content.

Differential
  • Transudative vs. exudative effusion
  • Hemothorax
  • Empyema
Next Step

Thoracentesis for fluid analysis as clinically indicated.

2. Pleural/chest wall tumor invasion, MRI

Concerning

Assessment of chest wall or pleural invasion by an adjacent lung malignancy, a key staging question where MRI outperforms CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal fat plane between tumor and chest wall, with abnormal signal extending into chest wall musculature; a dynamic cine sequence can assess for fixation during respiration.

Differential
  • Chest wall invasion (T3/T4 disease, affects resectability)
  • Tumor abutting but not invading the chest wall
Next Step

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology review for staging and surgical planning.

3. Pulmonary nodule/mass, MRI characterization

Variable

Lung nodule or mass, occasionally further characterized by MRI, particularly for superior sulcus (Pancoast) tumors given superior soft-tissue contrast.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal and enhancement characteristics assessed, though CT remains the primary modality for most pulmonary nodule characterization.

Differential
  • Primary lung malignancy
  • Metastasis
  • Benign nodule
Next Step

CT remains first-line; MRI reserved for specific indications such as Pancoast tumor staging.

4. Diaphragmatic invasion, MRI

Concerning

Assessment of diaphragmatic invasion by an adjacent lung or pleural malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption of the normal thin diaphragmatic contour with tumor signal extending across it, best assessed with dynamic respiratory cine imaging.

Differential
  • Diaphragmatic invasion (affects staging)
  • Tumor abutting but not invading the diaphragm
Next Step

Multidisciplinary thoracic oncology review for staging.

Diaphragm & Esophagus

4 findings

1. Diaphragmatic paralysis/eventration, MRI

Benign

Elevated, thinned diaphragm from paralysis (phrenic nerve palsy) or congenital eventration, assessed with dynamic MRI.

Imaging Characteristics

Elevated hemidiaphragm with paradoxical or absent motion on dynamic cine sequences during a sniff maneuver.

Differential
  • Phrenic nerve palsy (paradoxical motion)
  • Congenital eventration (thinned but present muscle)
Next Step

Correlate with respiratory symptoms; pulmonology/thoracic surgery referral if significantly symptomatic.

2. Esophageal mass, MRI staging

Concerning

Esophageal malignancy, with MRI used selectively for local staging when CT/EUS are insufficient.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential or focal wall thickening with variable T2 signal and restricted diffusion; assess for periesophageal fat invasion and adjacent structure involvement.

Differential
  • Esophageal carcinoma
  • Esophagitis (more symmetric, less mass-like)
Next Step

GI/thoracic oncology referral; endoscopic ultrasound remains a key complementary staging tool.

3. Esophageal duplication cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital foregut duplication cyst intimately associated with the esophageal wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly T2-hyperintense cystic lesion closely applied to the esophageal wall, without an enhancing solid component.

Differential
  • Bronchogenic cyst
  • Neurogenic tumor (if solid components present)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic; otherwise incidental and benign.

4. Achalasia, MRI signs

Variable

Esophageal motility disorder occasionally imaged with MRI as part of a broader mediastinal workup.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated, fluid-and-debris-filled esophagus with smooth tapering at the gastroesophageal junction; dynamic sequences may show absent peristalsis.

Differential
  • Primary achalasia
  • Pseudoachalasia from a GE junction malignancy (important to exclude)
Next Step

GI referral for manometry and endoscopy to exclude pseudoachalasia.

Miscellaneous

5 findings

1. Fibrosing mediastinitis, MRI

Concerning

Fibrotic mediastinal process, often from prior histoplasmosis, encasing mediastinal structures.

Imaging Characteristics

Infiltrative, often calcified soft tissue encasing and narrowing the central airways, pulmonary vessels, and/or SVC, with variable T2 signal depending on disease activity.

Differential
  • Post-histoplasmosis fibrosing mediastinitis (classic cause in endemic regions)
  • IgG4-related disease
  • Malignancy (key differential, particularly if non-calcified and progressive)
Next Step

Pulmonology/cardiothoracic surgery referral; biopsy may be needed to exclude malignancy.

2. Lymphatic malformation (chest wall/mediastinum)

Benign

Congenital malformation of the lymphatic system, which can involve the chest wall, axilla, or mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiloculated, T2-hyperintense cystic mass that can infiltrate across fascial planes without respecting normal anatomic boundaries.

Differential
  • Lymphangioma/lymphatic malformation
  • Other cystic mediastinal lesion
Next Step

Surgical/IR referral for sclerotherapy or resection if symptomatic.

3. Post-radiation change, chest MRI

Benign

Expected fibrotic change within a prior radiation field.

Imaging Characteristics

Geographic area of signal change conforming to the radiation port, with fibrotic low T2 signal and volume loss, distinct from the irregular margins of tumor recurrence.

Differential
  • Expected radiation fibrosis
  • Tumor recurrence (discrete, enhancing, restricted diffusion)
Next Step

Serial imaging for stability; biopsy if a discrete new or growing lesion is identified within the field.

4. Chest wall hematoma, MRI

Variable

Blood collection within the chest wall soft tissues, from trauma, anticoagulation, or post-procedural bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal characteristics vary with blood product age; acute hematoma is often T1-isointense/T2-hyperintense, evolving to T1-hyperintense as it matures.

Differential
  • Traumatic hematoma
  • Anticoagulation-related
  • Post-procedural (post-biopsy, post-line placement)
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; correlate with anticoagulation status and trauma history.

5. Thoracic outlet syndrome, MRI

Variable

Compression of the brachial plexus and/or subclavian vessels at the thoracic outlet.

Imaging Characteristics

Positional narrowing of the space between the clavicle and first rib on provocative (arm-abduction) imaging, sometimes with an associated cervical rib or fibrous band.

Differential
  • Neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome (brachial plexus compression)
  • Vascular thoracic outlet syndrome (subclavian artery/vein compression)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/thoracic surgery referral for symptomatic cases; provocative positional imaging is key to diagnosis.

Pleural Effusion

6 findings

1. Simple pleural effusion, ultrasound

Variable

Free-flowing anechoic fluid in the pleural space, readily detected by point-of-care ultrasound with high sensitivity.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic space between the parietal and visceral pleura, changing shape with respiration; the 'spine sign' (visualization of the spine above the diaphragm, normally obscured by aerated lung) confirms effusion.

Differential
  • Transudative (heart failure, cirrhosis)
  • Exudative (parapneumonic, malignant)
Next Step

Thoracentesis with Light's criteria if new or of unclear etiology; ultrasound guidance improves procedural safety.

2. Complex/septated pleural effusion, ultrasound

Concerning

Pleural fluid with internal septations or debris, suggesting an exudative or infected process.

Imaging Characteristics

Fibrin strands, septations, or floating debris within the fluid collection, sometimes creating a loculated, multi-chambered appearance ('plankton sign' with swirling debris).

Differential
  • Empyema
  • Complicated parapneumonic effusion
  • Hemothorax
Next Step

Consider CT for full extent; drainage (often with fibrinolytics) for loculated infected collections.

3. Empyema, ultrasound

Concerning

Infected pleural fluid collection, often with characteristic sonographic features.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, echogenic fluid with septations and a thickened, sometimes irregular pleural surface; unlike a simple effusion, the pattern often does not change freely with respiration.

Differential
  • Empyema
  • Organized hemothorax
Next Step

Chest tube drainage (often with fibrinolytics) or surgical referral for organized empyema, plus antibiotics.

4. Hemothorax, ultrasound

Concerning

Blood within the pleural space, assessed via thoracic ultrasound in trauma (eFAST).

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic, often complex fluid within the pleural space, which may show swirling internal echoes distinguishing it from simple effusion.

Differential
  • Traumatic hemothorax
  • Anticoagulant-related
  • Malignant effusion with hemorrhage
Next Step

Chest tube drainage per trauma protocol; correlate with mechanism and hemodynamic status.

5. Pleural effusion volume estimation

Variable

Semi-quantitative sonographic assessment of effusion size to guide management.

Imaging Characteristics

Maximal interpleural distance measured at end-expiration in the posterior axillary line with the patient upright or semi-recumbent; various formulas estimate volume from this measurement.

Differential
  • Small effusion (may be managed conservatively)
  • Large effusion (more likely to warrant drainage)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms and underlying cause to guide need for thoracentesis.

6. Malignant pleural effusion, ultrasound features

Concerning

Pleural fluid with sonographic features suggesting malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Pleural nodularity/thickening (>1 cm), diaphragmatic thickening (>7 mm), or a visible pleural-based mass in association with the effusion.

Differential
  • Malignant pleural effusion/mesothelioma
  • Benign effusion with reactive pleural thickening
Next Step

Thoracentesis with cytology and consideration of pleural biopsy for tissue diagnosis.

Pneumothorax

6 findings

1. Absent lung sliding

Concerning

Loss of the normal to-and-fro motion of the visceral against parietal pleura, the primary sonographic sign of pneumothorax.

Imaging Characteristics

No sliding motion at the pleural line on real-time B-mode imaging; on M-mode, this produces a 'barcode' or 'stratosphere' sign instead of the normal 'seashore' sign.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax
  • Prior pleurodesis/adhesions
  • Mainstem intubation (absent sliding on the non-ventilated side)
  • Complete atelectasis
  • Apnea/breath-holding (transient absent sliding)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; absent sliding alone is sensitive but not fully specific for pneumothorax.

2. Lung point

Concerning

The specific transition point where sliding lung meets non-sliding (pneumothorax) lung, a highly specific sign for pneumothorax.

Imaging Characteristics

A discrete point on the chest wall where lung sliding is present on one side of the transducer position and absent on the other, corresponding to the edge of the pneumothorax.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax (lung point is highly specific when identified)
Next Step

Confirms pneumothorax; correlate size/clinical significance with the extent of the non-sliding area and patient stability.

3. Presence of lung sliding (pneumothorax excluded at that site)

Benign

Normal sonographic finding that reliably excludes pneumothorax at the scanned location.

Imaging Characteristics

Normal to-and-fro sliding motion of the pleural line with respiration, producing the normal 'seashore sign' on M-mode.

Differential
  • No pneumothorax at the scanned location (does not exclude pneumothorax elsewhere in the chest)
Next Step

No further action needed at that site; scan multiple zones if clinical suspicion remains for a different location.

4. B-lines present (pneumothorax excluded at that site)

Benign

Visualization of B-lines, which by definition arise from the visceral pleura and therefore exclude pneumothorax at that specific location.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertical, laser-like hyperechoic artifacts extending from the pleural line to the edge of the screen, moving synchronously with lung sliding.

Differential
  • Normal finding excluding pneumothorax at that site
Next Step

No further action needed regarding pneumothorax at that location.

5. Subcutaneous emphysema, ultrasound limitation

Variable

Air within the subcutaneous tissue that obscures deeper ultrasound assessment of the pleura.

Imaging Characteristics

Dense, dirty shadowing artifact obscuring visualization of the pleural line and deeper structures.

Differential
  • Subcutaneous emphysema from pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum tracking to the chest wall
  • Post-procedural air
Next Step

Recognize as a technical limitation; use an alternative window or CT if pneumothorax assessment is needed and the view is obscured.

6. Skin/rib artifact mimicking absent sliding

Benign

Technical pitfall where poor probe technique or rib shadowing can mimic absent lung sliding.

Imaging Characteristics

Inconsistent or absent sliding due to improper probe angulation, excessive pressure, or scanning directly over a rib rather than an intercostal space.

Differential
  • Technical artifact (not true pneumothorax)
Next Step

Re-scan with proper technique (perpendicular probe orientation within an intercostal space) before concluding pneumothorax is present.

Consolidation / Pneumonia

6 findings

1. Hepatization pattern (tissue-like consolidation)

Concerning

Sonographic appearance of consolidated lung resembling the echotexture of liver tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Consolidated lung loses its normal aerated appearance and takes on a homogeneous, tissue-like echotexture similar to the liver, often with visible air bronchograms within it.

Differential
  • Pneumonia (most common cause)
  • Atelectasis (compressive or obstructive)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical picture; dynamic air bronchograms favor pneumonia over atelectasis.

2. Dynamic air bronchograms

Concerning

Air bronchograms that move with respiration within consolidated lung, helping distinguish pneumonia from atelectasis.

Imaging Characteristics

Punctate or linear hyperechoic foci (air) within consolidated lung tissue that move outward during inspiration.

Differential
  • Pneumonia (dynamic air bronchograms favor this diagnosis)
  • Resolving/aerating consolidation
Next Step

Supports a diagnosis of pneumonia over obstructive atelectasis (which typically shows static, non-moving air bronchograms).

3. Static air bronchograms

Variable

Air bronchograms that do not move with respiration, suggesting obstructive atelectasis rather than pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Punctate or linear hyperechoic foci within consolidated lung that remain fixed in position across the respiratory cycle.

Differential
  • Obstructive atelectasis (central endobronchial obstruction, mucus plug)
  • Resorptive/complete atelectasis
Next Step

Consider bronchoscopy or CT to identify a central obstructing lesion if the pattern persists.

4. Shred sign

Concerning

Irregular, ragged border between consolidated and aerated lung, seen in non-translobar pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, jagged interface between hepatized (consolidated) lung and the normally aerated lung deep to it.

Differential
  • Pneumonia (partial/non-translobar consolidation)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical picture; supports a pneumonic rather than fully atelectatic process.

5. Pleural line abnormality overlying pneumonia

Concerning

Irregular or thickened pleural line associated with underlying consolidation.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, thickened, or fragmented pleural line overlying an area of consolidation or B-lines.

Differential
  • Pneumonia
  • Pulmonary infarct
  • Pleural-based malignancy
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context and consider additional imaging if the diagnosis remains unclear.

6. Pediatric consolidation, subpleural

Concerning

Small, subpleural consolidation pattern often seen in pediatric pneumonia.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, wedge-shaped, hypoechoic subpleural consolidation, sometimes with adjacent B-lines and a small parapneumonic effusion.

Differential
  • Pediatric bacterial or viral pneumonia
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam given lung ultrasound's high sensitivity for pediatric pneumonia; supportive care/antibiotics as indicated.

Interstitial Syndrome (B-lines)

5 findings

1. Focal B-lines (few, localized)

Variable

A small number of B-lines in a localized area, which can be a normal finding or reflect focal pathology.

Imaging Characteristics

Fewer than 3 B-lines per rib interspace in a limited number of zones.

Differential
  • Normal finding (a few B-lines can be normal, especially at the lung bases)
  • Focal interstitial process (early localized edema, contusion, focal pneumonia)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; focal findings are less specific than a diffuse bilateral pattern.

2. Diffuse bilateral B-lines (interstitial syndrome)

Concerning

Numerous B-lines across multiple bilateral lung zones, a sonographic pattern of interstitial syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Three or more B-lines per intercostal space across multiple bilateral zones, sometimes coalescing into 'white lung' with severe involvement.

Differential
  • Cardiogenic pulmonary edema
  • ARDS
  • Diffuse interstitial pneumonia (viral, atypical)
  • Pulmonary fibrosis (chronic, often with irregular pleural line)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical volume status and acuity; echocardiography if cardiogenic edema suspected.

3. B-lines with irregular pleural line (chronic pattern)

Variable

Combination suggesting chronic interstitial lung disease rather than acute pulmonary edema.

Imaging Characteristics

B-lines associated with a thickened, irregular, and fragmented pleural line, rather than the smooth pleural line typically seen in acute cardiogenic edema.

Differential
  • Pulmonary fibrosis/chronic interstitial lung disease
  • Chronic changes from prior ARDS
Next Step

Correlate with known chronic lung disease history; CT chest for further characterization if new.

4. Confluent B-lines / 'white lung'

Concerning

Severe, coalescent B-lines obscuring the normal pleural line pattern, reflecting severe interstitial/alveolar involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

B-lines so numerous and coalescent that individual lines can no longer be distinguished, creating a nearly solid white appearance on the screen.

Differential
  • Severe cardiogenic pulmonary edema
  • Severe ARDS
  • Severe diffuse pneumonia
Next Step

Correlate with respiratory status and hemodynamics; escalate respiratory support as clinically indicated.

5. Asymmetric/unilateral B-lines

Variable

B-lines confined to one lung or region, favoring a focal rather than diffuse cardiogenic process.

Imaging Characteristics

B-line pattern present on one side or in one region while the contralateral/other lung zones remain relatively clear (A-line predominant).

Differential
  • Unilateral pneumonia/contusion
  • Asymmetric cardiogenic edema (less typical, but can occur)
  • Aspiration (dependent distribution)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; asymmetric findings warrant consideration of a focal process rather than global cardiogenic edema.

Diaphragm

5 findings

1. Diaphragmatic excursion, normal

Benign

Normal diaphragmatic movement assessed by M-mode ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, adequate caudal displacement of the diaphragm during inspiration, typically 1-2 cm during quiet breathing and greater with deep inspiration.

Differential
  • Normal diaphragmatic function
Next Step

No action needed.

2. Diaphragmatic paralysis, ultrasound

Concerning

Absent or paradoxical diaphragmatic movement, suggesting phrenic nerve dysfunction.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent or paradoxical (upward rather than downward) diaphragmatic movement during inspiration on the affected side, best demonstrated with a sniff test.

Differential
  • Phrenic nerve palsy (post-surgical, malignant infiltration, idiopathic)
  • Severe diaphragmatic weakness from neuromuscular disease
Next Step

Correlate with respiratory symptoms and surgical/malignancy history; pulmonology referral for further workup.

3. Reduced diaphragmatic excursion (weakness)

Variable

Diminished but not absent diaphragmatic movement.

Imaging Characteristics

Diaphragmatic excursion below expected normal range, without frank paradoxical motion.

Differential
  • Diaphragmatic weakness (neuromuscular disease, critical illness myopathy)
  • Ventilator-induced diaphragmatic dysfunction (in mechanically ventilated patients)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical weaning parameters in ventilated patients; pulmonology referral for further characterization.

4. Diaphragmatic thickening fraction, reduced

Variable

Reduced diaphragmatic muscle thickening during inspiration, a marker used in ventilator weaning assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening fraction (percentage increase in diaphragm thickness from end-expiration to end-inspiration) below the threshold associated with successful extubation (commonly cited threshold around 30%, though institutional protocols vary).

Differential
  • Diaphragmatic weakness/atrophy from prolonged mechanical ventilation
  • Critical illness myopathy
Next Step

Correlate with overall weaning readiness assessment; used as an adjunct to traditional weaning parameters.

5. Subphrenic fluid collection, ultrasound

Concerning

Fluid collection beneath the diaphragm, assessed via a thoracic or upper abdominal window.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or complex fluid collection beneath the diaphragm, distinguished from a pleural effusion by its relationship to the diaphragmatic contour.

Differential
  • Subphrenic abscess
  • Ascites
  • Hemoperitoneum
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; CT for full characterization and IR drainage if abscess confirmed.

Chest Wall & Soft Tissue

4 findings

1. Rib fracture, ultrasound

Variable

Fracture of a rib identified by point-of-care ultrasound, which can be more sensitive than plain film for anterior/cartilaginous fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical step-off or discontinuity of the rib's hyperechoic surface, sometimes with an associated hypoechoic hematoma.

Differential
  • Traumatic rib fracture (including costochondral junction injuries not well seen on plain film)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam; assess for associated pneumothorax/hemothorax with lung ultrasound.

2. Chest wall hematoma/abscess, ultrasound

Variable

Fluid collection within the chest wall soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Hematoma: complex, often heterogeneous collection without significant internal vascularity. Abscess: complex fluid collection, sometimes with internal debris/gas and surrounding hyperemia.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic or post-procedural hematoma
  • Chest wall abscess (infectious)
Next Step

Drainage for a significant abscess; conservative management for a stable hematoma.

3. Chest wall soft-tissue mass, ultrasound

Variable

Mass identified within the chest wall soft tissues on ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid or cystic mass distinct from underlying pleura/lung, with characteristics (echogenicity, vascularity, margins) suggesting the tissue of origin.

Differential
  • Lipoma (benign, homogeneous)
  • Soft-tissue sarcoma (heterogeneous, vascular)
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Further imaging (CT/MRI) and biopsy for indeterminate or concerning masses.

4. Necrotizing soft-tissue infection, chest wall

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection of the chest wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened subcutaneous tissue with fluid tracking along fascial planes and, in advanced cases, subcutaneous gas causing dirty shadowing.

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
  • Simple cellulitis/abscess (less extensive fascial involvement)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation for debridement; do not delay for further imaging if clinically suspected.

Pediatric-Specific

4 findings

1. Pediatric pneumonia, lung ultrasound

Concerning

Consolidation pattern in children, for whom lung ultrasound has particularly high diagnostic accuracy given better acoustic windows.

Imaging Characteristics

Subpleural consolidation with dynamic air bronchograms and often an adjacent small effusion; lung ultrasound has high sensitivity and specificity for pediatric pneumonia.

Differential
  • Bacterial or viral pneumonia
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam; lung ultrasound can reduce reliance on chest X-ray in appropriate pediatric settings.

2. Pediatric pneumothorax, ultrasound

Concerning

Pneumothorax assessment in children, including in the neonatal ICU setting.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent lung sliding with a lung point identifying the transition, using the same principles as in adults.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax (spontaneous, traumatic, or iatrogenic in ventilated neonates)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical status; decompression per size and hemodynamic significance.

3. Transient tachypnea of the newborn, lung ultrasound

Benign

Self-limited neonatal respiratory condition from delayed clearance of fetal lung fluid.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse B-lines with a characteristic 'double lung point' (differing B-line density between upper and lower lung fields) and a generally normal pleural line.

Differential
  • Transient tachypnea of the newborn (self-limited)
  • Neonatal pneumonia (less symmetric, may show consolidation)
  • Respiratory distress syndrome (more severe, uniform findings)
Next Step

Supportive care; typically resolves within 24-72 hours.

4. Respiratory distress syndrome (surfactant deficiency), lung ultrasound

Concerning

Neonatal respiratory condition from surfactant deficiency, most common in preterm infants.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, dense B-lines/white lung pattern with an abnormal, often thickened and irregular pleural line, and reduced or absent lung sliding in severe cases.

Differential
  • Respiratory distress syndrome (surfactant deficiency)
  • Severe transient tachypnea of the newborn
  • Neonatal pneumonia
Next Step

NICU management; correlate with gestational age and clinical respiratory status.

Miscellaneous POCUS Signs

4 findings

1. Curtain sign

Benign

Normal aerated lung sliding into view and obscuring a deeper structure (such as the liver or a subdiaphragmatic collection) during inspiration.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperechoic, moving aerated lung margin that periodically slides over and obscures a deeper abdominal/thoracic structure with each breath.

Differential
  • Normal finding reflecting normal lung aeration at the lung base
Next Step

Recognize as a normal artifact; if it obscures a needed view, adjust probe position or timing relative to respiration.

2. Lung pulse

Variable

Subtle rhythmic movement of the pleural line synchronized with the cardiac impulse rather than respiration, seen when there is no lung sliding but the lung remains in contact with the chest wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, rhythmic pulsations of the pleural line at the cardiac rate in the absence of true lung sliding.

Differential
  • Complete atelectasis/mainstem intubation (lung remains apposed to chest wall, transmitting cardiac pulsation)
  • Excludes pneumothorax at that specific site (since pneumothorax would eliminate the lung pulse by separating the pleural layers)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; presence of a lung pulse argues against pneumothorax at that location.

3. A-lines (normal aerated lung pattern)

Benign

Horizontal reverberation artifacts indicating normally aerated lung.

Imaging Characteristics

Horizontal, equally spaced hyperechoic lines parallel to and deep to the pleural line, representing reverberation artifact from normally aerated lung.

Differential
  • Normal aerated lung
Next Step

No action needed; a predominantly A-line pattern with normal lung sliding argues against significant pulmonary pathology at that site.

4. Pleural effusion with atelectatic lung ('jellyfish sign')

Variable

Collapsed, floating lung tissue within a pleural effusion, resembling a jellyfish moving within the surrounding fluid.

Imaging Characteristics

Mobile, undulating lung tissue floating within anechoic pleural fluid, moving with respiration and cardiac pulsation.

Differential
  • Large pleural effusion with passive compressive atelectasis of the adjacent lung
Next Step

Correlate with the underlying cause of the effusion; expect re-expansion of the lung once the effusion is drained if no underlying obstruction is present.

01 / Arterial Disease

PAD & Chronic Limb-Threatening Ischemia

A deeper look than Vol. 1a's introduction — built to the 2024 ACC/AHA/Multisociety Lower Extremity PAD Guideline, the first major update to reframe PAD around four distinct clinical subsets.

Four Clinical Subsets & the WIfI Classification

Each subset has a different natural history and management priority — the guideline explicitly warns against treating "asymptomatic" as synonymous with "low risk," since MACE risk remains elevated even without leg symptoms.
Why WIfI matters

The WIfI classification (Wound, Ischemia, foot Infection) gives CLTI a shared language across vascular surgery, podiatry, and wound care — much like SCAI staging does for cardiogenic shock (Vol. 5). A composite WIfI stage predicts 1-year amputation risk and directly informs how urgently revascularization should proceed relative to wound/infection management.

Medical Therapy — What's New in 2024

The established pillars

Smoking cessation, high-intensity statin therapy, blood pressure control, and supervised exercise therapy remain the foundation — supervised exercise in particular has outcomes for claudication comparable to revascularization in appropriately selected patients.

Low-dose rivaroxaban + aspirin

Building on COMPASS and VOYAGER PAD trial data, low-dose rivaroxaban (2.5mg twice daily) added to low-dose aspirin is now a reasonable option to reduce major adverse cardiovascular and limb events in symptomatic PAD — a genuinely new addition to the standard medical regimen beyond the traditional pillars.

Multidisciplinary care is now explicitly recommended

The 2024 guideline formally recommends a multispecialty team — vascular medicine, vascular surgery/interventionalists, podiatry, and wound care working together — particularly for CLTI, reflecting evidence that siloed, single-specialty care produces worse limb salvage outcomes than coordinated team-based management.

Revascularization Threshold by Subset

Asymptomatic PAD generally does not warrant revascularization — risk factor modification is the priority. Claudication warrants revascularization only after an adequate trial of supervised exercise and medical therapy fails to provide acceptable quality of life, since the procedural risk must be weighed against a non-limb-threatening baseline. CLTI is the clearest revascularization indication — the goal shifts from symptom improvement to limb salvage, and delay directly costs tissue.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Vascular surgery or interventional vascular medicine for any confirmed CLTI — the WIfI stage should be communicated directly, since it drives urgency triage on the receiving end.
Urgent (days, not weeks) referral
New rest pain or non-healing ulceration in a patient with known PAD — this is the transition point from chronic symptomatic disease to CLTI and changes the entire management timeline.
Transfer if no vascular surgery/endovascular capability on-site
Any CLTI patient at a facility without revascularization capability — arrange transfer proactively rather than waiting for wound deterioration or infection to force the issue.
Transport considerations
Protect the affected limb from further trauma or pressure during transport; standard transport is usually adequate for stable CLTI without active infection, but escalate to more urgent transport if signs of limb-threatening infection or acute deterioration develop.
Who to call
Vascular surgery for surgical revascularization candidacy; interventional radiology/cardiology for endovascular-first approaches depending on local practice patterns; podiatry and wound care in parallel for any CLTI with tissue loss.

Acute Limb Ischemia

A limb-viability clock measured in hours — the Rutherford classification exists specifically to communicate urgency in a single shared vocabulary.

Rutherford Staging

Stage IIb ("immediately threatened") is the critical inflection point — this is salvageable, but only with immediate revascularization, not scheduled or next-day intervention.
Paresthesia and paralysis are late, ominous findings

Pain, pallor, and pulselessness can be present in viable or marginally threatened limbs — but new paresthesia (sensory loss) or paralysis (motor loss) signal nerve and muscle ischemia severe enough to indicate Stage IIb or worse. Don't wait for these late findings to escalate; a limb without sensory or motor deficit but with an absent pulse and rest pain already warrants urgent evaluation.

Expandable: Time-Critical Decision Points

Step 1 — Determine viability stage immediately on presentation

A focused vascular exam (pulses, Doppler signals both arterial and venous, sensory and motor exam) takes minutes and directly determines the entire subsequent pathway — this should happen before, not after, imaging is arranged.

Step 2 — Stage III (irreversible)? Consider primary amputation

Profound sensory and motor loss with no Doppler signal at all indicates tissue that cannot be salvaged — attempting revascularization of an irreversibly ischemic limb risks reperfusion injury (severe hyperkalemia, myoglobinuria, compartment syndrome, systemic acidosis) without benefit. This is a difficult but necessary determination made jointly with vascular surgery.

Step 3 — Stage I or IIa? Imaging can proceed before intervention

A viable or marginally threatened limb tolerates the time needed for CT angiography or formal angiography to define the anatomy and plan the specific revascularization approach (endovascular thrombectomy/thrombolysis vs. open surgical embolectomy vs. bypass).

Step 4 — Stage IIb? Go straight to the OR/angio suite

Immediately threatened limbs do not have time for extensive imaging workup — anticoagulate immediately (heparin, to prevent thrombus propagation) and proceed directly to revascularization, with imaging obtained on the way to or during the procedure rather than delaying intervention for a formal study beforehand.

Anticoagulate Immediately, Regardless of Stage

Systemic heparin should be started as soon as ALI is suspected, before the etiology (embolic vs. thrombotic) or exact revascularization plan is determined — this prevents proximal and distal thrombus propagation and buys time for definitive treatment planning. This is one of the few interventions in this diagnosis that should never be delayed pending further workup.

Watch for reperfusion complications after revascularization

Once flow is restored, monitor closely for compartment syndrome (may require prophylactic or therapeutic fasciotomy), hyperkalemia, myoglobinuria/rhabdomyolysis, and systemic acidosis from washout of ischemic metabolic byproducts — these can be as life-threatening as the ischemia itself, particularly after prolonged ischemia time before revascularization.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Vascular surgery emergently for any suspected ALI regardless of stage — even Stage I/IIa needs prompt surgical involvement given how quickly staging can change.
ICU admission
Post-revascularization for any patient with significant ischemia time before treatment, given reperfusion injury risk; any patient with hemodynamic instability from the underlying embolic source (e.g., new AFib with RVR) or from reperfusion complications.
Transfer if no vascular surgery on-site
Emergent transfer for any Stage IIb or higher — this is one of the true time-critical vascular emergencies, and pre-arrival notification to the receiving vascular surgery team is essential.
Transport considerations
Continue heparin infusion uninterrupted throughout transport; keep the limb protected and in a dependent (not elevated) position to maximize perfusion; a critical care or specialized transport team for any patient with reperfusion complications already evident.
Who to call
Vascular surgery directly and emergently; cardiology in parallel if an embolic source needs urgent evaluation (echo for thrombus, rhythm management for AFib).
03 / Arterial Disease

Aortic Aneurysm & Dissection

Vol. 1a covered the core pathophysiology and rupture-risk-by-diameter framework — this section goes deeper into the thoracic/abdominal distinction and the genetic syndromes that change the rules entirely.

Thoracic vs. Abdominal, and the Heritable Syndromes

Location and etiology change both the screening strategy and the repair threshold — a young patient with a dilated aortic root needs a fundamentally different workup and follow-up plan than an older patient with a typical infrarenal AAA.
Vascular Ehlers-Danlos is the one that breaks the "watch and wait" model

Unlike Marfan or Loeys-Dietz, where surveillance imaging and a defined diameter threshold guide timing, vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome carries meaningful spontaneous arterial rupture and dissection risk even at normal or near-normal diameters. A young patient with a strong family history of unexplained arterial rupture or dissection, easy bruising, and characteristic thin, translucent skin deserves genetics referral regardless of what imaging shows at a single point in time.

When to Suspect a Genetic Syndrome

Clinical red flags

Aortic aneurysm/dissection at a young age (generally <50), a family history of the same, disproportionate tall stature or skeletal features (arachnodactyly, pectus deformity), or a combination of aortic disease with other systemic features (lens dislocation, joint hypermobility, distinctive facial features) should all prompt consideration of a heritable etiology.

Why it matters practically

Confirming a genetic syndrome changes the repair threshold (often smaller than standard TAA thresholds), triggers family cascade screening (first-degree relatives should be screened even if asymptomatic), and often changes activity recommendations (avoiding isometric/high-intensity exercise, contact sports) given the different risk profile of the underlying connective tissue defect.

Surveillance Imaging Cadence

Once identified, aneurysms are followed with serial imaging (echocardiography for the aortic root, CT or MRI for more distal segments) at intervals determined by size and growth rate — smaller, stable aneurysms are typically followed annually, while larger or rapidly growing aneurysms (growth >0.5cm/year is itself a red flag regardless of absolute size) warrant closer interval follow-up and earlier surgical discussion.

Bicuspid aortic valve and aortopathy

Bicuspid aortic valve is associated with its own aortopathy independent of Marfan/Loeys-Dietz/vEDS — aortic dilation in this population follows its own size-index-based thresholds (indexed to body surface area) and deserves dedicated aortic imaging surveillance alongside valve function monitoring, not just an incidental note on an echo report.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiothoracic surgery (for thoracic) or vascular surgery (for abdominal) upon reaching the size threshold for repair, or sooner if rapid growth or symptoms develop; genetics referral for any suspected heritable syndrome.
Emergent evaluation
Any new chest, back, or abdominal pain in a patient with a known aneurysm — this could represent expansion, impending rupture, or dissection and warrants immediate imaging, not routine follow-up scheduling.
Transfer if no cardiothoracic/vascular surgery on-site
Any aneurysm approaching or exceeding the repair threshold, or any acute symptoms in a patient with a known aneurysm — err toward earlier transfer given the catastrophic consequences of rupture.
Transport considerations
Blood pressure control during transport for any symptomatic aneurysm (treat as potential impending rupture/dissection until proven otherwise); avoid unnecessary Valsalva or straining.
Who to call
Cardiothoracic surgery for thoracic aortic disease; vascular surgery for abdominal aortic disease; medical genetics for suspected heritable connective tissue disease, ideally involved before major family-planning or activity decisions are made.

Vasculitis Spectrum

Organizing vasculitis by vessel size — large, medium, and small — turns a sprawling list of eponymous syndromes into a manageable framework, built to the 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria.

Large, Medium, and Small Vessel Disease

Vessel size predicts both typical presentation (large vessel disease produces claudication-like and pulse-deficit findings; small vessel disease produces palpable purpura and glomerulonephritis) and the urgency of specific complications.
Giant cell arteritis is a true ophthalmologic emergency

Visual loss from GCA can become permanent within hours — if clinical suspicion is reasonable (age >50, new headache, jaw claudication, elevated inflammatory markers, especially with any visual symptoms), start high-dose glucocorticoids immediately. Do not wait for temporal artery biopsy confirmation, which can be performed within 1-2 weeks of steroid initiation without significantly compromising diagnostic yield — but visual loss waits for no one.

GCA vs. Takayasu — Overlapping But Distinct

Both are large-vessel vasculitides, and the 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria were specifically developed and validated to help distinguish them — age remains a key discriminator (Takayasu classically under 40, GCA classically over 50) but a genuine overlap zone exists in the 40-60 range, where imaging findings, presence of polymyalgia rheumatica features, and specific branch vessel involvement pattern help resolve the distinction.

Buerger's Disease — The One Where the Treatment Is Behavioral

Thromboangiitis obliterans occurs almost exclusively in tobacco users (including smokeless tobacco and possibly cannabis) and produces distal extremity ischemia in young patients without typical atherosclerotic risk factors. Complete and total tobacco cessation — not partial reduction — is the only intervention that reliably halts progression; continued use, even in small amounts, is associated with ongoing disease activity and progression to amputation.

Treatment has shifted toward biologics and rituximab

Glucocorticoids remain first-line induction therapy across large-vessel vasculitis, but tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor blockade) is now a recognized steroid-sparing option specifically for GCA. For ANCA-associated vasculitis, rituximab has increasingly displaced cyclophosphamide as induction therapy in many cases, given comparable efficacy with a more favorable long-term toxicity profile (particularly regarding infertility and secondary malignancy risk with cyclophosphamide).

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Rheumatology for any suspected vasculitis, ideally same-day for large-vessel disease with visual or neurologic symptoms; ophthalmology emergently for any visual symptoms in suspected GCA.
Start treatment before confirmation
High-dose glucocorticoids for suspected GCA with visual symptoms should begin immediately, in parallel with — not after — arranging biopsy or imaging confirmation.
ICU admission
Any vasculitis with severe multi-organ involvement (pulmonary hemorrhage in ANCA-associated disease, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis with acute kidney injury, mesenteric involvement in PAN).
Transfer if no rheumatology on-site
Any complex vasculitis requiring immunosuppressive induction therapy decision-making, or any diagnostic uncertainty between overlapping syndromes (GCA vs. Takayasu, or distinguishing ANCA-associated subtypes).
Who to call
Rheumatology as the primary specialty for essentially all vasculitis management; ophthalmology emergently for GCA with visual symptoms; nephrology for renal involvement in any of the ANCA-associated or PAN spectrum.

Renovascular Disease

Two mechanisms producing the same hypertensive phenotype through completely different disease processes — and a management approach that diverges sharply between them.

Atherosclerotic RAS vs. Fibromuscular Dysplasia

Age and sex distribution alone should raise or lower suspicion for each — a young woman with new severe hypertension and no other risk factors points strongly toward FMD, while an older patient with diffuse atherosclerosis points toward the atherosclerotic mechanism.
Stenting for atherosclerotic RAS is not routinely beneficial

Multiple randomized trials (including CORAL) have shown that renal artery stenting does not improve blood pressure or renal outcomes compared to optimal medical therapy alone for most patients with atherosclerotic RAS — this substantially narrowed the indications for intervention compared to older practice patterns. Reserve stenting for specific scenarios: recurrent flash pulmonary edema, refractory hypertension despite maximal medical therapy, or progressive renal function decline attributable to the stenosis.

The AKI-After-ACEi/ARB Pattern, Explained

In a kidney with significant arterial stenosis, angiotensin II-mediated efferent arteriolar constriction is a critical compensatory mechanism maintaining glomerular filtration pressure despite reduced renal blood flow. ACE inhibitors and ARBs remove this compensation — in bilateral RAS (or unilateral stenosis to a solitary functioning kidney), this can precipitate acute kidney injury shortly after starting or increasing the dose of one of these agents. A significant creatinine rise (generally >30%) after ACEi/ARB initiation should prompt renal artery imaging workup rather than simply discontinuing the drug and moving on.

FMD Beyond the Renal Arteries

Multi-vessel disease is common

FMD can affect the carotid and vertebral arteries (presenting with headache, pulsatile tinnitus, dissection, or stroke), and is associated with an increased prevalence of intracranial aneurysms — a patient diagnosed with renal FMD reasonably warrants screening imaging of the cervicocranial vasculature.

Association with SCAD

Fibromuscular dysplasia has a recognized association with spontaneous coronary artery dissection (see Vol. 6) — the underlying arteriopathy is thought to be a shared predisposing factor, and a patient with one of these diagnoses reasonably warrants consideration of screening for the other.

Duplex ultrasound as first-line imaging

Renal duplex ultrasound is a reasonable first-line, non-invasive test for either etiology — operator-dependent but avoids contrast and radiation exposure. CT or MR angiography provides more definitive anatomic detail when duplex is equivocal or when intervention is being planned.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Nephrology or vascular medicine for any confirmed or strongly suspected renovascular disease, particularly when AKI, refractory hypertension, or recurrent flash pulmonary edema are present.
Urgent evaluation
New severe hypertension in a young patient (especially female) without other risk factors — this warrants reasonably prompt FMD workup rather than routine outpatient follow-up timelines.
Transfer if no interventional nephrology/vascular capability on-site
Any patient meeting criteria for intervention (recurrent flash pulmonary edema, refractory hypertension, progressive renal decline) needs access to renal artery intervention capability.
Transport considerations
Standard transport is generally adequate; ensure any recent ACEi/ARB dosing changes are clearly documented if AKI prompted the transfer, since this affects the receiving team's diagnostic approach.
Who to call
Nephrology for the renal function and hypertension management angle; vascular medicine or interventional radiology for imaging and potential intervention planning.

Mesenteric Ischemia

"Pain out of proportion to exam" is the single most important phrase in this section — recognizing acute mesenteric ischemia before peritoneal signs develop is the whole game.

Three Mechanisms of Acute Disease

Embolic disease strikes suddenly in a patient with an identifiable cardiac source; thrombotic disease evolves on a background of chronic mesenteric atherosclerosis; NOMI occurs without any fixed occlusion at all, purely from low-flow vasoconstriction.
Don't wait for peritoneal signs

Severe abdominal pain with a benign-appearing, soft, minimally tender abdomen is the classic early presentation — and it's precisely the presentation most likely to be dismissed or worked up slowly. By the time peritoneal signs (rebound, guarding, rigidity) develop, transmural bowel infarction has typically already occurred. Maintain a low threshold for CT angiography in any patient with severe, acute abdominal pain disproportionate to exam findings, particularly with risk factors (AFib, recent MI, atherosclerotic disease, low-flow state).

NOMI — The One That's Easy to Miss in the ICU

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia occurs in critically ill patients already receiving vasopressors, in cardiogenic or septic shock, or recently post-cardiac surgery — settings where abdominal pain is easily attributed to the primary illness, sedation may mask symptoms, and the patient often cannot reliably report pain at all. An unexplained rise in lactate, new metabolic acidosis, or feeding intolerance in a hemodynamically tenuous ICU patient should prompt consideration of NOMI even without classic abdominal findings.

Diagnostic Approach

CT angiography

First-line imaging for suspected acute mesenteric ischemia — can identify the occlusion site and distinguish embolic/thrombotic disease from NOMI's diffuse vasoconstriction pattern, while also assessing for bowel wall changes suggesting infarction.

Lactate is a marker, not a rule-out

Elevated lactate supports the diagnosis but a normal lactate does NOT exclude early mesenteric ischemia — lactate rises only once significant tissue ischemia/infarction has occurred, and clinical suspicion should drive imaging regardless of an initially normal level.

Chronic Mesenteric Ischemia

The "intestinal angina" pattern — postprandial pain 15-30 minutes after eating, resolving over 1-2 hours as metabolic demand falls — develops food fear and progressive weight loss as patients unconsciously restrict intake to avoid triggering pain. Given the extensive collateral circulation between the celiac, superior mesenteric, and inferior mesenteric arteries, symptomatic disease typically requires significant stenosis of at least two of the three vessels; single-vessel disease is usually well compensated and asymptomatic.

Chronic disease can acutely decompensate

A patient with known chronic mesenteric ischemia (or unrecognized chronic disease) can develop acute-on-chronic thrombotic occlusion — a sudden worsening of previously stable postprandial pain, or a new pattern of continuous rather than postprandial pain, should prompt urgent reevaluation rather than reassurance that "this is just their usual pain."

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Vascular surgery and general surgery jointly for any suspected acute mesenteric ischemia — both revascularization and potential bowel resection capability need to be immediately available.
Emergent surgical evaluation
Any peritoneal signs, hemodynamic instability, or CT evidence of bowel infarction/pneumatosis — these findings indicate a surgical abdomen requiring emergent exploration, not just revascularization alone.
Transfer if no vascular/GI surgery capability on-site
Any suspected acute mesenteric ischemia at a facility without emergent revascularization and bowel resection capability — this is one of the highest-mortality surgical emergencies and delay directly costs bowel length and patient survival.
Transport considerations
Aggressive fluid resuscitation en route given third-spacing and potential impending shock; avoid vasopressors if possible given the risk of worsening mesenteric vasoconstriction (particularly relevant if NOMI is a consideration), using fluids preferentially to support perfusion.
Who to call
Vascular surgery for revascularization; general/GI surgery in parallel given the substantial likelihood of needing bowel resection; interventional radiology as an alternative or adjunct for endovascular revascularization approaches.
07 / Arterial Disease

Carotid Disease & Raynaud's

Two very different entities sharing this final section — large-vessel stroke prevention and small-vessel vasospasm — but each with a clear decision framework worth knowing cold.

Carotid Stenosis Management Thresholds

Top: symptomatic status changes both the intervention threshold and the urgency — recent stroke/TIA meaningfully raises the benefit of intervention compared to the same degree of stenosis found incidentally. Bottom: primary vs. secondary Raynaud's phenomenon.
Optimal medical therapy has changed the asymptomatic calculus

Contemporary medical therapy (high-intensity statins, modern antiplatelet regimens, aggressive risk factor control) has substantially reduced the stroke rate in medically-managed asymptomatic carotid stenosis compared to older trial-era data — narrowing, though not eliminating, the benefit of intervention in this population. This makes asymptomatic carotid stenosis a genuinely shared decision-making conversation rather than an automatic referral for intervention once a threshold diameter is met.

Timing of Intervention After Symptomatic Event

For symptomatic patients meeting intervention criteria, earlier revascularization (within 2 weeks of the index TIA/stroke) captures the greatest risk reduction — the risk of recurrent stroke is highest in the days immediately following the initial event, and delaying intervention past this window meaningfully reduces the net benefit. This creates real urgency around symptomatic carotid disease workup and specialist referral, more than is often appreciated outside of stroke-focused practice.

CEA vs. CAS — Choosing the Approach

Favor CEA

Older patients (particularly >70), and anatomy unfavorable for stenting (extensive calcification, tortuous vessels, unfavorable aortic arch anatomy for catheter access).

Favor CAS

Younger patients, a "hostile neck" from prior surgery or radiation therapy that increases open surgical risk, and significant cardiac comorbidity that elevates general anesthesia/open surgical risk.

Raynaud's Phenomenon

The primary/secondary distinction is the entire clinical task here — primary Raynaud's is a benign, common condition requiring reassurance and conservative management (cold avoidance, layered clothing, smoking cessation), while secondary Raynaud's is a marker of underlying systemic disease requiring its own dedicated workup and monitoring.

Digital ulceration or asymmetric presentation changes everything

Tissue loss, asymmetric involvement, onset after age 30-40, and abnormal nailfold capillaroscopy findings are red flags for secondary Raynaud's that warrant rheumatologic referral and autoantibody testing (ANA with reflex to specific antibodies including anti-centromere and anti-Scl-70/topoisomerase for scleroderma spectrum disease) — these patients need monitoring for progression to a defined connective tissue disease, which can sometimes precede other diagnostic features by years.

Management escalation for severe/secondary disease

Calcium channel blockers (typically dihydropyridines like nifedipine) are first-line pharmacologic therapy when conservative measures are insufficient. For severe secondary disease with digital ulceration or critical ischemia, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, topical nitrates, or in severe cases IV prostacyclin analogs and even botulinum toxin injection or surgical sympathectomy have roles — this escalation pathway is generally managed jointly with rheumatology given the underlying systemic disease driving severity.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Vascular surgery/neurology (stroke service) for any symptomatic carotid stenosis meeting intervention criteria — timing matters, so this should not wait for routine outpatient referral; rheumatology for any suspected secondary Raynaud's.
Urgent evaluation
Recent TIA or minor stroke with carotid stenosis on imaging — the 2-week window for maximal intervention benefit makes this a time-sensitive referral, similar in spirit to other "don't delay" vascular presentations in this atlas.
Transfer if no vascular surgery/interventional neurology on-site
Symptomatic carotid stenosis requiring intervention at a facility without that capability; digital ischemia/ulceration from severe Raynaud's not responding to outpatient escalation.
Transport considerations
Standard neurologic/stroke transport precautions for symptomatic carotid disease (blood pressure parameters per the specific post-stroke/TIA protocol in use); protect ischemic digits from further cold exposure or trauma during any transport for severe Raynaud's complications.
Who to call
Vascular surgery and/or neurology (stroke) jointly for carotid intervention decision-making; rheumatology for secondary Raynaud's workup and escalating therapy.

What Changed · 2024

Stop treating every creatinine bump as a reason to stop the ACEi

KDIGO's first CKD guideline update since 2012 formalizes a threshold for when a rising creatinine on RAS-inhibitor therapy actually warrants stopping the drug — and it's higher than a lot of reflexive practice assumes. It also overturns blanket low-potassium dietary advice and hardens the case against combining ACE inhibitors with ARBs.

02 · Deep Dive · Myth-Busting

A real threshold for when to stop RAS inhibitors

ACEi/ARB continuation after creatinine rise Formalized threshold

An initial eGFR dip after starting a RAS inhibitor is expected physiology — vasodilation of the efferent arteriole lowers intraglomerular pressure. The guideline gives this a number: continue ACEi or ARB therapy unless serum creatinine rises by more than 30% within 4 weeks of starting or increasing the dose. Below that threshold, the expected hemodynamic effect is doing what it's supposed to do, not signaling harm.

Practical monitoring: check BP, serum creatinine, and serum potassium within 2–4 weeks of initiating or increasing a RASi dose. RASi should be dosed at the highest tolerated, approved dose, since the outcome benefits seen in trials were achieved at those doses — under-dosing to avoid a modest creatinine rise may be giving up real benefit for a change that doesn't meet the harm threshold.

03 · Deep Dive · Myth-Busting

Hyperkalemia on a RASi doesn't mean stop the RASi

Managing hyperkalemia while continuing RASi Reframed

The guideline's practice point is explicit: hyperkalemia associated with RASi use can often be managed by measures to reduce serum potassium — rather than by reflexively decreasing the RASi dose or stopping it altogether. This preserves the drug's kidney and cardiovascular protective effect rather than trading it away at the first elevated potassium.

⚡ The dietary advice changed too

Traditional "low-potassium diet" counseling told patients to avoid high-potassium foods broadly — including most fruits and vegetables. The updated guidance narrows the target: avoid highly processed foods specifically, while explicitly not restricting fruits and vegetables. Potassium absorption differs meaningfully by food source (plant-based vs. animal-based vs. processed), and blanket produce restriction was never well-supported by that distinction.

04 · Deep Dive

Dual RAS blockade: avoid it, full stop

The guideline recommends avoiding any combination of ACEi, ARB, and direct renin inhibitor therapy in people with CKD, with or without diabetes (Grade 1B). This rests on ONTARGET and VA NEPHRON-D trial data: dual RAS blockade lowers proteinuria but confers no long-term cardiovascular or kidney benefit, while meaningfully increasing the risk of hyperkalemia and AKI. The guideline explicitly places a higher value on avoiding that harm than on the proteinuria-lowering signal.

05 · Deep Dive

Newer agents move toward broader, earlier use

SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (e.g., finerenone) all get expanded roles across a broader range of CKD phenotypes than prior guidance reflected — including in patients without diabetes, as trial evidence for kidney and cardiovascular benefit has matured. GFR estimation itself was refined too: validated equations now incorporate cystatin C for specific populations, improving drug-dosing accuracy at the margins where creatinine-only estimates are least reliable.

✓ A focused update is already underway

KDIGO has launched a focused update specifically to Chapter 3 (delaying CKD progression and managing complications), given how quickly the evidence for these newer therapies is still evolving. Expect the specifics of this section to be revised again on a shorter cycle than the rest of the guideline — check for the update before finalizing institutional protocols built on this chapter.

Nephrotoxin stewardship also gets more explicit attention: NSAIDs, PPIs, herbal supplements, and certain antimicrobials are named as common avoidable contributors to kidney injury in CKD patients, warranting active risk/benefit review rather than routine continuation.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Don't stop an ACEi/ARB for a creatinine bump under 30% within the first 4 weeks — that's expected physiology, not a signal to intervene.
  • A hyperkalemic patient on a RASi doesn't automatically need the RASi stopped — treat the potassium first when reasonably possible.
  • Update your dietary counseling: the target is processed food, not fruits and vegetables.
  • Don't combine ACEi + ARB (or add a direct renin inhibitor to either) — this combination is recommended against, not just discouraged.
  • Reconsider routine NSAID and PPI use in CKD patients as part of nephrotoxin stewardship, not just at the extremes of renal function.

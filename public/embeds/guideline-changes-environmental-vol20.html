02 · Deep Dive

WBGT displaces heat index as the preferred metric

Environmental heat-risk assessment Metric upgraded

Wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) — which incorporates humidity, wind, sun angle, and cloud cover alongside air temperature — is now the preferred metric for evaluating environmental heat risk, when available. Heat index remains an acceptable second-line option when WBGT can't be measured, but it's explicitly framed as the fallback, not the equal alternative.

03 · Deep Dive

Ice-water immersion: the how, not just the what

Cold-water immersion protocol Expanded detail, 2024

The core recommendation is unchanged and unambiguous: ice-water immersion is the treatment for heat stroke, and if true ice-water immersion is logistically impossible, the coldest water available should be used instead. What's new in 2024 is operational — the guideline adds specific detail for implementing this in both prehospital/field settings and the hospital, addressing the practical gap between "cool the patient" as a principle and actually doing it under real conditions.

Rectal
Preferred site for core temperature monitoring during cooling
Continue during arrest
Cooling, cardiac monitoring, and defibrillation can all be performed safely during immersion

Cardiac arrest in exertional heat stroke is most often pulseless electrical activity driven by hyperkalemia and acidosis — a reminder that standard ACLS metabolic troubleshooting applies, and that cooling should continue rather than pause during resuscitation.

04 · Deep Dive

Oral first for mild cases, IV for heat stroke, with real numbers

Oral fluid replacement is first-line for mild-to-moderate heat illness. For heat stroke specifically, IV fluids are recommended over oral, reflecting the volume depletion that frequently accompanies exertional heat stroke:

Adults1–2 L isotonic (0.9% NaCl or LR)

05 · Practical Implementation

What real ED cold-water immersion looks like

⚡ Real-world protocol design, Jan 2025

A published ED implementation report describes practical logistics many departments haven't solved: standard cold-water tubs used in prehospital or sporting-event settings are often unfeasible in a hospital, so some EDs have adopted large ice-bucket-and-bag systems instead. On imaging timing, one described practice is to cool rapidly and remove the patient from the cooling bag before obtaining CT — rather than delaying cooling for imaging, or imaging mid-immersion.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Use WBGT for heat-risk assessment when it's available to you; fall back to heat index only when it isn't.
  • Get the patient into ice water (or the coldest water you can access) — don't wait for a "perfect" setup if a workable one is available now.
  • Measure core temperature rectally, not orally or with a temporal scanner, when managing heat stroke.
  • Continue cooling through cardiac arrest — cardiac monitoring and defibrillation are safe during immersion, and PEA from hyperkalemia/acidosis is the expected arrest rhythm.
  • Start heat stroke patients on IV fluids (1–2 L adults, 20 mL/kg pediatric) rather than oral rehydration.
  • Don't delay cooling to get a CT — cool first, image after removing the patient from the cooling setup.

02 · Myth-Busting

The fasting myth ACEP settled over a decade ago

Preprocedural fasting Level B, since 2014

ACEP's clinical policy is explicit: do not delay procedural sedation in adults or pediatric ED patients based on fasting time. Preprocedural fasting of any duration has not demonstrated a reduction in the risk of emesis or aspiration in the ED setting. A systematic review of 23 pediatric preoperative fasting trials (2,350 children) found essentially no aspiration signal tied to fasting status, and children allowed to drink were more comfortable with no difference in gastric volume or pH.

⚡ Why this still needs saying in 2026

This recommendation is over a decade old and has been reaffirmed without change as recently as January 2024 — and ED practice still frequently defaults to delaying sedation for a fasting window that the evidence never supported. If your department is still asking "how long since they ate" as a gating question before sedation, that practice is running against guidance that predates this atlas's entire 2024–2026 window by a decade.

For patients at genuinely elevated aspiration risk (OSA, obesity, age under 12 months), the guidance points toward ketamine specifically — it preserves airway reflexes, and no aspiration cases have been reported in the cited literature even in these higher-risk groups.

02 · Deep Dive

What's actually new: non-injectable ketamine delivery

Intranasal and nebulized ketamine Emerging evidence, 2025

A 2025 systematic review evaluated non-injectable (intranasal, nebulized) ketamine for pediatric ED sedation — a genuinely useful option for needle-averse children or when IV access is difficult to obtain before sedation is needed. This is evidence accumulation rather than a formal guideline update, but it's shaping real practice in departments adopting these routes.

03 · Deep Dive

Propofol is spreading into pediatric EDs — carefully

A 2025 quality-improvement study documented the structured process of safely implementing propofol procedural sedation in a pediatric ED — reflecting propofol's Level A safety designation now extending into pediatric practice patterns beyond the adult-predominant use it originally had. Ketamine remains Level A for children; propofol's addition to Level A safety evidence applies to both children and adults, and combination ketamine-propofol ("ketofol") sits at Level B for both age groups.

04 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Stop asking "when did they last eat" as a gate before sedation — it isn't guideline-supported and hasn't been for over a decade.
  • For higher aspiration-risk patients (OSA, obesity, infants), ketamine is the evidence-preferred agent specifically because it preserves airway reflexes.
  • Intranasal/nebulized ketamine is a reasonable option to know about for needle-averse pediatric patients — evidence is accumulating, if not yet a formal guideline recommendation.
  • Propofol use in pediatric EDs is expanding with structured safety protocols — this isn't an adults-only agent anymore in practice.
  • This is a "myth still alive" volume more than a "guideline changed" volume — the gap here is adoption, not evidence.

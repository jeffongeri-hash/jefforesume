Guideline Changes Atlas Sepsis · Vol. 2 — 2026 SSC Update
What Changed · Published March 2026

The 2026 SSC guidelines retired qSOFA and rewired the resuscitation algorithm

Five years after the 2021 edition, the Surviving Sepsis Campaign's fifth update — 129 statements, 46 of them new — replaces the sepsis screening tool most EDs have used since Sepsis-3, upgrades antimicrobial-stewardship practices to strong recommendations, and downgrades several hemodynamic recommendations from "recommend" to "suggest" as the evidence base matured into more nuanced, patient-specific guidance.

01 · Overview

At a glance

The 2026 update is built on GRADE methodology with a 69-person international panel, requiring ≥75% response and ≥80% agreement to finalize each statement. Carryover statements from 2021 were re-voted for continued relevance, not just reprinted.

⚡ Why this update exists

Sepsis-3 (2016) built the field's mental model around qSOFA. Since then, prospective screening-tool comparisons accumulated, beta-lactam pharmacokinetic data matured toward prolonged infusion, and hemodynamic RCTs (vasopressor selection, fluid strategy after initial resuscitation) forced several 2021 "recommend" statements down to "suggest" as real-world heterogeneity became clearer. The panel frames several downgrades explicitly as a maturing evidence base demanding more individualized care, not a reversal of prior guidance.

03 · Deep Dive

qSOFA is out as a standalone screen

Screening tool Strong recommendation

"For acutely ill patients in hospital, we recommend using NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS, or SIRS over qSOFA as a single tool to screen for sepsis."

This lands as one of the guideline's highest-certainty statements — the same tier as the MAP-65 target and beta-lactam infusion strategy. qSOFA was never intended by Sepsis-3 as a screening tool (it was designed to identify high-risk patients already suspected of infection), and its poor sensitivity for early screening has been a recurring critique. The 2026 panel formalizes that critique into guidance: pick a track-and-trigger score your institution already uses for early warning — NEWS2 is common in many US and UK systems — rather than relying on qSOFA at triage.

✓ Verify before use

This does not retire qSOFA entirely — it remains reasonable for rapid bedside risk-stratification once infection is already suspected. What changes is its role as a stand-alone institutional screening trigger. Confirm which track-and-trigger tool your ED's sepsis alert is actually built on.

04 · Deep Dive

Antimicrobial stewardship gets stronger backing

Prolonged beta-lactam infusion & de-escalation Upgraded to Recommend

Both statements moved from conditional ("suggest") to strong ("recommend") on the same underlying question, now with more mature evidence

For adults with sepsis or septic shock, the panel now recommends prolonged infusion of beta-lactams for maintenance dosing (after an initial loading bolus) over conventional bolus dosing, and recommends de-escalating antimicrobial therapy once a confirmed microbiological diagnosis and susceptibility profile is available. Both were "suggest" (conditional) in 2021.

⚡ Real-world timing context

The guideline retains its risk-stratified 1-hour (septic shock/probable sepsis) vs. 3-hour (possible sepsis) antimicrobial timing framework. A 2024–2025 multicenter deep-learning risk-stratification study (observational, not part of the guideline evidence base) found mortality benefit from 1-hour dosing concentrated in patients likely to have sepsis, with little signal in low-shock-risk patients dosed within 1 vs. 3 hours — supportive context for individualizing urgency rather than treating every "possible sepsis" chart as a 1-hour mandate.

05 · Deep Dive

Fluid resuscitation: stronger on crystalloid choice, new on what happens next

Balanced crystalloids & dynamic measures Upgraded

30 mL/kg initial crystalloid bolus in the first 3 hours is unchanged (carryover, conditional, low certainty)

Balanced crystalloids now carry an explicit preference "over" 0.9% saline rather than the softer 2021 "instead of" phrasing, and dynamic measures (passive leg raise, pulse pressure variation, etc.) are preferred over physical exam or static measures alone with strengthened wording.

What happens after the initial bolus new in 2026

Two genuinely new statements address the phase 2021 left unresolved: for patients with persistent hypoperfusion after the initial 30 mL/kg, the panel now suggests either a liberal or restrictive strategy based on individual patient and health-system factors — 2021 had insufficient evidence to say anything at all here. And for the first time, the guideline addresses the back end explicitly: active fluid removal (de-resuscitation) after the initial resuscitation phase is complete, aimed at preventing fluid overload.

06 · Deep Dive · Safety-Relevant

Vasopressors: still norepinephrine-first, but the "why" got more specific

Norepinephrine Downgraded vs. vasopressin/angiotensin II Carryover vs. dopamine

Read this one carefully — it is not a retreat from norepinephrine as first-line therapy

The 2026 guideline still recommends norepinephrine over dopamine with high certainty — unchanged and reaffirmed. What softened is the comparison specifically against vasopressin or angiotensin II: the panel now only suggests (rather than recommends) norepinephrine over those two agents, reflecting evolving evidence on when a non-catecholamine pressor might reasonably substitute or be added earlier.

New nuance: concomitant cardiac dysfunction new

For septic shock with concomitant cardiac dysfunction, the panel now suggests either norepinephrine or epinephrine as first-line — with a practical remark that norepinephrine may be preferred in tachyarrhythmia or significant sinus tachycardia, and epinephrine in bradyarrhythmia or significant sinus bradycardia. This did not exist as a distinct recommendation in 2021.

⚠ Two new "suggest against" statements
  • Beta-blockers in septic shock — suggested against, newly addressed in 2026.
  • Terlipressin — suggested against, newly addressed in 2026.

07 · Deep Dive

Corticosteroids: a qualifier quietly disappeared

IV corticosteroids Scope broadened

Both eras use "suggest" (conditional) — the change is in who qualifies, not how strongly it's recommended
⚡ Flagging unsettled interpretation

2021: "For adults with septic shock and an ongoing requirement for vasopressor therapy, we suggest using IV corticosteroids." 2026: "For adults with septic shock, we suggest using intravenous corticosteroids" — the vasopressor-dependency qualifier is gone from the headline statement. Some guideline trackers file this under "downgraded"; it may be more accurate to read it as a broadened eligibility population at unchanged conditional strength. Check the full-text remarks in the primary guideline before changing institutional corticosteroid triggers, since a qualifier disappearing from a summary table doesn't always mean the underlying rationale dropped the same nuance.

08 · Deep Dive

Blood purification: a narrow "no" became a broad one

Blood purification therapies Broadened "against"

2021 named one modality; 2026 names a class

2021: suggest against polymyxin B hemoperfusion specifically. 2026: suggest against hemoperfusion, high-dose hemofiltration, or plasma exchange as a class — closing off adjacent modalities that some centers had used as polymyxin B alternatives without dedicated evidence of their own.

09 · Deep Dive

An entirely new domain: post-sepsis survivorship

Posthospital recovery support New domain

2026 is the first SSC adult guideline to formally address life after the ICU. Health systems receive a good-practice statement to implement strategies supporting sepsis survivors and families during posthospital recovery. Notably, the panel found insufficient evidence to recommend structured sepsis-focused educational material for primary care providers over usual care — an honest gap flag rather than a manufactured recommendation.

10 · Safety Flag

Don't over-read the cardiac output monitoring statement

✓ Insufficient evidence ≠ don't use it

The panel states there is insufficient evidence to recommend minimally invasive or noninvasive cardiac output monitoring in addition to usual care for septic shock. This is an evidence gap, not a recommendation against the technology — don't let it be read in your department as "guideline says don't use it." Clinical judgment on when advanced hemodynamic monitoring adds value is unchanged by this statement.

11 · Methodology

How the panel got here

A 69-person international panel spanning 23 countries used GRADE methodology, developing PICO questions focused on patient-centered outcomes. Consensus required a minimum 75% response rate and 80% agreement; statements failing the first vote got up to two additional rounds.

12 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Stop leaning on qSOFA alone as your screening trigger — confirm your ED's sepsis alert runs on NEWS/NEWS2/MEWS/SIRS instead.
  • Push toward prolonged beta-lactam infusions where pharmacy workflows allow — this is now a strong recommendation, not a nice-to-have.
  • After the initial 30 mL/kg bolus, fluid strategy is explicitly individualized — there's no single "right" answer the guideline hands you, and active de-resuscitation once perfusion is restored is now itself a recommendation.
  • Norepinephrine remains first-line — the softened language applies to vasopressin/angiotensin II comparisons, not to dopamine, where the evidence against it is still high-certainty.
  • With concomitant cardiac dysfunction, either norepinephrine or epinephrine is reasonable first-line — let heart rate trends guide the choice.
  • Don't stop chasing corticosteroid eligibility questions on "not yet on vasopressors" alone — verify against full-text before changing your trigger.

Right Upper Quadrant (RUQ)17 diagnoses

Biliary and hepatic pathology dominates — and the biliary tree alone deserves a full differential of its own, not just ‘cholecystitis.’

Acute calculous cholecystitis

+
HallmarkMurphy's sign (inspiratory arrest on RUQ palpation) + fever + leukocytosis; US shows wall thickening >3mm, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy's sign, with stones present.
Pearl: Distinguish from biliary colic by duration and systemic signs — colic resolves in hours without fever; cholecystitis persists >6h with an inflammatory picture.

Acalculous cholecystitis

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkRUQ pain/fever in a critically ill, fasting/TPN-dependent, or mechanically ventilated patient, with cholecystitis findings on imaging but NO stones.
Pearl: Higher rate of gangrene and perforation than calculous disease because it's recognized later — keep it on the differential in any ICU patient with unexplained sepsis and no other source.

Emphysematous cholecystitis

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkGas within the gallbladder wall or lumen on imaging; disproportionately affects diabetics and older men.
Pearl: A more virulent, gas-forming process (often Clostridium or E. coli) with higher gangrene/perforation risk — treat as a surgical emergency, not routine cholecystitis management.

Biliary colic (symptomatic cholelithiasis)

+
HallmarkTransient RUQ/epigastric pain post-fatty meal, resolves within 1–6 hours, no fever or peritoneal signs.
Pearl: A normal-appearing gallbladder wall on US (no thickening/pericholecystic fluid) despite visible stones is what argues against cholecystitis — the stones themselves don't make the diagnosis.

Choledocholithiasis

+
HallmarkRUQ pain with elevated bilirubin/alk phos and a dilated common bile duct on imaging, without fever — a stone in the duct without infection yet.
Pearl: The absence of fever/hypotension here (vs. cholangitis below) generally allows urgent rather than emergent ERCP — but watch the trajectory closely since this can progress quickly.

Acute ascending cholangitis

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkCharcot's triad: fever, jaundice, RUQ pain. Reynold's pentad adds hypotension + altered mental status — a surgical/GI emergency requiring urgent biliary decompression (ERCP).
Pearl: Don't wait for all three Charcot components — sensitivity of the full triad is poor; a rising bilirubin with fever is enough to escalate.

Mirizzi syndrome

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkObstructive jaundice from a stone impacted in the cystic duct/gallbladder neck extrinsically compressing the common hepatic duct — can mimic a malignant biliary stricture on imaging.
Pearl: Consider in a patient with obstructive jaundice and a large impacted cystic duct stone before assuming cholangiocarcinoma — the imaging appearance can be deceptively similar.

Gallstone ileus

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkSmall bowel obstruction from a gallstone that eroded through a cholecystoenteric fistula; Rigler's triad on imaging — pneumobilia, SBO, and an ectopic gallstone.
Pearl: A rare cause of SBO, more common in older women without a hernia or prior surgery to otherwise explain obstruction — look specifically for pneumobilia on CT.

Biliary dyskinesia

+
HallmarkBiliary-colic-type pain with a normal ultrasound (no stones) and a low gallbladder ejection fraction on HIDA scan with CCK stimulation.
Pearl: Don't dismiss classic biliary colic symptoms just because the ultrasound is “normal” — HIDA/CCK-EF is the appropriate next step, and a low EF supports cholecystectomy.

Porcelain gallbladder / gallbladder carcinoma

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkCalcified gallbladder wall on imaging, often an incidental finding; carcinoma may present with a RUQ mass, weight loss, and jaundice in advanced disease.
Pearl: Porcelain gallbladder carries an associated increased gallbladder cancer risk (more modest than classically taught, but real) and is generally an indication for cholecystectomy.

Acute viral/toxic hepatitis

+
HallmarkRUQ pain/tenderness + jaundice + marked transaminitis (often in the thousands for viral/toxic causes).
Pearl: Ask specifically about acetaminophen (staggered overdose is easy to miss), supplements, and new medications — drug-induced liver injury is common and under-asked.

Hepatic abscess (pyogenic/amebic)

+
HallmarkFever + RUQ pain + travel history (amebic) or a biliary/GI source (pyogenic); imaging shows a rim-enhancing hepatic lesion.
Pearl: Amebic abscess classically occurs without active dysentery at presentation — travel history alone should raise it even without diarrhea.

Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkClassic triad: RUQ pain, hepatomegaly, and new ascites from hepatic venous outflow obstruction.
Pearl: Consider in a patient with a hypercoagulable state (myeloproliferative disorders are the most common underlying cause) presenting with painful hepatomegaly and new ascites.

Perforated duodenal ulcer (RUQ presentation)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSudden severe pain that can localize RUQ (tracking along the paracolic gutter) before becoming diffuse; free air under the diaphragm on upright CXR.
Pearl: Free air can be absent in up to 30% of perforations — a normal CXR does not exclude perforation if the clinical story fits.

Right lower lobe pneumonia (referred)

+
HallmarkRUQ pain with cough, fever, and tachypnea; exam may reveal decreased breath sounds/crackles at the right base.
Pearl: Diaphragmatic irritation from basilar pneumonia is a classic referred-pain mimic of RUQ pathology — a quick lung exam and CXR save an unnecessary RUQ US.

Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis)

PREGNANCY
+
HallmarkRUQ pain in a sexually active woman with concurrent or recent PID; “violin-string” adhesions between liver capsule and peritoneum if visualized surgically.
Pearl: Consider in any young woman with RUQ pain and pelvic pathology risk factors — pelvic exam and STI testing belong in the RUQ pain workup for this population.

Subphrenic / subhepatic abscess

+
HallmarkFever and RUQ pain days to weeks after abdominal surgery or perforation; imaging shows a fluid collection under the diaphragm or liver.
Pearl: Consider in a post-operative patient with a new fever plateau and RUQ discomfort — a delayed complication that's easy to miss once the patient is out of the immediate post-op window.

Left Upper Quadrant (LUQ)7 diagnoses

Splenic and gastric pathology; less common as a presenting complaint, which makes it easy to underweight.

Splenic infarct

+
HallmarkSudden sharp LUQ pain, often pleuritic; look for an embolic source (atrial fibrillation, endocarditis) or hemoglobinopathy (sickle cell).
Pearl: In sickle cell patients, recurrent infarcts lead to autosplenectomy over time — a normal-sized or small spleen on imaging doesn't rule out this history.

Splenic rupture

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkLUQ pain + hypotension after trauma, or spontaneous in a splenomegalic patient (classically infectious mononucleosis); Kehr's sign = referred left shoulder pain from diaphragmatic irritation.
Pearl: Ask about recent mono/EBV diagnosis and contact-sport participation — spontaneous rupture of a mono-enlarged spleen is a genuine, if uncommon, emergency.

Splenic abscess

+
HallmarkFever + LUQ pain in an immunocompromised patient or IV drug user; rim-enhancing splenic lesion on imaging.
Pearl: Consider in endocarditis with septic emboli — the spleen is a common seeding site.

Acute gastritis

+
HallmarkEpigastric/LUQ burning pain, often with NSAID, alcohol, or stress (critical illness) exposure; may have associated nausea/hematemesis if erosive.
Pearl: Ask specifically about OTC NSAID use — patients frequently don't volunteer ibuprofen or aspirin as a “medication.”

Left lower lobe pneumonia (referred)

+
HallmarkLUQ pain with respiratory symptoms; mirrors the RUQ/right-lung referred-pain mechanism.
Pearl: A basilar pneumonia can present as isolated upper-quadrant abdominal pain, especially in children and the elderly.

Splenic vein thrombosis

+
HallmarkLUQ pain with isolated gastric varices (without esophageal varices) on endoscopy, often in the setting of pancreatitis or pancreatic cancer.
Pearl: Consider in a patient with a pancreatitis history and a new upper GI bleed from isolated gastric varices — a distinct mechanism from portal-hypertension-related varices.

Pancreatic tail pseudocyst / mass

+
HallmarkLUQ fullness or pain, often weeks after a pancreatitis episode, with a cystic collection on imaging.
Pearl: A pseudocyst can compress adjacent structures or become infected — persistent LUQ pain after a resolved pancreatitis episode warrants imaging.

Epigastric12 diagnoses

Pancreatic, gastric, and cardiac pathology overlap here — the region most likely to hide a mimic.

Acute pancreatitis

+
HallmarkEpigastric pain radiating to the back, lipase >3x upper limit of normal; Cullen sign (periumbilical ecchymosis) and Grey Turner sign (flank ecchymosis) indicate hemorrhagic pancreatitis.
Pearl: Lipase magnitude does not correlate with severity — use a validated severity score (e.g., BISAP) rather than the lipase value itself to risk-stratify.

Chronic pancreatitis

+
HallmarkRecurrent epigastric pain with steatorrhea and/or new diabetes in a patient with chronic alcohol use or a prior acute pancreatitis history; pancreatic calcifications on imaging.
Pearl: Don't expect lipase to be markedly elevated the way it is in acute disease — calcifications on plain imaging are a late but fairly specific finding.

Peptic ulcer disease (gastric/duodenal)

+
HallmarkEpigastric pain with a meal-relationship pattern: gastric ulcers often worsen with food, duodenal ulcers classically improve with food and recur 2–3h later.
Pearl: H. pylori testing and NSAID history belong in every PUD workup — treating the ulcer without addressing the cause invites recurrence.

Perforated peptic ulcer

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSudden, severe, diffuse pain often following a preceding epigastric ulcer history; rigid “board-like” abdomen, free air on imaging.
Pearl: Pain can transiently improve as chemical peritonitis evolves before worsening again — don't be falsely reassured by a brief lull.

Boerhaave syndrome (esophageal rupture)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkMackler's triad: vomiting, severe chest/epigastric pain, and subcutaneous emphysema, following forceful retching/vomiting.
Pearl: Mortality rises sharply with delayed diagnosis — have a low threshold for contrast esophagram/CT after severe pain post-vomiting, especially with crepitus on exam.

GERD

+
HallmarkBurning epigastric/retrosternal pain, worse lying flat or postprandially, improved with antacids.
Pearl: GERD is a diagnosis of exclusion in a patient with cardiac risk factors and epigastric pain — don't anchor on it before an ECG.

Functional dyspepsia

+
HallmarkChronic/recurrent epigastric pain or fullness without an identifiable structural cause on endoscopy.
Pearl: A diagnosis of exclusion — alarm features (weight loss, GI bleeding, dysphagia, age >60 at onset) should prompt endoscopy before settling on this label.

Inferior MI (atypical GI presentation)

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkEpigastric pain, nausea, and diaphoresis without classic chest pain — particularly in diabetics, women, and the elderly.
Pearl: Get an ECG on essentially any epigastric pain patient with vascular risk factors, even when the story sounds like “indigestion.”

Aortic dissection (epigastric/back presentation)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSudden, severe, tearing pain, may radiate to the back; check pulse/BP differentials between extremities.
Pearl: A normal CXR or D-dimer does not exclude dissection — name the diagnosis and use a validated pretest tool (e.g., ADD-RS) rather than a single test to argue it away.

Gastric volvulus

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkBorchardt's triad: severe epigastric pain, retching without ability to vomit, and inability to pass a nasogastric tube.
Pearl: Rare, but the triad is close to pathognomonic — recognize it rather than attributing an unpassable NG tube to technique.

Gastric cancer

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkEpigastric pain with early satiety, unintentional weight loss, and possibly a palpable epigastric mass or Virchow's node (left supraclavicular) in advanced disease.
Pearl: New dyspepsia in a patient over 50, especially with alarm features (weight loss, anemia, dysphagia), warrants endoscopy rather than an empiric PPI trial alone.

Pancreatic cancer

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkPainless jaundice with a palpable, non-tender distended gallbladder (Courvoisier's sign) from head-of-pancreas tumors; epigastric pain radiating to the back in more advanced body/tail tumors.
Pearl: New-onset diabetes or unexplained migratory thrombophlebitis (Trousseau's syndrome) in an older adult can be an early paraneoplastic clue preceding the more obvious presentation.

Right Lower Quadrant (RLQ)12 diagnoses

Appendiceal and adnexal/gonadal pathology dominate, but inflammatory, infectious, and retroperitoneal mimics belong here too.

Acute appendicitis

CAN'T MISSPREGNANCYGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkPeriumbilical pain migrating to RLQ (McBurney's point), anorexia, low-grade fever, guarding; migration pattern reflects the shift from visceral to somatic pain.
Pearl: Presentation is blunted and delayed in pregnancy (appendix displaced upward/laterally by the gravid uterus) and in the elderly (attenuated fever/leukocytosis) — both groups perforate more often because diagnosis is delayed.

Ectopic pregnancy (right-sided)

CAN'T MISSPREGNANCY
+
HallmarkPositive pregnancy test + adnexal tenderness ± vaginal bleeding; rupture causes sudden severe pain, hypotension, and peritoneal signs.
Pearl: Check a pregnancy test in every reproductive-age patient with lower abdominal pain before attributing it to appendicitis or a GI cause — this is a non-negotiable step, not an optional one.

Ovarian torsion (right)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSudden severe unilateral pain with nausea/vomiting; US shows an enlarged ovary ± absent Doppler flow (flow can be present intermittently and does not exclude torsion).
Pearl: Don't be reassured by normal Doppler flow alone — torsion can intermittently detort, and clinical suspicion should drive emergent gynecologic evaluation regardless of flow findings.

Ovarian cyst rupture (right)

+
HallmarkSudden sharp pain, often mid-cycle (ruptured follicular/corpus luteum cyst); typically less peritoneal irritation than torsion, may have free fluid on US.
Pearl: Hemorrhagic cyst rupture in a patient on anticoagulation can cause significant hemoperitoneum — check a hemoglobin trend, not just a single value.

Testicular torsion (referred lower abdominal pain)

CAN'T MISSPEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkYounger males may present with lower abdominal pain before localizing to the scrotum; absent cremasteric reflex and a high-riding, horizontal testicle are supportive exam findings.
Pearl: Always ask about and examine the scrotum in a young male with lower abdominal pain — torsion is a time-sensitive (ideally <6h) diagnosis where imaging shouldn't delay urologic consultation if suspicion is high.

Mesenteric lymphadenitis (pediatric)

PEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkRLQ pain mimicking appendicitis in a child, often with a preceding or concurrent viral URI; CT/US shows enlarged mesenteric nodes with a normal appendix.
Pearl: A leading appendicitis mimic in children — the recent URI history is the key differentiator on history alone.

Meckel's diverticulitis

PEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkMimics appendicitis; classically associated with painless lower GI bleeding in children (rule of 2s: ~2% of population, 2 feet from ileocecal valve, 2% symptomatic).
Pearl: Consider in a child with recurrent “appendicitis-like” pain and a history of painless rectal bleeding — a Meckel's scan (technetium-99m) is the diagnostic study of choice.

Cecal diverticulitis

+
HallmarkRight-sided diverticulitis mimicking appendicitis, more common in patients of Asian descent and at a younger age than typical left-sided diverticulitis.
Pearl: CT differentiates the two; don't assume right-sided pain in a young patient is always appendicitis, especially in populations with higher right-sided diverticular prevalence.

Right-sided colon cancer

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkOften insidious — iron-deficiency anemia, occult bleeding, or vague RLQ discomfort rather than acute pain; can present acutely with obstruction or perforation.
Pearl: Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia in an adult over screening age warrants colon cancer workup even without dramatic pain.

Crohn's disease (terminal ileitis)

+
HallmarkRLQ pain mimicking appendicitis, often with a history of chronic diarrhea, weight loss, or prior similar episodes; imaging shows terminal ileal wall thickening rather than a dilated, non-compressible appendix.
Pearl: A normal appendix on CT with terminal ileal inflammation should prompt consideration of new or known Crohn's disease rather than proceeding straight to appendectomy.

Yersinia enterocolitica ileitis

PEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkRLQ pain and diarrhea mimicking appendicitis, often from undercooked pork or unpasteurized milk exposure; mesenteric adenitis and terminal ileitis with a grossly normal appendix if operated on.
Pearl: A classic “pseudoappendicitis” cause — consider it with the right exposure history, especially in children and young adults.

Psoas abscess

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPain with hip flexion (positive psoas sign), fever, and a limp or flexed-hip posture; often associated with adjacent GI pathology (Crohn's, diverticulitis, appendicitis) or vertebral osteomyelitis.
Pearl: Consider in a patient who holds the hip flexed and resists extension — a retroperitoneal process that's easy to miss without a directed exam maneuver.

Left Lower Quadrant (LLQ)7 diagnoses

Diverticular and adnexal pathology; sigmoid colon's fixed retroperitoneal position explains the pattern.

Acute diverticulitis

+
HallmarkLLQ pain (sigmoid colon), fever, leukocytosis — often called “left-sided appendicitis” for its analogous presentation.
Pearl: Uncomplicated diverticulitis in reliable, non-toxic outpatients is increasingly managed without antibiotics per some guidelines — know your local practice pattern before reflexively prescribing.

Ovarian torsion (left)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSame presentation and pearls as right-sided torsion, mirrored to the left adnexa.
Pearl: Laterality doesn't change the urgency — don't let a left-sided location lower your suspicion relative to the right.

Ectopic pregnancy (left)

CAN'T MISSPREGNANCY
+
HallmarkSame presentation and pearls as right-sided ectopic, mirrored to the left adnexa.
Pearl: Same non-negotiable pregnancy test rule applies regardless of laterality.

Ovarian cyst rupture (left)

+
HallmarkSame presentation and pearls as right-sided cyst rupture, mirrored to the left adnexa.
Pearl: Same hemoglobin-trend caution applies in anticoagulated patients regardless of laterality.

Sigmoid volvulus

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkElderly or nursing-home/chronically constipated patients with massive abdominal distension; “coffee bean” or “bent inner tube” sign on abdominal X-ray.
Pearl: A classic diagnosis in the chronically debilitated, immobile elderly patient — plain films are often diagnostic and can guide urgent decompression before CT is needed.

Ischemic colitis (splenic flexure / watershed)

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkCrampy LLQ pain followed by hematochezia, often in an older patient with vascular risk factors or a recent hypotensive episode; “thumbprinting” on imaging at watershed areas.
Pearl: Usually less severe than acute mesenteric ischemia (colonic, not small bowel) but still warrants inpatient monitoring for progression to transmural infarction.

Sigmoid colon cancer

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkChange in bowel habits, hematochezia, or LLQ discomfort with possible obstructive symptoms in an older adult.
Pearl: New LLQ pain with a change in stool caliber/frequency in a patient over screening age should prompt colonoscopy consideration even if an acute diverticulitis episode is also diagnosed.

Periumbilical / Diffuse10 diagnoses

Visceral referred pain, obstructive, vascular, and metabolic causes — the region where 'pain out of proportion to exam' matters most.

Early appendicitis (pre-migration)

+
HallmarkVague, poorly localized periumbilical pain before it migrates to the RLQ — reflects visceral referred pain via the T10 dermatome before somatic peritoneal irritation develops.
Pearl: A patient examined early in the course may have a benign-appearing abdomen — serial exams matter more than a single normal one if the story fits appendicitis.

Small bowel obstruction

+
HallmarkCrampy, colicky diffuse pain with distension, vomiting, and obstipation; prior abdominal surgery (adhesions) is the most common risk factor.
Pearl: High-pitched or absent bowel sounds are unreliable in isolation — CT with the transition point is the diagnostic study, and peritoneal signs should raise concern for strangulation.

Large bowel obstruction

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkDistension and obstipation with vomiting occurring later than in SBO, often from a colonic mass, volvulus, or severe stricture; imaging shows colonic dilation up to the transition point.
Pearl: A competent ileocecal valve can create a closed-loop obstruction with rapid cecal distension — cecal diameter >10–12cm is a surgical urgency for perforation risk.

Acute mesenteric ischemia

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkClassic teaching: pain out of proportion to a relatively benign-appearing exam, in a patient with vascular risk factors (atrial fibrillation, atherosclerosis, low-flow states).
Pearl: Lactate elevation is a late finding, not an early screening tool — a normal lactate does not exclude mesenteric ischemia early in its course; suspicion should drive CT angiography.

Diabetic ketoacidosis

CAN'T MISSPEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkDiffuse abdominal pain can be a presenting symptom of DKA, especially in children and young adults, independent of any intra-abdominal pathology.
Pearl: Check a point-of-care glucose and ketones in any diabetic (or undiagnosed young patient) with diffuse abdominal pain before pursuing an extensive GI workup.

Intussusception (pediatric)

CAN'T MISSPEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkIntermittent severe colicky pain with lethargy between episodes, “currant jelly” stools (a late finding), and a palpable sausage-shaped abdominal mass.
Pearl: The lethargy between episodes can be mistaken for a postictal or septic picture — consider intussusception in an infant/toddler with episodic pain and lethargy even without the classic stool finding.

Malrotation with midgut volvulus (infant)

CAN'T MISSPEDIATRIC
+
HallmarkBilious vomiting in an infant is midgut volvulus until proven otherwise; can progress rapidly to bowel necrosis.
Pearl: Bilious emesis in a neonate/young infant is a surgical emergency requiring immediate evaluation — one of the least forgivable misses in pediatrics given how fast ischemia progresses.

Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

CAN'T MISSGERIATRIC
+
HallmarkDiffuse abdominal and/or back pain, a pulsatile abdominal mass, and hypotension — the classic triad is present in only a minority of patients, so don't wait for all three.
Pearl: Consider in any older patient with unexplained hypotension and abdominal/flank/back pain, even without a known AAA history — bedside ultrasound can be diagnostic in seconds.

IBD flare (Crohn's disease / ulcerative colitis)

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkDiffuse or localized abdominal pain with bloody diarrhea (more classic for UC) or without (Crohn's more variable), weight loss, and a known IBD history.
Pearl: Toxic megacolon is the feared complication of a severe UC flare — colonic dilation with systemic toxicity is a surgical emergency, not just “another flare.”

Acute intermittent porphyria

+
HallmarkSevere diffuse abdominal pain with neuropsychiatric symptoms and dark/reddish urine, often triggered by certain medications, fasting, or hormonal changes.
Pearl: A rare but classic “surgical abdomen mimic” — normal imaging and labs in a patient with recurrent severe pain plus neuro/psych symptoms should raise this, especially with a triggering medication history.

Suprapubic / Pelvic7 diagnoses

Genitourinary and gynecologic pathology; overlaps heavily with the lower quadrants above.

Acute urinary retention

GERIATRIC
+
HallmarkSuprapubic pain/pressure with a palpably distended bladder; often in older men with BPH or in the setting of anticholinergic medications.
Pearl: Bladder scan or straight catheterization is both diagnostic and therapeutic — don't miss this easy fix while chasing a more complex differential.

Pelvic inflammatory disease

PREGNANCY
+
HallmarkBilateral lower abdominal/pelvic pain with cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness, and possibly fever in a sexually active woman.
Pearl: Treatment threshold is intentionally low — empiric treatment is recommended for sexually active women with pelvic/lower abdominal pain and no other identified cause, given the reproductive consequences of delayed treatment.

Tubo-ovarian abscess

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSevere bilateral or unilateral pelvic pain, fever, and a complex adnexal mass on imaging, typically a progression of untreated/undertreated PID.
Pearl: Rupture causes diffuse peritonitis and sepsis — a more severe, often inpatient-IV-antibiotic (± drainage) diagnosis than uncomplicated PID.

Endometriosis

+
HallmarkCyclic pelvic pain correlating with menses, often with a history of dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, or infertility.
Pearl: A chronic-pain diagnosis of exclusion in the acute setting — still rule out ectopic pregnancy and torsion first in any acute presentation before attributing pain to known endometriosis.

Mittelschmerz

+
HallmarkUnilateral mid-cycle pelvic pain coinciding with ovulation, typically mild and self-limited.
Pearl: A benign mimic of more serious adnexal pathology — still worth a pregnancy test and brief reassessment if pain is severe or persistent, since torsion/rupture can occur at any cycle point.

Acute cystitis

+
HallmarkSuprapubic pain/pressure with dysuria, frequency, and urgency; typically afebrile and without flank pain (which would suggest pyelonephritis instead).
Pearl: Flank pain, fever, or systemic symptoms should prompt reclassification as pyelonephritis with a different treatment course — don't undertreat an upper-tract infection as simple cystitis.

Ovarian mass/cyst (non-ruptured)

+
HallmarkDull, persistent pelvic pain or pressure with an adnexal mass on exam/imaging, without the sudden severe onset of torsion or rupture.
Pearl: Size and characteristics on ultrasound (simple vs. complex) guide urgency — a large or rapidly growing mass still warrants gynecologic follow-up even without acute features.

Flank5 diagnoses

Renal and retroperitoneal pathology; the colicky, writhing patient who can't get comfortable is the classic tell.

Nephrolithiasis (renal colic)

+
HallmarkSevere colicky flank pain radiating to the groin, with hematuria (present in the majority but its absence doesn't exclude a stone); patients characteristically writhe and cannot find a comfortable position.
Pearl: The writhing, can't-sit-still patient is a genuine bedside discriminator from peritonitis, where patients lie still to avoid movement — a useful gestalt clue while workup is pending.

Pyelonephritis

PREGNANCY
+
HallmarkFlank pain with fever, CVA (costovertebral angle) tenderness, and lower urinary tract symptoms (dysuria, frequency).
Pearl: Consider complicating factors — obstruction (stone), pregnancy, diabetes, or immunocompromise — that change disposition from oral outpatient therapy to admission and imaging.

Renal infarct

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSudden severe flank pain, often with an embolic source (atrial fibrillation, endocarditis) or hypercoagulable state; can mimic renal colic but typically lacks the classic colicky waxing/waning pattern.
Pearl: An underrecognized mimic of renal colic — consider it in a patient with atrial fibrillation and acute flank pain, especially if urinalysis lacks the hematuria you'd expect with a stone.

Renal vein thrombosis

+
HallmarkFlank pain with hematuria and acute kidney injury, classically associated with nephrotic syndrome (loss of antithrombin III) or another hypercoagulable state.
Pearl: Consider in a patient with known nephrotic syndrome and new flank pain — an underrecognized complication of the hypercoagulable state nephrotic syndrome creates.

Retroperitoneal hematoma

CAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkFlank/back pain with a falling hemoglobin, often in an anticoagulated patient, sometimes after a femoral vascular access procedure; Grey Turner sign (flank ecchymosis) can appear if hemorrhage tracks subcutaneously.
Pearl: Consider in any anticoagulated patient with new flank/back pain and hypotension or a hemoglobin drop, even without a clear traumatic mechanism — recent vascular access is a key historical clue.

Hallmarks and pearls are the highest-yield single clues, not the complete diagnostic picture for any of these — always confirm against current literature, local protocols, and your own clinical judgment before applying.

Acid Suppression / Reflux5 agents

PPIs, H2 blockers, antacids, and reflux-specific mechanical/motility agents — the foundation of PUD/GERD management and stress ulcer prophylaxis.

Omeprazole (and PPI class)

+
Mechanism of actionIrreversibly inhibits the H+/K+-ATPase (proton pump) in gastric parietal cells, blocking the final common step of acid secretion.
IndicationsGERD, PUD, H. pylori eradication (combination therapy), stress ulcer prophylaxis, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.
Dosage20–40mg PO daily, up to BID for severe disease/ZES; IV formulations available for inpatient/NPO patients.
PharmacokineticsProdrug activated in the acidic parietal cell canaliculus; half-life ~1h but effect lasts 24+ hours because binding is irreversible — full effect requires several days of dosing as pumps are progressively bound. Hepatic metabolism (CYP2C19).
ContraindicationsCaution with concurrent clopidogrel — CYP2C19 competition can reduce clopidogrel's activation to its active form; use pantoprazole instead if acid suppression is needed alongside clopidogrel.
Considerations: Long-term use is associated with increased C. diff risk, fracture risk, and B12/magnesium deficiency; take 30–60 minutes before a meal for best effect; rebound acid hypersecretion can occur on abrupt discontinuation after prolonged use.

Famotidine (H2RA class)

+
Mechanism of actionCompetitively blocks histamine H2 receptors on parietal cells, reducing acid secretion — less potent than PPIs.
IndicationsGERD, PUD, stress ulcer prophylaxis; also used as an H2-blockade adjunct in urticaria/anaphylaxis management.
Dosage20–40mg PO/IV BID; reduce dose in renal impairment.
PharmacokineticsOnset ~1h, duration 10–12h, half-life 2.5–3.5h (prolonged in renal impairment), renally eliminated.
ContraindicationsDose-adjust for renal impairment; rare QT prolongation reported at high doses.
Considerations: Tachyphylaxis develops with continuous multi-day use; less potent than PPIs but a reasonable short-term/PRN option given fewer long-term risk associations.

Calcium carbonate / aluminum-magnesium hydroxide (antacids)

+
Mechanism of actionDirect chemical neutralization of gastric acid.
IndicationsImmediate symptomatic relief of dyspepsia/heartburn.
DosageVaries by formulation; typically PRN with meals and at symptom onset.
PharmacokineticsOnset within minutes; short duration (30 min–2h); minimal systemic absorption, though some renal excretion of magnesium/aluminum occurs.
ContraindicationsAvoid magnesium/aluminum-containing antacids in significant renal impairment (accumulation risk); separate from other oral medications by 2 hours since antacids chelate and reduce absorption of many drugs (fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, levothyroxine).
Considerations: Magnesium-containing formulations cause diarrhea; aluminum-containing formulations cause constipation — many products combine both to offset each other.

Alginate-antacid raft-forming agents (e.g., Gaviscon)

+
Mechanism of actionAlginate reacts with gastric acid to form a viscous, low-density gel “raft” that floats on top of gastric contents, creating a physical barrier that blocks reflux into the esophagus — a mechanical effect distinct from acid neutralization or suppression.
IndicationsGERD, particularly postprandial and positional (bending/lying down) breakthrough symptoms.
Dosage10–20mL PO after meals and at bedtime.
PharmacokineticsActs locally and mechanically within the stomach; not systemically absorbed.
ContraindicationsSome formulations contain significant sodium or calcium content — caution in renal impairment or sodium-restricted patients.
Considerations: A genuinely useful add-on for patients with breakthrough postprandial reflux despite standing PPI therapy, since the mechanism (physical barrier) is complementary rather than redundant with acid suppression.

Baclofen (for refractory GERD)

+
Mechanism of actionGABA-B receptor agonist that reduces the frequency of transient lower esophageal sphincter relaxations (TLESRs) — the underlying mechanism behind the majority of reflux episodes, including many that occur despite adequate acid suppression.
IndicationsRefractory GERD with a significant TLESR component, particularly when symptoms persist despite optimized PPI therapy.
Dosage5–20mg PO TID; start low and titrate.
PharmacokineticsRenal elimination; crosses the blood-brain barrier, accounting for its CNS side effects.
ContraindicationsRenal impairment (dose reduction needed); do not stop abruptly after prolonged use — abrupt discontinuation carries a withdrawal seizure risk.
Considerations: CNS side effects (sedation, dizziness) often limit tolerability; generally reserved for refractory cases under specialist guidance rather than used as an early-line GERD therapy.

Metoclopramide (Reglan)

+
Mechanism of actionDopamine D2 receptor antagonist (central and peripheral) with 5-HT4 agonist activity; increases lower esophageal sphincter tone, gastric antral contractions, and gastric emptying; central antiemetic effect via chemoreceptor trigger zone D2 blockade.
IndicationsGastroparesis, GERD, nausea/vomiting, migraine adjunct.
Dosage10mg IV/PO up to QID; limit duration of use to under 12 weeks given tardive dyskinesia risk.
PharmacokineticsOnset: IV 1–3 min, PO 30–60 min; half-life 5–6h; renally eliminated (dose-adjust in renal impairment).
ContraindicationsParkinson's disease, GI obstruction/perforation, seizure disorder (lowers seizure threshold), pheochromocytoma.
Considerations: Carries a black box warning for tardive dyskinesia with prolonged use; can cause acute extrapyramidal symptoms/akathisia — treat with diphenhydramine or benztropine; give as a slow IV push to reduce akathisia risk. Most effective for: gastroparesis and GERD-related nausea, given its dual prokinetic/antiemetic action — the only agent on this list that treats both the motility problem and the nausea it causes.

Prochlorperazine (Compazine)

+
Mechanism of actionPhenothiazine; D2 receptor antagonist at the chemoreceptor trigger zone, with additional anticholinergic and antihistaminergic activity.
IndicationsNausea/vomiting broadly, and independently effective as a migraine abortive agent (not just for the associated nausea).
Dosage10mg IV/IM/PO q6–8h PRN; 25mg PR suppository.
PharmacokineticsOnset 10–20 min IV; half-life ~7h; hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsParkinson's disease, severe CNS depression, bone marrow suppression, children under 2 years or under 20lbs.
Considerations: Higher extrapyramidal symptom/akathisia risk than metoclopramide in some studies — many EDs co-administer diphenhydramine; also carries QT-prolongation risk. Most effective for: migraine headache with associated nausea — one of the best-evidenced components of an ED migraine cocktail, with efficacy against the headache itself, not just the nausea.

Droperidol

+
Mechanism of actionButyrophenone; potent D2 receptor antagonist with additional alpha-adrenergic blocking activity, producing a strong central antiemetic and sedative effect.
IndicationsRefractory nausea/vomiting, post-operative nausea, migraine (effective abortive agent).
Dosage0.625–1.25mg IV for antiemetic/migraine use — notably lower than the doses used for agitation.
PharmacokineticsOnset 3–10 min IV; half-life ~2h; hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsKnown QT prolongation or congenital long QT syndrome.
Considerations: Carries an FDA black box warning (added 2001) for QT prolongation and torsades de pointes risk, which sharply curtailed its use for years despite strong efficacy data; many EDs have since reintroduced it at low doses with baseline ECG/electrolyte optimization given accumulating reassuring real-world safety data — institutional protocols vary, so know your local policy. Most effective for: refractory nausea that hasn't responded to ondansetron/metoclopramide, and migraine — comparable or superior efficacy to prochlorperazine in some head-to-head data.

Promethazine (Phenergan)

+
Mechanism of actionPhenothiazine antihistamine (H1 antagonist) with anticholinergic and antidopaminergic (D2) activity; antiemetic effect via both CTZ D2 blockade and vestibular/H1-mediated pathways.
IndicationsNausea/vomiting broadly, particularly vertigo and motion-sickness-related nausea; also used as a sedative/allergic-reaction adjunct.
Dosage12.5–25mg IV/IM/PO/PR q4–6h PRN.
PharmacokineticsOnset ~20 min (faster IV); half-life 9–16h; hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsChildren under 2 years (black box warning — respiratory depression risk), severe CNS depression, narrow-angle glaucoma.
Considerations: Carries a black box warning for severe tissue injury (including gangrene) with IV extravasation or accidental intra-arterial injection — give IM or via a well-running large-bore IV line, never subcutaneously or intra-arterially. Most effective for: vertigo/motion-sickness-associated nausea, where its antihistamine/anticholinergic action on vestibular pathways gives it an edge over pure D2 antagonists.

Haloperidol (low-dose, antiemetic use)

+
Mechanism of actionButyrophenone; D2 receptor antagonist, mechanistically similar to droperidol.
IndicationsRefractory nausea/vomiting, including opioid-induced nausea; emerging off-label use for cyclic vomiting syndrome and cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome.
Dosage0.5–2mg IV/IM for antiemetic use — substantially lower than psychiatric/agitation dosing.
PharmacokineticsOnset 15–30 min IV; half-life 14–26h (notably longer than droperidol); hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsParkinson's disease, QT prolongation, severe CNS depression.
Considerations: Shares the extrapyramidal symptom and QT-prolongation risks of the antidopaminergic class. Most effective for: opioid-induced nausea and, per a growing body of ED case-series evidence, cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome and cyclic vomiting syndrome — though it isn't FDA-approved specifically for either indication.

Diphenhydramine

+
Mechanism of actionH1 antihistamine with anticholinergic properties; antiemetic effect primarily via vestibular/central H1 blockade, and separately useful for preventing or treating extrapyramidal side effects from antidopaminergic antiemetics.
IndicationsVertigo-associated nausea; routine adjunct given alongside metoclopramide/prochlorperazine/droperidol/haloperidol to prevent or treat EPS.
Dosage25–50mg IV/IM/PO q6h PRN.
PharmacokineticsOnset 15–30 min; half-life 4–8h (longer in the elderly); hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsNarrow-angle glaucoma; relative caution in severe asthma/COPD (anticholinergic drying of secretions) and with other CNS depressants.
Considerations: Significant sedation and anticholinergic burden — particularly risky in elderly patients (delirium risk; on the Beers criteria list). Most effective for: vertigo/motion-sickness nausea, and as EPS prophylaxis whenever a D2-antagonist antiemetic is given.

Scopolamine (transdermal)

+
Mechanism of actionAnticholinergic (muscarinic antagonist); reduces vestibular signal input to the central vomiting center.
IndicationsMotion sickness, vertigo-related nausea, post-operative nausea prophylaxis.
DosageOne transdermal patch behind the ear, applied at least 4 hours before the anticipated need (travel, surgery); effect lasts up to 72 hours.
PharmacokineticsSlow onset (hours) due to transdermal delivery; prolonged duration from a depot effect in the skin.
ContraindicationsNarrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention/BPH, myasthenia gravis.
Considerations: Delayed onset makes it unsuitable for acute, already-occurring nausea — it must be applied proactively, before the trigger. Most effective for: motion sickness and vestibular-origin nausea specifically; not useful for chemotherapy-induced or gastroenteritis-related nausea given its distinct, vestibular-specific mechanism.

Dexamethasone (antiemetic adjunct)

+
Mechanism of actionCorticosteroid; the precise antiemetic mechanism is incompletely understood but involves anti-inflammatory/anti-edema effects, prostaglandin inhibition, and modulation of central neurotransmitter pathways.
IndicationsChemotherapy-induced nausea/vomiting (standard adjunct for moderate-to-highly emetogenic regimens), post-operative nausea/vomiting prophylaxis.
Dosage4–10mg IV single dose for acute/prophylactic use; higher, scheduled dosing as part of formal multi-day chemo antiemetic protocols.
PharmacokineticsLong biologic half-life (36–54h); hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsActive untreated systemic infection (relative), uncontrolled hyperglycemia.
Considerations: Single-dose antiemetic use carries minimal steroid-related risk. Most effective for: chemotherapy-induced nausea as part of a combination regimen (with a 5-HT3 antagonist ± an NK1 antagonist), and post-op nausea prophylaxis given at induction — it's an adjunct that potentiates other antiemetics rather than a standalone treatment.

Domperidone

+
Mechanism of actionPeripheral D2 receptor antagonist; because it does not meaningfully cross the blood-brain barrier, it produces prokinetic/antiemetic effects (acting at the area postrema, which lies outside the BBB) with substantially less central/extrapyramidal side-effect burden than metoclopramide.
IndicationsGastroparesis, GERD-related nausea; used as a first-line prokinetic in many countries outside the US.
Dosage10mg PO TID–QID (dosing varies by country).
PharmacokineticsMinimal CNS penetration (a P-glycoprotein substrate at the blood-brain barrier); hepatic metabolism (CYP3A4).
ContraindicationsNot FDA-approved or commercially available in the US (accessible only via expanded-access IND or compounding); QT prolongation risk, which has led several countries to restrict its availability; avoid with other QT-prolonging drugs.
Considerations: The key US-practice pearl is availability, not efficacy — it's genuinely first-line internationally for gastroparesis specifically because it avoids metoclopramide's EPS/tardive dyskinesia risk, but most US clinicians will never have it on formulary. Most effective for: long-term gastroparesis management when EPS risk from metoclopramide is a concern (e.g., Parkinson's disease patients needing prokinetic support, or patients who've already had an EPS reaction to metoclopramide).

Ondansetron

+
Mechanism of actionSelective 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, blocking serotonin at vagal afferent terminals in the GI tract and centrally at the chemoreceptor trigger zone.
IndicationsNausea/vomiting from chemotherapy, post-operative, gastroenteritis, and general ED use.
Dosage4–8mg IV/PO/ODT; FDA safety limit of 16mg for a single IV dose given dose-dependent QT prolongation.
PharmacokineticsOnset ~30 min; half-life 3–6h; hepatic metabolism (CYP3A4, 2D6, 1A2) with renal elimination of metabolites.
ContraindicationsCongenital long QT syndrome; caution with other QT-prolonging medications.
Considerations: QT prolongation is dose-dependent — obtain an ECG for high doses or in patients with electrolyte abnormalities or other QT-prolonging medications, and correct hypokalemia/hypomagnesemia before or alongside administration.

Erythromycin (prokinetic dosing)

+
Mechanism of actionMotilin receptor agonist, stimulating antral contractions and accelerating gastric emptying — distinct from its antibacterial mechanism.
IndicationsGastroparesis (off-label); pre-endoscopy administration for upper GI bleed to improve visualization by clearing retained blood/contents.
Dosage3mg/kg IV over 30–60 minutes (pre-endoscopy); 250mg PO TID–QID for gastroparesis — notably lower than antibacterial dosing.
PharmacokineticsHepatic metabolism via CYP3A4 (both substrate and inhibitor, creating significant interaction potential); biliary excretion.
ContraindicationsQT prolongation; avoid with other QT-prolonging drugs or strong CYP3A4 interactions.
Considerations: Prokinetic benefit is subject to tachyphylaxis within days to weeks, limiting usefulness for chronic gastroparesis management; screen the medication list carefully for CYP3A4 interactions before use.

Pain / Antispasmodics3 agents

GI-specific antispasmodics and mucosal-protective agents — general analgesia is covered in the separate analgesics atlas.

Dicyclomine

+
Mechanism of actionAnticholinergic/antimuscarinic agent with direct smooth muscle relaxant effect on the GI tract, reducing spasm.
IndicationsIBS, functional abdominal pain/spasm.
Dosage10–20mg PO QID.
PharmacokineticsOnset 1–2h PO; parent drug half-life ~1.8h, though active metabolites persist longer; hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsGlaucoma, GI/GU obstruction, myasthenia gravis, severe ulcerative colitis.
Considerations: Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, constipation) warrant caution in the elderly (anticholinergic burden/delirium risk); avoid in severe UC — anticholinergic slowing of colonic motility can precipitate toxic megacolon (see the IBD section).

Hyoscyamine

+
Mechanism of actionAnticholinergic/antispasmodic, mechanistically similar to dicyclomine.
IndicationsIBS, adjunct for biliary/renal colic-associated spasm, general GI spasm.
Dosage0.125–0.25mg SL/PO QID PRN.
PharmacokineticsRapid onset sublingually; half-life 3–5h.
ContraindicationsSame anticholinergic-class contraindications as dicyclomine: glaucoma, GI/GU obstruction, myasthenia gravis, severe ulcerative colitis.
Considerations: Same toxic-megacolon caution as dicyclomine applies — avoid anticholinergic antispasmodics in a patient with known or suspected severe/fulminant colitis.

Sucralfate (Carafate)

+
Mechanism of actionForms a viscous protective barrier by binding to proteins exposed at an ulcer base in an acidic environment, shielding it from acid, pepsin, and bile.
IndicationsPUD (adjunct), stress ulcer prophylaxis, oral/esophageal mucositis pain relief.
Dosage1g PO QID, ideally on an empty stomach — 1 hour before meals and at bedtime.
PharmacokineticsMinimal systemic absorption; acts locally at the mucosal surface.
ContraindicationsCaution in renal failure (aluminum component can accumulate with prolonged use).
Considerations: Binds and reduces absorption of many other oral medications — separate dosing by at least 2 hours; requires an acidic environment to activate, so avoid co-administering simultaneously with acid suppressants when possible.

Esophageal Spasm4 agents

Agents targeting esophageal smooth muscle specifically — mechanistically distinct from the intestinal antispasmodics above, since esophageal spastic disorders respond to a different drug class entirely.

Diltiazem (esophageal spasm)

+
Mechanism of actionCalcium channel blocker; reduces smooth muscle contractility in the esophageal wall, lowering both the amplitude and frequency of spastic contractions.
IndicationsDiffuse esophageal spasm, and the related hypercontractile disorders (“nutcracker”/jackhammer esophagus).
Dosage60–90mg PO before meals (dosing extrapolated from small studies rather than large trials).
PharmacokineticsHepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsHypotension, significant bradycardia/heart block, severe left ventricular dysfunction.
Considerations: Evidence base is modest (small studies/case series), but the favorable safety profile makes it a reasonable first trial before escalating to more invasive options like endoscopic botulinum toxin injection.

Sildenafil (esophageal spasm)

+
Mechanism of actionPDE-5 inhibitor; increases cGMP within esophageal smooth muscle, promoting relaxation — the same mechanistic class as its better-known indication, applied here to the esophagus instead.
IndicationsRefractory spastic esophageal motility disorders not responding to first-line measures.
DosageUsed off-label; dosing is variable and drawn from small studies rather than a standardized regimen.
PharmacokineticsHepatic metabolism (CYP3A4).
ContraindicationsConcurrent nitrates (risk of severe, potentially life-threatening hypotension), unstable cardiac disease.
Considerations: Based on limited trial data — reserve for specialist-guided use in refractory cases, and screen the medication list carefully for nitrates before considering it.

Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (imipramine, amitriptyline)

+
Mechanism of actionVisceral pain modulation through central and peripheral mechanisms at doses well below those used for depression — not primarily a motility effect, but a reduction in esophageal hypersensitivity/pain perception.
IndicationsFunctional chest pain of esophageal origin and esophageal hypersensitivity, including some presentations of non-cardiac chest pain once cardiac causes are excluded.
DosageSubstantially lower than antidepressant dosing — e.g., imipramine 25–50mg at bedtime.
PharmacokineticsHepatic metabolism; anticholinergic and antihistaminergic properties account for most side effects.
ContraindicationsCardiac conduction abnormalities (QT prolongation risk), narrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention risk.
Considerations: Effect takes weeks to build, so set expectations accordingly; anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth, sedation, constipation) can limit tolerability, particularly in older adults.

Peppermint oil

+
Mechanism of actionDirect smooth muscle relaxant effect via calcium-channel-blocking activity on GI (including esophageal) smooth muscle.
IndicationsEsophageal spasm and, more commonly, IBS-related intestinal spasm as an adjunct.
DosageEnteric-coated capsules or diluted oil formulations; dosing varies by product.
PharmacokineticsActs locally on GI smooth muscle; minimal systemic absorption with enteric-coated formulations.
ContraindicationsSignificant GERD or hiatal hernia.
Considerations: Generally well-tolerated with a favorable safety profile, making it a reasonable low-risk first adjunct — but its lower esophageal sphincter-relaxing effect can worsen reflux, so avoid it (or use cautiously) in patients with significant GERD.

Constipation5 agents

Bulk-forming, osmotic, stimulant, and secretagogue agents, plus opioid-induced constipation (PAMORA) options.

Polyethylene glycol 3350

+
Mechanism of actionOsmotic laxative; non-absorbable, retains water in the colon to soften stool and increase volume, stimulating peristalsis.
IndicationsAcute and chronic constipation; also used at higher volumes for bowel preparation.
Dosage17g powder dissolved in 4–8oz liquid PO daily for constipation; much larger volumes for bowel prep protocols.
PharmacokineticsNot systemically absorbed; onset 1–3 days with daily dosing, or 30–60 minutes with large-volume bowel-prep dosing.
ContraindicationsBowel obstruction, ileus.
Considerations: Generally considered first-line for chronic constipation given a favorable safety and tolerability profile, with minimal electrolyte disturbance compared to other osmotic agents.

Lactulose

+
Mechanism of actionNon-absorbable synthetic disaccharide; osmotic effect in the colon, and separately, colonic bacterial fermentation acidifies the lumen, converting ammonia (NH3) to ammonium (NH4+) which is then trapped and excreted.
IndicationsConstipation; hepatic encephalopathy.
DosageConstipation: 15–30mL PO daily–BID. Hepatic encephalopathy: 30–45mL PO TID–QID, titrated to 2–3 soft stools per day.
PharmacokineticsMinimal systemic absorption; colonic bacterial metabolism produces gas, which drives its most common side effect (bloating/flatulence).
ContraindicationsGalactosemia.
Considerations: For hepatic encephalopathy, titrate to clinical stool response rather than a fixed dose — this is a titrate-to-effect medication, not a set-and-forget one.

Senna / Bisacodyl (stimulant laxatives)

+
Mechanism of actionStimulates colonic myenteric plexus activity, increasing peristalsis, and alters colonic electrolyte/water transport.
IndicationsConstipation (short-term/PRN), opioid-induced constipation prevention (typically paired with a stool softener), bowel-prep adjunct.
DosageSenna 8.6–17.2mg PO daily–BID; bisacodyl 5–10mg PO daily or 10mg PR suppository.
PharmacokineticsOnset ~6–12h orally; bisacodyl suppository onset 15–60 minutes.
ContraindicationsBowel obstruction, acute abdomen/suspected appendicitis, severe dehydration.
Considerations: First-line adjunct for opioid-induced constipation prophylaxis, generally paired with a stool softener; reserve for intermittent rather than indefinite daily use where possible.

Docusate sodium

+
Mechanism of actionStool softener (surfactant) — reduces stool surface tension, allowing water and fat to penetrate and soften it.
IndicationsPrevention of straining (post-operative, post-MI, hemorrhoids); mild constipation prophylaxis.
Dosage100mg PO BID.
PharmacokineticsMinimal systemic absorption.
ContraindicationsNo major contraindications.
Considerations: Evidence for docusate as monotherapy for *established* constipation is weak — it's best used preventively; pair with a stimulant or osmotic agent when actually treating existing constipation.

Methylnaltrexone (PAMORA)

+
Mechanism of actionPeripherally-acting mu-opioid receptor antagonist; blocks opioid effects on the GI tract without meaningfully crossing the blood-brain barrier, preserving central analgesia.
IndicationsOpioid-induced constipation refractory to standard laxative therapy.
Dosage8–12mg SC (weight-based), or 450mg PO daily.
PharmacokineticsMinimal CNS penetration (does not reverse central analgesic effect); renal elimination.
ContraindicationsKnown or suspected GI obstruction.
Considerations: Reserved for refractory opioid-induced constipation after standard laxatives fail; use caution in patients with compromised GI wall integrity (e.g., malignancy) given a reported risk of GI perforation.

Diarrhea / GI Bleeding Adjuncts6 agents

Anti-motility, adsorbent, and targeted secretory-diarrhea agents, plus the somatostatin/octreotide class used for upper GI/variceal bleeding.

Loperamide

+
Mechanism of actionPeripheral mu-opioid receptor agonist acting on intestinal smooth muscle, slowing motility and increasing water/electrolyte absorption; minimal CNS penetration at therapeutic doses.
IndicationsAcute/chronic non-infectious diarrhea, IBS-D.
Dosage4mg initial dose, then 2mg after each loose stool, OTC max 16mg/day.
PharmacokineticsOnset ~1h; half-life 9–14h; hepatic metabolism; minimal systemic absorption/CNS penetration at labeled doses.
ContraindicationsBloody diarrhea or high fever (invasive bacterial pathogen concern until excluded), C. diff-associated diarrhea/pseudomembranous colitis, and active severe ulcerative colitis — toxic megacolon risk in all three.
Considerations: Supratherapeutic/misuse dosing (taken for an opioid-like euphoric effect) is a recognized cause of fatal cardiac arrhythmias (QT prolongation, torsades) — “loperamide abuse” is a real ED presentation, not just a theoretical risk.

Diphenoxylate-atropine

+
Mechanism of actionDiphenoxylate is an opioid agonist slowing GI motility; atropine is added in a subtherapeutic dose specifically to discourage deliberate misuse (causes anticholinergic effects at higher doses).
IndicationsAcute diarrhea.
Dosage2.5–5mg (1–2 tablets) PO QID PRN, per product labeling.
PharmacokineticsHepatic metabolism to the active metabolite difenoxin; crosses the blood-brain barrier more than loperamide at high doses, contributing to abuse potential.
ContraindicationsSame infectious-diarrhea and pseudomembranous colitis contraindications as loperamide; avoid in young children (respiratory depression risk).
Considerations: Higher CNS depression/abuse potential than loperamide given better CNS penetration — loperamide is generally preferred when an anti-motility agent is appropriate.

Bismuth subsalicylate

+
Mechanism of actionAntisecretory, mildly antimicrobial, and anti-inflammatory (salicylate-mediated) effects on the GI tract.
IndicationsTraveler's diarrhea (treatment and prophylaxis), dyspepsia, mild diarrhea.
Dosage524mg (2 tablets or 30mL) PO QID PRN.
PharmacokineticsThe salicylate component is absorbed systemically (relevant for aspirin allergy/Reye syndrome concerns); bismuth itself is minimally absorbed.
ContraindicationsAspirin allergy; avoid in children/teenagers with a viral illness (Reye syndrome risk); caution with concurrent anticoagulation given the salicylate component.
Considerations: Causes harmless black discoloration of the stool and tongue — worth counseling patients on proactively so it isn't mistaken for melena and doesn't trigger an unnecessary GI bleed workup.

Octreotide

+
Mechanism of actionSomatostatin analog; inhibits secretion of multiple GI hormones (gastrin, VIP, insulin, glucagon), reduces splanchnic blood flow, and inhibits intestinal secretion.
IndicationsRefractory secretory diarrhea (carcinoid syndrome, VIPoma), variceal bleeding adjunct.
DosageVariceal bleeding: 50mcg IV bolus, then 25–50mcg/hr infusion. Secretory diarrhea: 50–100mcg SC BID–TID, titrated.
PharmacokineticsHalf-life ~1.5–2h (immediate-release); hepatic metabolism.
ContraindicationsCaution in gallstone disease — reduces gallbladder motility and promotes gallstone formation with prolonged use.
Considerations: Not a first-line agent for routine/undifferentiated diarrhea — reserved for specific secretory or variceal-bleeding indications, not general use.

Somatostatin (native peptide, upper GI/variceal bleeding)

+
Mechanism of actionThe native peptide hormone from which octreotide was synthetically derived — the same broad inhibitory effects on splanchnic blood flow and GI hormone secretion, reducing portal pressure and bleeding from varices.
IndicationsAcute variceal upper GI bleeding; used as an adjunct in some acute non-variceal bleeding protocols. More commonly used outside the US, where octreotide has largely taken its place.
Dosage250mcg IV bolus, then a continuous infusion of 250–500mcg/hr — continuous infusion is essential, not optional, given the pharmacokinetics below.
PharmacokineticsExtremely short half-life of only 1–3 minutes (compared to octreotide's ~90–120 minutes) — this is the single defining difference between the two drugs, and why somatostatin cannot be dosed intermittently the way octreotide can.
ContraindicationsSimilar cautions to octreotide, notably gallbladder motility effects with prolonged use.
Considerations: Worth knowing this exists as a distinct agent from octreotide, even in US practice where it's rarely stocked — octreotide's much longer half-life and resulting practical dosing advantage is exactly why it displaced somatostatin in most American EDs and ICUs despite sharing the same mechanism.

Cholestyramine

+
Mechanism of actionBile acid sequestrant; binds bile acids in the gut lumen, preventing their reabsorption and their colonic-irritant/secretory effect.
IndicationsBile acid diarrhea (post-cholecystectomy diarrhea, ileal resection or Crohn's-related bile acid malabsorption), pruritus of cholestasis.
Dosage4g PO 1–2x/day, titrated.
PharmacokineticsNot systemically absorbed; acts locally within the gut lumen.
ContraindicationsComplete biliary obstruction.
Considerations: Significantly reduces absorption of many other oral medications and fat-soluble vitamins — separate dosing by several hours; particularly useful in Crohn's patients with ileal disease or resection causing bile acid malabsorption (see the IBD section).

Antibiotics for GI Infections5 agents

C. diff, traveler's diarrhea, intra-abdominal/diverticulitis, and H. pylori eradication regimens.

Vancomycin (oral, for C. difficile)

+
Mechanism of actionInhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis; the oral formulation acts locally within the gut lumen against C. diff with minimal systemic absorption.
IndicationsC. difficile infection — current first-line therapy per updated IDSA/SHEA guidelines for both initial and severe episodes.
Dosage125mg PO QID standard dosing; 500mg PO QID for severe/fulminant disease, often combined with IV metronidazole and rectal vancomycin if ileus is present.
PharmacokineticsMinimal systemic absorption when taken orally — this is why oral vancomycin does not require the nephrotoxicity/level monitoring used for IV vancomycin.
ContraindicationsNo major contraindications for oral use given minimal systemic absorption.
Considerations: This first-line status is a relatively recent guideline shift — worth actively updating if your working knowledge still defaults to metronidazole-first for C. diff.

Metronidazole

+
Mechanism of actionForms cytotoxic free radicals within anaerobic/microaerophilic organisms after intracellular reduction, causing DNA damage.
IndicationsIntra-abdominal infections requiring anaerobic coverage (diverticulitis, abscess), H. pylori regimens, giardiasis, amebiasis; alternative/adjunct for C. diff when vancomycin/fidaxomicin is unavailable or in combination for fulminant disease.
DosageC. diff (adjunct/alternative): 500mg PO TID. Intra-abdominal infection: 500mg IV/PO q8h, combined with an agent covering gram-negative/aerobic organisms.
PharmacokineticsExcellent oral bioavailability; hepatic metabolism; half-life 6–8h.
ContraindicationsAvoid alcohol during treatment and for 3 days after (disulfiram-like reaction); relative caution in the first trimester of pregnancy.
Considerations: Metallic taste is a common complaint; peripheral neuropathy can occur with prolonged use; no longer first-line monotherapy for C. diff per current guidelines (see oral vancomycin above).

Rifaximin

+
Mechanism of actionNon-absorbed rifamycin derivative; inhibits bacterial RNA synthesis, acting locally within the gut lumen.
IndicationsTraveler's diarrhea, hepatic encephalopathy (adjunct to lactulose), IBS-D.
DosageTraveler's diarrhea: 200mg PO TID x3 days. Hepatic encephalopathy: 550mg PO BID.
PharmacokineticsMinimal systemic absorption (<1%); acts locally.
ContraindicationsHypersensitivity to rifamycins.
Considerations: Not effective for invasive bacterial diarrhea (e.g., invasive Shigella/Salmonella with systemic toxicity) given its minimal systemic absorption — appropriate only for non-invasive traveler's diarrhea, not a undifferentiated febrile dysentery.

Ciprofloxacin + metronidazole (diverticulitis/intra-abdominal regimen)

+
Mechanism of actionCiprofloxacin inhibits bacterial DNA gyrase/topoisomerase IV (gram-negative coverage); metronidazole provides anaerobic coverage (mechanism above).
IndicationsAcute diverticulitis (complicated, or selected uncomplicated cases), other mixed aerobic-anaerobic intra-abdominal infections.
DosageCiprofloxacin 500mg PO BID (or 400mg IV q12h) plus metronidazole 500mg PO/IV TID, typically 4–7 days depending on severity and response.
PharmacokineticsCiprofloxacin: good oral bioavailability, renal elimination (dose-adjust in renal impairment). Metronidazole: as above.
ContraindicationsFluoroquinolones: tendon rupture risk (higher with concurrent steroids or in the elderly), QT prolongation, and an aortic aneurysm/dissection warning; avoid in myasthenia gravis.
Considerations: Uncomplicated diverticulitis in reliable, non-toxic outpatients is increasingly managed without antibiotics per some guidelines — reserve this regimen for complicated disease, immunocompromise, significant comorbidity, or systemic toxicity; amoxicillin-clavulanate alone is a reasonable single-agent alternative.

H. pylori eradication regimens (triple / bismuth quadruple therapy)

+
Mechanism of actionCombination regimen: a PPI raises gastric pH to improve antibiotic efficacy, paired with 2–3 antimicrobial agents targeting H. pylori.
IndicationsH. pylori-associated PUD, gastric MALT lymphoma, and any confirmed H. pylori infection.
DosageTriple therapy: PPI BID + amoxicillin 1g BID + clarithromycin 500mg BID x14 days. Bismuth quadruple therapy: PPI BID + bismuth subsalicylate + metronidazole 250–500mg QID + tetracycline 500mg QID x10–14 days.
PharmacokineticsCombination regimen — pharmacokinetics of each component as detailed in their individual entries above.
ContraindicationsPenicillin allergy rules out amoxicillin-containing triple therapy (use bismuth quadruple instead); clarithromycin has significant CYP3A4 drug-interaction potential (notably certain statins and QT-prolonging drugs).
Considerations: Rising clarithromycin resistance has shifted many regions toward bismuth quadruple therapy as first-line rather than reserving it for treatment failures; confirm eradication with urea breath or stool antigen testing at least 4 weeks after completing therapy and after holding the PPI for 1–2 weeks beforehand to avoid a false negative.

Dosages reflect typical adult ED/outpatient dosing conventions — always verify against current prescribing information, renal/hepatic dose adjustments, and local formulary before prescribing.

Risk Factors & Epidemiology

Family history / genetics

First-degree relatives have substantially increased risk; NOD2/CARD15 mutations specifically associated with ileal Crohn's disease.

Age of onset

Bimodal distribution — a peak in the teens/20s and a second, smaller peak in the 50s–60s.

Ethnicity

Ashkenazi Jewish populations have a notably increased incidence of both UC and Crohn's.

Smoking

The single most divergent risk factor between the two diseases — harmful and disease-worsening in Crohn's, but associated with a lower incidence and milder course of UC (never a reason to recommend starting, given smoking's other harms).

Appendectomy

Some epidemiologic association with reduced UC risk, particularly when performed for confirmed appendicitis at a young age; the relationship with Crohn's is less clear and possibly opposite.

NSAIDs

Can trigger flares in both diseases and are generally best avoided in known IBD patients in favor of acetaminophen for pain control.

Geography / “hygiene hypothesis”

Higher incidence in industrialized, urban environments, with rising incidence in newly industrializing regions — implicates environmental/microbiome factors alongside genetic susceptibility.

Diet

Western dietary pattern (high fat, high refined sugar, low fiber) shows epidemiologic association with IBD risk, though causality remains unproven and this shouldn't be overstated to patients as a cause of their disease.

Extraintestinal Manifestations

A useful organizing principle: manifestations that parallel bowel disease activity generally improve when the flare is treated; those that run an independent course do not, and need their own separate management track.

Peripheral arthritis

Typically parallels intestinal disease activity — tends to improve as the underlying flare is treated.

Axial arthritis / ankylosing spondylitis

Runs an independent course from bowel activity — treating the flare will not necessarily improve axial symptoms.

Erythema nodosum

Tender, red nodules (classically the anterior shins) that generally parallel disease activity.

Pyoderma gangrenosum

Can arise independent of bowel disease activity; classically starts as a pustule that ulcerates with a violaceous, undermined border — avoid debridement, which can worsen it (pathergy).

Episcleritis

Generally parallels bowel disease activity; typically not sight-threatening.

Uveitis

Runs independent of bowel activity and is potentially sight-threatening — warrants urgent ophthalmology involvement regardless of how the underlying bowel disease is doing.

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)

Classically associated with ulcerative colitis (though it occurs in Crohn's too); runs a course independent of bowel disease activity and increases cholangiocarcinoma risk — an important long-term surveillance consideration distinct from the colorectal cancer risk below.

Venous thromboembolism

IBD itself is an independent hypercoagulable state, with risk further elevated during active flares — relevant to VTE prophylaxis decisions covered in the ER management section below.

Long-Term Disease Trajectory — What These Patients Are Likely to Face

The natural history each disease tends toward, beyond the current flare — useful for counseling patients on what surveillance and vigilance actually looks like long-term.

Ulcerative colitis

ColectomyRoughly 15–30% of UC patients will require colectomy over their lifetime, with risk correlating with disease extent and severity.
Colorectal cancer surveillanceSurveillance colonoscopy typically begins 8–10 years after diagnosis (earlier and more frequently with pancolitis or concurrent PSC), given cumulative cancer risk with extensive, long-duration disease.
Toxic megacolonA severe-flare complication (see ER management below) — the reason severe colitis is managed as an inpatient emergency rather than an outpatient medication adjustment.
DysplasiaChronic mucosal inflammation over years predisposes to dysplastic changes, which is the biologic basis for surveillance colonoscopy rather than relying on symptoms to prompt screening.

Crohn's disease

Stricture → bowel obstructionTransmural inflammation with chronic fibrosis narrows the bowel lumen over time, a common cause of recurrent partial or complete obstruction.
Fistula formationEnteroenteric, enterocutaneous, enterovesical, and rectovaginal fistulas can all develop from transmural, penetrating disease — often requiring combined medical and surgical management.
Abscess formationPenetrating disease can seed intra-abdominal or perianal abscesses, requiring drainage in addition to medical therapy.
Eventual surgeryUp to 70–80% of Crohn's patients will require at least one surgery over their lifetime — and unlike UC, surgery is not curative; disease recurrence at or near the anastomosis is common.
Malnutrition / vitamin deficiencyTerminal ileal disease or resection impairs B12 and bile acid absorption; extensive small bowel involvement can impair fat-soluble vitamin (A, D, E, K) absorption.
Colorectal cancerElevated specifically with colonic involvement, similar in principle to UC's extent-and-duration-based risk.

IBD-Specific Medications

The treatment ladder from topical anti-inflammatory therapy through steroid-sparing and biologic agents — each with mechanism, indications, dosage, contraindications, and considerations.

Mesalamine (5-ASA)

+
Mechanism of actionTopical anti-inflammatory action within the gut lumen (mechanism not fully elucidated; involves NF-κB pathway inhibition and modulation of prostaglandin/leukotriene production).
IndicationsInduction and maintenance of remission in mild-to-moderate ulcerative colitis; considerably less effective in Crohn's disease.
DosageOral formulations vary by brand/release mechanism (pH-dependent or time-dependent coating targeting the terminal ileum/colon); rectal suppositories/enemas for isolated distal (proctitis/left-sided) disease.
ContraindicationsSalicylate hypersensitivity.
Considerations: Formulation and route matter clinically — combining oral and rectal mesalamine is more effective than oral alone for left-sided or extensive disease; a common real-world undertreatment error is prescribing oral therapy alone for distal disease that would benefit from a rectal formulation.

Corticosteroids (prednisone, budesonide, IV methylprednisolone/hydrocortisone)

+
Mechanism of actionBroad anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive action via the glucocorticoid receptor.
IndicationsInduction of remission in moderate-to-severe flares — explicitly not a maintenance therapy given cumulative side-effect burden.
DosagePrednisone 40–60mg/day oral with a taper; IV methylprednisolone 40–60mg/day or hydrocortisone 100mg IV q8h for severe/hospitalized flares; budesonide 9mg/day for mild-moderate ileal/right-colon Crohn's (high first-pass hepatic metabolism gives fewer systemic effects than prednisone).
ContraindicationsUntreated systemic infection; live vaccines should be avoided while on immunosuppressive doses.
Considerations: Steroid-dependence (unable to taper without relapse) or steroid-refractoriness is a treatment-failure signal that should prompt escalation to a steroid-sparing agent, not another steroid course — taper appropriately to avoid adrenal insufficiency, and screen for latent TB/hepatitis B before the biologic therapy that often follows.

Azathioprine / 6-mercaptopurine (thiopurines)

+
Mechanism of actionPurine synthesis inhibition, producing a broad immunosuppressive effect.
IndicationsSteroid-sparing maintenance therapy; frequently combined with a biologic to reduce immunogenicity (anti-drug antibody formation) against the biologic agent.
DosageAzathioprine 2–2.5mg/kg/day; 6-mercaptopurine 1–1.5mg/kg/day.
ContraindicationsKnown TPMT deficiency without dose adjustment (check TPMT genotype/phenotype before starting — severe myelosuppression risk in poor metabolizers).
Considerations: Slow onset of action (weeks to months) — not appropriate for acute flare induction; requires regular CBC/LFT monitoring; long-term use carries increased lymphoma and non-melanoma skin cancer risk, so sun protection counseling is worth including.

Anti-TNF biologics (infliximab, adalimumab)

+
Mechanism of actionBind and neutralize tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interrupting a key inflammatory cytokine cascade in IBD.
IndicationsModerate-to-severe UC or Crohn's, steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent disease, and fistulizing Crohn's disease (a particular strength of this class).
DosageInfliximab 5mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 8 weeks; adalimumab 160mg SC at week 0, 80mg at week 2, then 40mg every 2 weeks.
ContraindicationsActive serious infection, untreated latent TB, a history of demyelinating disease; relative contraindication in NYHA class III/IV heart failure.
Considerations: Screen for latent TB and hepatitis B before starting; increased infection risk; rare but serious associations include lymphoma, demyelinating disease, and drug-induced lupus-like syndrome — combination with a thiopurine can reduce anti-drug antibody formation and improve durability of response.

Vedolizumab (gut-selective biologic)

+
Mechanism of actionBlocks the α4β7 integrin, preventing lymphocyte adhesion and migration specifically into gut tissue — gut-selective rather than systemically immunosuppressive.
IndicationsModerate-to-severe UC or Crohn's, including as an option when anti-TNF therapy has failed or is contraindicated.
Dosage300mg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 8 weeks.
ContraindicationsActive serious infection.
Considerations: The gut-selective mechanism carries a theoretically lower risk of systemic infection/malignancy compared to anti-TNF agents, though onset of action can be slower — counsel patients that full effect may take longer to become apparent than with anti-TNF therapy.

Ustekinumab (anti-IL-12/23 biologic)

+
Mechanism of actionBlocks the shared p40 subunit of interleukin-12 and interleukin-23, interrupting Th1 and Th17-mediated inflammatory pathways.
IndicationsModerate-to-severe Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, including after anti-TNF failure.
DosageWeight-based IV induction dose, then 90mg SC every 8 weeks for maintenance.
ContraindicationsActive serious infection.
Considerations: Generally well-tolerated with a favorable long-term safety profile relative to older biologic classes; still requires the same baseline infection screening (TB, hepatitis B) as other biologics before starting.

JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib)

+
Mechanism of actionBlock Janus kinase (JAK)-STAT intracellular signaling downstream of multiple cytokine receptors, broadly dampening inflammatory signaling.
IndicationsModerate-to-severe ulcerative colitis refractory to other therapies.
DosageTofacitinib 10mg PO BID for induction, then 5mg PO BID for maintenance.
ContraindicationsActive serious infection; use caution in patients over 50 with cardiovascular risk factors.
Considerations: Carries a black box warning for increased risk of major cardiovascular events, malignancy, venous thromboembolism, and death compared to TNF inhibitors, based on post-marketing safety data — this warrants an explicit risk-benefit discussion, particularly in older patients or those with cardiovascular risk factors.

ER Flare Management

Severity assessment

The Truelove and Witts criteria remain the most widely used bedside severity framework for UC: mild disease is <4 stools/day without systemic toxicity; severe disease is ≥6 bloody stools/day plus at least one of: heart rate >90, temperature >37.8°C, hemoglobin <10.5g/dL, or ESR >30. Crohn's severity is less standardized at the bedside but the same systemic toxicity markers (fever, tachycardia, anemia, elevated inflammatory markers) apply.

Labs to send

  • CBC (anemia, leukocytosis), CRP/ESR (inflammatory burden), albumin (nutritional status and a severity marker), electrolytes
  • Stool studies, including C. difficile toxin/PCR — non-negotiable. Superimposed C. diff can both mimic and trigger an IBD flare, and its presence changes management substantially (treat the infection; be cautious about escalating immunosuppression on top of an active C. diff infection)
  • Stool culture/ova and parasites to exclude infectious mimics when the picture isn't clearly a known IBD flare

Imaging

CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast for any suspected complication — abscess, perforation, obstruction, or toxic megacolon — particularly relevant in Crohn's given its transmural complications. A plain abdominal X-ray is a fast first screen for colonic dilation (toxic megacolon, generally defined as transverse colon diameter >6cm) or free air.

🚩 The single most important prescribing pearl in this section

Avoid anti-motility agents (loperamide, diphenoxylate-atropine) and anticholinergic antispasmodics (dicyclomine, hyoscyamine) in a patient with severe or fulminant colitis — slowing colonic motility in this setting is a recognized precipitant of toxic megacolon. This is one of the few places in this whole atlas where the standard symptomatic-relief reflex is actively dangerous.

Treatment approach

  • IV fluid and electrolyte repletion
  • IV corticosteroids (methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone) for moderate-severe flares
  • VTE prophylaxis — IBD flares are an independent hypercoagulable state, and most guidelines support VTE prophylaxis during hospitalization even in the presence of GI bleeding, which surprises many clinicians on first encounter
  • GI and/or surgery consultation for severe flares or any sign of a structural complication
  • Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics are reasonable when a complication (perforation, abscess, toxic megacolon) is suspected, but are not indicated for an uncomplicated flare

🚩 Can't-miss complications

  • Toxic megacolon — colonic dilation with systemic toxicity; a surgical emergency
  • Perforation — free air, peritonitis
  • Massive lower GI bleeding
  • Abscess or fistula (Crohn's) and bowel obstruction from stricturing disease (Crohn's)

Hospitalization Criteria / Red Flags

  • Meets Truelove-Witts severe criteria (above)
  • Signs of toxic megacolon — abdominal distension, decreased/absent bowel sounds, systemic toxicity, colonic dilation on imaging
  • Peritoneal signs or suspected perforation
  • Inability to tolerate oral intake or significant dehydration
  • Significant GI bleeding, especially if requiring transfusion
  • Failure of outpatient steroid therapy (steroid-refractory flare)
  • New or significant electrolyte derangement
  • Signs of sepsis or a suspected abscess

Patient Education for Flare Prevention

Medication adherence

The single biggest modifiable factor — non-adherence to maintenance therapy is a leading, and largely preventable, cause of relapse. Worth asking about directly and non-judgmentally at every visit.

NSAID avoidance

NSAIDs can trigger flares in both UC and Crohn's — steer patients toward acetaminophen for pain control where possible.

Smoking

Cessation counseling is appropriate for Crohn's patients given its clear disease-worsening effect and association with higher surgical recurrence. For UC, cessation can occasionally coincide with a flare in former smokers — still worth recommending given smoking's broader health risks, but worth mentioning to patients so an unexpected flare after quitting isn't mistaken for a treatment failure.

Infection precautions

Review vaccination status, especially before starting or while on biologics/immunosuppressants — live vaccines should be avoided during active immunosuppression. Reinforce hand hygiene and sick-contact avoidance.

Stress management

Stress doesn't cause IBD, but it can worsen symptom perception and may influence flare frequency in some patients — a referral for stress-reduction strategies or counseling is a reasonable adjunct, not a replacement for medical therapy.

Diet

No single diet has strong evidence for preventing flares, though a low-residue/low-fiber approach can help symptomatically during active flares or with known strictures. Avoid implying to patients that diet caused their disease — that framing adds guilt without evidence behind it.

Lab monitoring adherence

Patients on thiopurines or biologics need regular CBC/LFT monitoring (and sometimes drug-level monitoring) — missed labs are a preventable gap that can let a preventable complication go unnoticed.

Early symptom recognition

Encourage patients to seek care early when they notice their own early flare symptoms rather than waiting — earlier intervention generally means a shorter, milder course and a lower chance of needing hospitalization.

01

Sternocleidomastoid (SCM)

Origin

Sternal head: manubrium; clavicular head: medial clavicle

Insertion

Mastoid process and lateral superior nuchal line

Nerve / Root

Spinal accessory (CN XI) — motor; C2–C3 — proprioception/pain

Action

Unilateral: lateral flexion to same side + rotation of face to OPPOSITE side. Bilateral: neck flexion, accessory inspiration

Blood Supply

Sternocleidomastoid branch of occipital and superior thyroid arteries

Palpation

Grasp the muscle belly between thumb and fingers as the patient rotates the head against resistance

Special Test

Resisted rotation to the opposite side while palpating; CN XI tested via resisted shoulder shrug + head rotation against resistance

Referred Pain

Trigger points refer to the occiput, vertex, cheek, and periorbital region — classic non-vascular headache mimic

Clinical Pearl

Congenital torticollis presents with a palpable SCM mass/fibrosis in infancy; painful acute torticollis in adults is usually a muscle spasm, not a structural lesion — red flags (fever, trauma, neuro deficit) should prompt imaging

02

Trapezius (upper/middle/lower fibers)

Origin

External occipital protuberance, nuchal ligament, spinous processes C7–T12

Insertion

Upper: lateral clavicle; Middle: acromion/scapular spine; Lower: base of scapular spine

Nerve / Root

Spinal accessory (CN XI) — motor; C3–C4 — proprioception

Action

Upper: scapular elevation + upward rotation; Middle: scapular retraction; Lower: scapular depression + upward rotation

Blood Supply

Transverse cervical artery

Palpation

Trace from occiput along the nuchal line to the scapular spine and down to T12 spinous processes

Special Test

Shoulder shrug against resistance (upper fibers); scapular retraction against resistance (middle fibers)

Referred Pain

Upper fibers: classic trigger point refers up the posterolateral neck to the temple — a very common tension headache generator

Clinical Pearl

Isolated CN XI injury (e.g., after lymph node biopsy in the posterior triangle) causes shoulder droop and winging with weak abduction past 90°, distinct from long thoracic nerve winging

03

Scalenes (anterior, middle, posterior)

Origin

Transverse processes of C3–C7

Insertion

Anterior/middle: 1st rib; Posterior: 2nd rib

Nerve / Root

Ventral rami of C3–C8 (segmental)

Action

Ipsilateral lateral flexion, contralateral rotation assist, elevate ribs 1–2 as accessory inspiratory muscles

Blood Supply

Ascending cervical artery

Palpation

Palpate lateral to the SCM in the posterior triangle, deep and posterior to the clavicular head

Special Test

Adson's test (radial pulse change with neck extension/rotation to ipsilateral side + inspiration) screens for TOS, though poor specificity

Referred Pain

Refers to the chest, medial scapular border, and down the arm into the thumb and index finger — frequently mistaken for cervical radiculopathy

Clinical Pearl

The brachial plexus and subclavian artery pass between anterior and middle scalenes — hypertrophy or a cervical rib narrows this space, producing neurogenic or arterial thoracic outlet syndrome

04

Longus colli / Longus capitis

Origin

Anterior vertebral bodies/transverse processes C1–T3

Insertion

Atlas and vertebral bodies above origin (colli); basilar occiput (capitis)

Nerve / Root

Ventral rami C1–C6

Action

Primary neck flexors; stabilize cervical lordosis

Blood Supply

Ascending cervical and vertebral arteries

Palpation

Not directly palpable externally — deep prevertebral muscles

Special Test

Resisted chin tuck / craniocervical flexion test

Referred Pain

Deep aching anterior neck pain, often with odynophagia when inflamed

Clinical Pearl

Retropharyngeal calcific tendinitis (longus colli) mimics epiglottitis/retropharyngeal abscess — CT shows amorphous calcification anterior to C1–C2 without a rim-enhancing fluid collection

05

Splenius capitis / cervicis

Origin

Nuchal ligament, spinous processes C7–T6

Insertion

Capitis: mastoid process/superior nuchal line; Cervicis: transverse processes C1–C3

Nerve / Root

Dorsal rami of middle cervical spinal nerves

Action

Bilateral: neck extension; Unilateral: ipsilateral lateral flexion and rotation

Blood Supply

Occipital and deep cervical arteries

Palpation

Deep to trapezius along the posterolateral neck below the occiput

Special Test

Resisted neck extension with rotation toward the tested side

Referred Pain

Vertex headache (splenius capitis) — a classic cervicogenic headache pattern

Clinical Pearl

Part of the deep extensor sling most affected in whiplash-associated disorder; deconditioning here contributes to chronic post-whiplash headache

06

Levator scapulae

Origin

Transverse processes C1–C4

Insertion

Superior angle of scapula

Nerve / Root

Dorsal scapular nerve (C5) + direct branches of C3–C4

Action

Scapular elevation and downward rotation; assists ipsilateral neck lateral flexion

Blood Supply

Transverse cervical and dorsal scapular arteries

Palpation

Superior angle of the scapula, deep to upper trapezius

Special Test

Resisted scapular elevation with the neck rotated and flexed away from the tested side (stretches the muscle under load)

Referred Pain

Angle of the neck into the superior/medial scapular border — a very common myofascial pain complaint

Clinical Pearl

"Text neck"/desk-posture pain generator — chronically shortened with forward head posture, producing the classic superior angle knot patients point to directly

07

Suboccipital group (rectus capitis post. major/minor, obliquus capitis sup./inf.)

Origin

C1–C2 posterior arch/spinous process and C2 transverse process

Insertion

Occiput and atlas transverse process (fine control of the atlanto-occipital/atlanto-axial joints)

Nerve / Root

Suboccipital nerve (dorsal ramus C1)

Action

Fine proprioceptive control and small-range extension/rotation of the head on C1–C2

Blood Supply

Vertebral and occipital arteries — the vertebral artery loops through this triangle, vulnerable in cervical manipulation

Palpation

Just below the occiput, medial to the mastoid process

Special Test

Not isolated on standard MMT; assessed indirectly via cervicogenic headache/dizziness provocation testing

Referred Pain

Occipital and retro-orbital headache — greater occipital neuralgia often originates from irritation near this group

Clinical Pearl

Extremely high proprioceptor density (among the highest of any skeletal muscle) — implicated in cervicogenic dizziness and cervicogenic headache via dense connections to the trigeminocervical nucleus

Shoulder Girdle

11 muscles
01

Deltoid (anterior / middle / posterior)

Origin

Anterior: lateral clavicle; Middle: acromion; Posterior: scapular spine

Insertion

Deltoid tuberosity of humerus

Nerve / Root

Axillary nerve (C5–C6)

Action

Anterior: flexion + internal rotation; Middle: abduction (0–90°, prime mover); Posterior: extension + external rotation

Blood Supply

Posterior circumflex humeral artery

Palpation

Lateral shoulder over the humeral head; ask patient to abduct against resistance

Special Test

Resisted abduction at 90°; sensory testing over the lateral shoulder (regimental badge area) for axillary nerve integrity

Referred Pain

Diffuse deep ache over the deltoid insertion (lateral arm) — a nonspecific referral zone shared with rotator cuff pathology

Clinical Pearl

Axillary nerve is the muscle most at risk in anterior shoulder dislocation and proximal humerus (surgical neck) fracture — always document regimental badge sensation before and after reduction

02

Supraspinatus

Origin

Supraspinous fossa of scapula

Insertion

Greater tuberosity of humerus (superior facet)

Nerve / Root

Suprascapular nerve (C5–C6)

Action

Initiates abduction (first 0–15°) and works with deltoid throughout abduction; compresses humeral head into glenoid (dynamic stabilizer)

Blood Supply

Suprascapular artery

Palpation

Just anterior to the scapular spine in the supraspinous fossa, or the tendon insertion just anterior/inferior to the acromion

Special Test

Empty can (Jobe) test — resisted abduction at 90° with thumbs pointed down; drop-arm sign for full-thickness tear

Referred Pain

Deep lateral deltoid/upper arm ache — rarely localizes precisely to the scapula despite the muscle belly's location

Clinical Pearl

Most commonly torn rotator cuff tendon — impinges beneath the coracoacromial arch (Neer/Hawkins-Kennedy positive); full-thickness tears show a positive drop-arm sign

03

Infraspinatus

Origin

Infraspinous fossa of scapula

Insertion

Greater tuberosity (middle facet)

Nerve / Root

Suprascapular nerve (C5–C6)

Action

Primary external rotator of the arm with the shoulder at the side; dynamic humeral head stabilizer

Blood Supply

Suprascapular and circumflex scapular arteries

Palpation

Below the scapular spine, over the infraspinous fossa

Special Test

Resisted external rotation with elbow at 90° flexed and adducted to the side

Referred Pain

Anterior shoulder and deep into the arm — infraspinatus trigger points are a very common source of anterior shoulder pain mistaken for biceps tendinopathy

Clinical Pearl

Weakness here (with intact teres minor) suggests a suprascapular nerve lesion at the spinoglenoid notch (e.g., from a paralabral cyst) rather than a primary cuff tear

04

Teres minor

Origin

Upper 2/3 of lateral scapular border

Insertion

Greater tuberosity (inferior facet)

Nerve / Root

Axillary nerve (C5–C6)

Action

External rotation and weak adduction/extension; dynamic stabilizer

Blood Supply

Circumflex scapular artery

Palpation

Lower lateral scapular border, just above teres major

Special Test

Hornblower's sign — inability to hold the hand near the mouth with the arm abducted 90° indicates teres minor + infraspinatus tear

Referred Pain

Posterior shoulder overlying the muscle itself, fairly localized compared to other cuff muscles

Clinical Pearl

Shares the axillary nerve with deltoid — quadrangular space syndrome (compression by fibrous bands/hypertrophy) can selectively weaken teres minor with posterior shoulder pain and lateral arm numbness

05

Subscapularis

Origin

Subscapular fossa (anterior scapula)

Insertion

Lesser tuberosity of humerus

Nerve / Root

Upper and lower subscapular nerves (C5–C6)

Action

Primary internal rotator; anterior dynamic stabilizer preventing anterior humeral head translation

Blood Supply

Subscapular artery

Palpation

Difficult to palpate directly; assessed functionally rather than manually

Special Test

Lift-off test (Gerber) and belly-press test — inability to lift the hand off the lower back, or elbow drops back during belly press, indicates subscapularis dysfunction

Referred Pain

Posterior shoulder capsule region despite being the most anterior cuff muscle — a frequent source of diagnostic confusion

Clinical Pearl

Subscapularis tear is the most commonly missed cuff tear on exam — always test it specifically, since anterior instability and biceps subluxation (through a torn pulley) often coexist

06

Teres major

Origin

Inferior angle of scapula

Insertion

Medial lip of intertubercular (bicipital) groove of humerus

Nerve / Root

Lower subscapular nerve (C5–C7)

Action

Adduction, extension, and internal rotation — "lat's little helper"

Blood Supply

Subscapular artery

Palpation

Inferior scapular angle, anterior to latissimus dorsi as it wraps around

Special Test

Resisted internal rotation and adduction from a flexed/abducted position

Referred Pain

Posterior axillary fold and posterior arm, often indistinguishable from latissimus referral clinically

Clinical Pearl

Forms the posterior wall of the quadrangular space along with teres minor, long head of triceps, and humeral shaft — relevant landmark for axillary nerve/posterior circumflex humeral vessel compression

07

Latissimus dorsi

Origin

Spinous processes T7–L5, thoracolumbar fascia, iliac crest, lower 3–4 ribs, inferior scapular angle

Insertion

Floor of intertubercular groove of humerus

Nerve / Root

Thoracodorsal nerve (C6–C8)

Action

Extension, adduction, and internal rotation of the shoulder; "cough muscle" / crutch-walking muscle (depresses the trunk on a fixed arm)

Blood Supply

Thoracodorsal artery

Palpation

Posterior axillary fold — grasp it as the patient coughs or adducts against resistance

Special Test

Resisted shoulder adduction/extension from an abducted, externally rotated start

Referred Pain

Inferior scapular angle and posterior shoulder, sometimes extending down the medial arm to the ring/little finger — mimics ulnar-sided complaints

Clinical Pearl

Harvested as a myocutaneous flap in breast reconstruction because of its reliable, isolated thoracodorsal pedicle — iatrogenic injury during axillary lymph node dissection produces winging distinct from serratus/trapezius patterns

08

Pectoralis major (clavicular / sternocostal heads)

Origin

Clavicular head: medial clavicle; Sternocostal head: sternum and costal cartilages 1–6

Insertion

Lateral lip of intertubercular groove of humerus

Nerve / Root

Lateral pectoral nerve (clavicular head, C5–C7) and medial pectoral nerve (sternocostal head, C8–T1)

Action

Adduction and internal rotation; clavicular head flexes, sternocostal head extends the flexed arm back down

Blood Supply

Thoracoacromial and lateral thoracic arteries

Palpation

Anterior chest wall/axillary fold; have the patient push hands together ("prayer test") to bring out the muscle bulk

Special Test

Resisted horizontal adduction ("crush" test) with arms at 90° flexion

Referred Pain

Anterior chest and medial arm — pectoral trigger points can mimic cardiac chest pain and must be distinguished with ECG/troponin when risk factors are present

Clinical Pearl

Rupture (classically bench-press-related) causes a palpable/visible defect and ecchymosis at the axillary fold; MRI grades tear location (myotendinous vs. bony avulsion) which drives surgical vs. conservative management

09

Pectoralis minor

Origin

Ribs 3–5

Insertion

Coracoid process of scapula

Nerve / Root

Medial pectoral nerve (C8–T1)

Action

Scapular protraction/depression and downward rotation; accessory inspiration

Blood Supply

Thoracoacromial artery

Palpation

Deep to pectoralis major, below the coracoid process

Special Test

Wright's test (hyperabduction) for pulse/symptom reproduction; pectoralis minor length test for postural shortening

Referred Pain

Anterior chest with radiation into the ulnar forearm/hand when compressing the plexus

Clinical Pearl

Tight/hypertrophied pectoralis minor is a leading cause of neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome by compressing the brachial plexus beneath the coracoid — reproduced by the hyperabduction (Wright's) test

10

Serratus anterior

Origin

Outer surfaces of ribs 1–8/9

Insertion

Costal (anterior) surface of the medial scapular border

Nerve / Root

Long thoracic nerve (C5–C7)

Action

Scapular protraction and upward rotation; holds the scapula against the thoracic wall

Blood Supply

Lateral thoracic artery

Palpation

Lateral thoracic wall below the axilla, most easily felt during resisted protraction (a punching motion)

Special Test

Wall push-up test — medial scapular winging indicates serratus anterior weakness

Referred Pain

Lateral chest wall pain that can mimic rib pathology or pleurisy

Clinical Pearl

Long thoracic nerve injury (axillary node dissection, chest tube placement, or backpack palsy) causes classic medial winging worst with a wall push-up, distinct from trapezius winging (lateral/inferior winging at rest)

11

Rhomboid major / minor

Origin

Minor: C7–T1 spinous processes; Major: T2–T5 spinous processes

Insertion

Medial scapular border (minor above the spine, major below)

Nerve / Root

Dorsal scapular nerve (C4–C5)

Action

Scapular retraction and downward rotation

Blood Supply

Dorsal scapular artery

Palpation

Medial scapular border between the spine and scapula, deep to trapezius

Special Test

Resisted scapular retraction with hands on hips, elbows pulled back ("prayer/reverse-fly" position)

Referred Pain

Sharp, localized ache along the medial scapular border — one of the most common myofascial complaints in prolonged desk posture

Clinical Pearl

Dorsal scapular nerve runs through and can be compressed by the middle scalene — an underrecognized cause of interscapular burning pain in overhead athletes and desk workers

Upper Arm

4 muscles
01

Biceps brachii (long / short head)

Origin

Long head: supraglenoid tubercle; Short head: coracoid process

Insertion

Radial tuberosity + bicipital aponeurosis into deep forearm fascia

Nerve / Root

Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C6)

Action

Elbow flexion (strongest when forearm supinated) and forearm supination (its primary mechanical advantage — stronger supinator than pronator teres is a pronator)

Blood Supply

Brachial artery

Palpation

Anterior arm; tendon palpable in the antecubital fossa, lateral to the brachial artery

Special Test

Resisted elbow flexion with forearm supinated; Speed's test (resisted forward flexion with elbow extended, forearm supinated) for biceps tendinopathy/SLAP

Referred Pain

Anterior shoulder into the muscle belly — biceps tendinopathy pain often overlaps with subacromial impingement pain

Clinical Pearl

Distal biceps rupture produces the "reverse Popeye" deformity (bulge moves proximally); the hook test (inability to hook a finger under the intact distal tendon) is highly sensitive and specific

02

Triceps brachii (long / lateral / medial heads)

Origin

Long head: infraglenoid tubercle; Lateral head: posterior humerus above radial groove; Medial head: posterior humerus below radial groove

Insertion

Olecranon process of ulna

Nerve / Root

Radial nerve (C6–C8)

Action

Elbow extension (long head also assists shoulder extension/adduction)

Blood Supply

Deep brachial (profunda brachii) artery

Palpation

Posterior arm; tendon at the olecranon

Special Test

Resisted elbow extension from a flexed position; test wrist/finger extension to screen concurrent radial nerve injury with humeral shaft fractures

Referred Pain

Posterior elbow and forearm; a lateral head trigger point can refer pain over the lateral epicondyle, mimicking tennis elbow

Clinical Pearl

The radial nerve and profunda brachii artery run in the spiral (radial) groove directly against the humeral shaft between the lateral and medial heads — a midshaft humerus fracture here classically causes wrist drop (Holstein-Lewis pattern)

03

Brachialis

Origin

Distal anterior humerus

Insertion

Coronoid process and tuberosity of ulna

Nerve / Root

Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C6); a small lateral contribution from the radial nerve

Action

Pure elbow flexor regardless of forearm position — the true "workhorse" flexor, unlike biceps which is position-dependent

Blood Supply

Brachial artery

Palpation

Deep to biceps, palpable at the distal medial and lateral arm just above the elbow

Special Test

Resisted elbow flexion with the forearm pronated (minimizes biceps contribution)

Referred Pain

Deep anterior elbow ache, sometimes radiating to the base of the thumb

Clinical Pearl

Because brachialis inserts on the ulna (not the radius), it flexes the elbow with equal strength in pronation or supination — useful for distinguishing brachialis-dominant from biceps-dominant weakness on exam

04

Coracobrachialis

Origin

Coracoid process of scapula

Insertion

Medial mid-shaft of humerus

Nerve / Root

Musculocutaneous nerve (C5–C7)

Action

Shoulder flexion and adduction (weak, assistive)

Blood Supply

Brachial artery (muscular branches)

Palpation

Deep in the axilla, medial to the short head of biceps; difficult to isolate on palpation

Special Test

Not typically isolated on standard MMT — assessed as part of shoulder flexion/adduction

Referred Pain

Deltoid region and posterior arm along the triceps — an atypical, easily missed referral zone

Clinical Pearl

The musculocutaneous nerve pierces directly through coracobrachialis — a useful surgical/anatomic landmark and a rare site of nerve entrapment producing anterolateral forearm numbness

Forearm & Wrist

14 muscles
01

Pronator teres

Origin

Humeral head: medial epicondyle; Ulnar head: coronoid process

Insertion

Middle lateral radius

Nerve / Root

Median nerve (C6–C7)

Action

Forearm pronation; weak elbow flexion

Blood Supply

Ulnar recurrent artery

Palpation

Proximal-medial forearm, crossing obliquely from the medial epicondyle

Special Test

Resisted pronation with the elbow extended (removes brachioradialis contribution)

Referred Pain

Proximal volar forearm, can extend into the palm along the median distribution

Clinical Pearl

Median nerve passes between its two heads — pronator teres syndrome causes proximal median compression with volar forearm pain WORSE with resisted pronation, distinguishing it from carpal tunnel (worse at night/with wrist flexion)

02

Pronator quadratus

Origin

Distal anterior ulna

Insertion

Distal anterior radius

Nerve / Root

Anterior interosseous nerve, a branch of median (C7–C8)

Action

Prime mover of forearm pronation (acts at all elbow positions, unlike pronator teres)

Blood Supply

Anterior interosseous artery

Palpation

Deep, distal volar forearm just proximal to the wrist — not directly palpable

Special Test

Resisted pronation with the elbow fully flexed to neutralize pronator teres

Referred Pain

Deep distal forearm ache, poorly localized

Clinical Pearl

Anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) syndrome (pure motor) weakens pronator quadratus, FPL, and FDP to index/middle — tested with the classic "OK sign": patient cannot form a circle and instead makes a triangular pinch (Kiloh-Nevin sign)

03

Supinator

Origin

Lateral epicondyle, radial collateral and annular ligaments, supinator crest of ulna

Insertion

Proximal lateral radius

Nerve / Root

Posterior interosseous nerve (deep branch of radial, C5–C6)

Action

Forearm supination (with biceps for power; alone for unresisted supination)

Blood Supply

Radial recurrent artery

Palpation

Deep to the extensor muscle mass just distal to the lateral epicondyle — not well isolated

Special Test

Resisted supination with the elbow flexed 90° and adducted to the trunk (minimizes biceps)

Referred Pain

Lateral elbow and proximal forearm, a major mimic of lateral epicondylitis

Clinical Pearl

The posterior interosseous nerve pierces the supinator through the arcade of Frohse — radial tunnel syndrome/PIN entrapment here causes deep lateral forearm pain WITHOUT sensory loss (PIN is purely motor distal to the elbow), unlike lateral epicondylitis

04

Flexor carpi radialis (FCR)

Origin

Medial epicondyle (common flexor origin)

Insertion

Base of 2nd (and 3rd) metacarpal

Nerve / Root

Median nerve (C6–C7)

Action

Wrist flexion and radial deviation

Blood Supply

Radial artery

Palpation

Most radial of the volar wrist tendons; prominent with resisted wrist flexion

Special Test

Resisted wrist flexion with radial deviation

Referred Pain

Volar radial wrist, can extend into the thenar eminence

Clinical Pearl

FCR tendinopathy causes pain at the volar-radial wrist near the scaphoid tubercle, often confused with De Quervain's or scaphoid pathology — palpate the tendon in resisted flexion to differentiate

05

Flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU)

Origin

Humeral head: medial epicondyle; Ulnar head: olecranon/posterior ulna

Insertion

Pisiform, hook of hamate, base of 5th metacarpal

Nerve / Root

Ulnar nerve (C7–T1)

Action

Wrist flexion and ulnar deviation

Blood Supply

Ulnar artery

Palpation

Most ulnar of the volar wrist tendons, inserting on the pisiform

Special Test

Resisted wrist flexion with ulnar deviation; Tinel's sign at the cubital tunnel screens the adjacent ulnar nerve

Referred Pain

Volar ulnar wrist and into the hypothenar eminence

Clinical Pearl

The ulnar nerve runs directly deep to FCU through the cubital tunnel — this is the classic site of ulnar nerve entrapment (cubital tunnel syndrome), producing ring/small finger paresthesias worsened by elbow flexion

06

Palmaris longus

Origin

Medial epicondyle

Insertion

Palmar aponeurosis

Nerve / Root

Median nerve (C7–C8)

Action

Weak wrist flexion; tenses palmar fascia

Blood Supply

Ulnar artery (via the anterior ulnar recurrent)

Palpation

Most superficial and central volar wrist tendon — check for its presence with thumb-to-little-finger opposition and wrist flexion (Schaeffer's test)

Special Test

Not separately tested on standard MMT given frequent absence and redundancy with FCR/FCU

Referred Pain

Not a significant independent referred-pain generator

Clinical Pearl

Absent in ~10–15% of the population (unilateral or bilateral) with zero functional deficit — the most commonly harvested tendon graft (e.g., for UCL reconstruction, "Tommy John" surgery) precisely because it's expendable

07

Flexor digitorum superficialis (FDS)

Origin

Medial epicondyle, coronoid process, proximal radius

Insertion

Sides of middle phalanges, digits 2–5

Nerve / Root

Median nerve (C7–T1)

Action

Flexes the PIP joints (and secondarily MCP/wrist); each finger can be tested in isolation

Blood Supply

Ulnar artery

Palpation

Volar forearm, deep to the superficial wrist flexors

Special Test

Isolate by holding adjacent fingers extended (blocks FDP) and asking the patient to flex only the tested finger's PIP joint

Referred Pain

Diffuse volar forearm; not classically a distinct referral pattern

Clinical Pearl

Zone II flexor tendon lacerations ("no man's land," A1–A4 pulleys) involve both FDS and FDP in a tight fibro-osseous sheath — repairs here have the highest adhesion/re-rupture risk in hand surgery

08

Flexor digitorum profundus (FDP)

Origin

Proximal 3/4 of anterior/medial ulna and interosseous membrane

Insertion

Base of distal phalanges, digits 2–5

Nerve / Root

Radial half (index/middle): anterior interosseous (median, C8–T1); Ulnar half (ring/small): ulnar nerve (C8–T1)

Action

Flexes DIP joints (only muscle that can) and secondarily PIP/MCP/wrist

Blood Supply

Ulnar and anterior interosseous arteries

Palpation

Deep volar forearm, beneath FDS

Special Test

Isolate by holding the PIP joint extended and asking the patient to flex the DIP joint

Referred Pain

Deep volar forearm ache when strained

Clinical Pearl

"Jersey finger" is FDP avulsion from the distal phalanx, classically the ring finger during a grasp-and-pull mechanism (e.g., grabbing a jersey) — inability to flex the DIP joint in isolation is diagnostic

09

Flexor pollicis longus (FPL)

Origin

Anterior radius and interosseous membrane

Insertion

Base of distal phalanx of thumb

Nerve / Root

Anterior interosseous nerve (median, C8–T1)

Action

Flexes the thumb IP joint (only muscle that can) and MCP joint

Blood Supply

Anterior interosseous artery

Palpation

Radial volar forearm, deep, difficult to isolate by palpation

Special Test

Resisted flexion of the thumb IP joint while stabilizing the MCP joint

Referred Pain

Radial-volar forearm; overlaps with De Quervain's referral zone at the wrist

Clinical Pearl

Weakened in AIN syndrome along with FDP (index/middle) and pronator quadratus — the OK sign test directly assesses FPL

10

Extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL) / brevis (ECRB)

Origin

ECRL: lateral supracondylar ridge; ECRB: lateral epicondyle (common extensor origin)

Insertion

Base of 2nd metacarpal (ECRL), base of 3rd metacarpal (ECRB)

Nerve / Root

Radial nerve (ECRL) and posterior interosseous nerve (ECRB) — both C6–C7

Action

Wrist extension and radial deviation

Blood Supply

Radial recurrent artery

Palpation

Dorsoradial forearm, distal to the lateral epicondyle

Special Test

Resisted wrist extension with radial deviation, elbow extended, reproduces lateral epicondylar pain if pathologic

Referred Pain

Lateral epicondyle radiating down the dorsal forearm to the wrist

Clinical Pearl

ECRB is the primary tendon involved in lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow") — repetitive eccentric overload produces angiofibroblastic tendinosis (degenerative, not primarily inflammatory) at its origin

11

Extensor carpi ulnaris (ECU)

Origin

Lateral epicondyle and posterior ulna

Insertion

Base of 5th metacarpal

Nerve / Root

Posterior interosseous nerve (C7–C8)

Action

Wrist extension and ulnar deviation

Blood Supply

Posterior interosseous artery

Palpation

Dorsal-ulnar wrist, distal to the ulnar head

Special Test

Resisted wrist extension with ulnar deviation; provocative forearm supination/pronation with wrist flexion reproduces subluxation

Referred Pain

Dorsal-ulnar wrist, can mimic TFCC pathology

Clinical Pearl

Runs in its own subsheath (6th dorsal compartment) — subluxation out of the ulnar groove (ECU instability) causes a painful snapping at the dorsal-ulnar wrist with forearm rotation, common in racquet/golf athletes

12

Extensor digitorum (ED) / Extensor digiti minimi (EDM) / Extensor indicis (EI)

Origin

Lateral epicondyle (common extensor origin); EI also from posterior ulna

Insertion

Loss of intrinsic function (e.g., from diabetic peripheral neuropathy affecting the small distal nerve branches first) produces claw toes — unopposed long extensor/flexor pull with MTP hyperextension and IP flexion, a major contributor to diabetic forefoot ulceration over the metatarsal heads

07

Flexor hallucis brevis / Adductor hallucis

Origin

FHB: cuboid/lateral cuneiform; Adductor hallucis: metatarsal bases (oblique head) and MTP ligaments (transverse head)

Insertion

Base of proximal phalanx of great toe (both sides, via medial and lateral sesamoids for FHB)

Nerve / Root

FHB: medial plantar nerve; Adductor hallucis: lateral plantar nerve (S1–S2)

Action

Great toe MTP flexion (FHB, working through the sesamoid complex); great toe adduction (adductor hallucis, a deforming force in hallux valgus)

Blood Supply

Plantar metatarsal arteries

Palpation

Plantar surface of the 1st metatarsal head (sesamoids) and the first web space (adductor)

Special Test

Resisted great toe MTP flexion; assess hallux valgus angle for adductor hallucis contracture

Referred Pain

Plantar great toe/1st MTP; can mimic gout of the great toe

Clinical Pearl

The medial and lateral sesamoids embedded in FHB tendons absorb enormous first-ray push-off load — sesamoiditis or a sesamoid stress fracture presents as focal plantar 1st MTP pain, and adductor hallucis tightness is a key deforming contributor in progressive hallux valgus (bunion)

Movement Pathway Tracer

For each functional movement, the ordered sequence: where the signal originates, which nerve carries it, which muscle fires first, and what fires next — the same order you'd walk through to localize a weakness on exam.

01

Ankle Dorsiflexion

Deep fibular (peroneal) nerve — L4, L5
  1. 1

    Corticospinal signal descends from the motor cortex (leg area, medial precentral gyrus)

  2. 2

    Synapses onto L4–L5 anterior horn cells in the lumbar spinal cord

  3. 3

    Exits via the L4–L5 nerve roots → forms the common fibular (peroneal) nerve within the sciatic nerve

  4. 4

    Common fibular nerve wraps around the fibular neck (most vulnerable compression point in the body) → becomes the deep fibular nerve

  5. 5

    Deep fibular nerve enters the anterior compartment, innervating tibialis anterior FIRST (primary mover)

  6. 6

    Simultaneous/near-simultaneous activation of extensor digitorum longus, extensor hallucis longus, and peroneus tertius (synergists) via the same nerve

  7. 7

    Tibialis anterior contracts concentrically, pulling the foot into dorsiflexion and slight inversion while the toe extensors assist and prevent toe drag

02

Ankle Plantarflexion

Tibial nerve — S1, S2
  1. 1

    Motor cortex signal descends the corticospinal tract to S1–S2 anterior horn cells

  2. 2

    S1–S2 roots combine into the tibial division of the sciatic nerve in the posterior thigh

  3. 3

    Tibial nerve continues through the popliteal fossa into the posterior (deep and superficial) leg compartments

  4. 4

    Innervates gastrocnemius and soleus (the prime-mover "triceps surae") — soleus fires first/tonically for postural control, gastrocnemius adds explosive power

  5. 5

    Deep posterior compartment synergists (tibialis posterior, FDL, FHL) co-activate to stabilize the arch and toes during push-off

  6. 6

    Triceps surae contracts, pulling the calcaneus upward via the Achilles tendon and driving the foot into plantarflexion for toe-off

03

Overhead Throwing Motion (Baseball/Football)

Multiple: axillary/suprascapular (C5-C6) in cocking, thoracodorsal/medial+lateral pectoral (C6-T1) in acceleration, radial (C6-C8) for elbow extension, median/ulnar (C6-T1) for wrist/forearm at release
  1. 1

    Late cocking: legs/trunk generate force first ("kinetic chain" — the arm is the last and fastest-moving link) → scapula retracts/stabilizes via serratus anterior + rhomboids

  2. 2

    Posterior deltoid + infraspinatus + teres minor (axillary + suprascapular nerves, C5–C6) externally rotate and abduct the humerus to maximal cocking (~170° of external rotation in elite pitchers) — this phase places peak anterior capsule/labral (SLAP) and UCL stress

  3. 3

    Acceleration: rapid reversal — pectoralis major, latissimus dorsi, and subscapularis (medial/lateral pectoral C5-T1, thoracodorsal C6-C8, lower subscapular C5-C7) fire explosively to internally rotate the humerus — among the fastest joint rotations recorded in human movement (>7000°/sec at the shoulder)

  4. 4

    Simultaneously, the elbow whips into extension via triceps brachii (radial nerve, C6-C8), placing peak valgus stress on the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) — the mechanism behind "Tommy John" injuries

  5. 5

    Ball release: wrist flexors/pronators (median/ulnar nerves, C6-T1) snap the wrist and pronate the forearm for release accuracy and spin

  6. 6

    Deceleration/follow-through: posterior cuff (infraspinatus, teres minor) and posterior deltoid eccentrically brake the arm — this eccentric deceleration phase is a major site of posterior cuff overuse injury in high-volume throwers

04

Non-Contact ACL Injury Mechanism

Femoral nerve (L2-L4) drives quad-dominant landing; deficient hip abductor control reflects superior gluteal nerve (L4-S1) - mediated neuromuscular control, not a focal nerve lesion
  1. 1

    Athlete decelerates from a jump or cut with the knee near full extension (0–30° of flexion) — the position of least hamstring mechanical advantage and greatest ACL strain

  2. 2

    Quadriceps fires dominantly (femoral nerve, L2-L4) to control landing/deceleration forces, generating a strong anterior tibial shear force via the patellar tendon's angle of pull

  3. 3

    Hamstring co-contraction (which normally opposes anterior tibial translation) is neuromuscularly insufficient or delayed — often due to fatigue, poor pre-activation, or trunk/hip control deficits

  4. 4

    Weak hip abductor/external rotator control (gluteus medius, superior gluteal nerve L4-S1) allows the femur to adduct and internally rotate relative to a planted foot → dynamic knee valgus ("knee-in, toe-out" collapse)

  5. 5

    Combined anterior tibial shear + valgus + tibial internal rotation loads the ACL beyond its failure tensile strength, typically with an audible pop and immediate hemarthrosis

05

"OK Sign" (Thumb–Index Pinch)

Anterior interosseous nerve (AIN), a branch of the median nerve — C7, C8, T1
  1. 1

    Median nerve descends the anterior forearm and gives off the anterior interosseous nerve (AIN) just distal to the elbow, deep to pronator teres

  2. 2

    AIN travels along the interosseous membrane innervating (in order along its course) pronator quadratus, flexor pollicis longus, and the radial half of flexor digitorum profundus (index + middle)

  3. 3

    Flexor pollicis longus contracts, flexing the thumb IP joint

  4. 4

    Flexor digitorum profundus (index slip) contracts, flexing the index DIP joint

  5. 5

    Both tips meet to form a rounded "O" — a normal, circular OK sign

06

Power Grip / Grip Strength

Median nerve (C6-T1) for FDS/FDP radial half + thenar; Ulnar nerve (C8-T1) for FDP ulnar half + intrinsics + hypothenar; Radial nerve (C6-C8) for wrist extensor stabilization
  1. 1

    Wrist extensors (radial nerve, C6-C8) fire FIRST and continuously to stabilize the wrist in slight extension — this pre-positioning lengthens the finger flexors over the wrist for maximum tension (length-tension relationship), which is why grip strength drops sharply with the wrist flexed

  2. 2

    FDS (median nerve) flexes the PIP joints, providing the bulk of early grip force generation

  3. 3

    FDP (median nerve — radial half; ulnar nerve — ulnar half) flexes the DIP joints, completing full digital flexion around the object

  4. 4

    Intrinsic muscles (lumbricals + interossei, median/ulnar nerves) fine-tune MCP flexion and prevent the extrinsic flexors from causing an inefficient claw-like grip pattern

  5. 5

    Thenar and hypothenar muscles (median nerve for thenar; ulnar nerve for hypothenar) cup the palm around the object, and adductor pollicis (ulnar nerve) reinforces thumb-side closure for maximal force

07

Shoulder Abduction (Overhead Reach)

Suprascapular nerve (C5-C6) → Axillary nerve (C5-C6) → Spinal accessory/CN XI + long thoracic (C5-C7)
  1. 1

    Supraspinatus (suprascapular nerve, C5-C6) initiates the first 0-15° of abduction and provides a compressive force that seats the humeral head in the glenoid

  2. 2

    Deltoid (axillary nerve, C5-C6) takes over as the prime mover from roughly 15-90°, aided by continued rotator cuff centering to prevent superior humeral head migration

  3. 3

    From 90-180°, the scapula itself must rotate upward for the arm to continue rising — trapezius (CN XI) and serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve, C5-C7) drive this scapulothoracic rotation in a fixed ~2:1 glenohumeral-to-scapulothoracic ratio (scapulohumeral rhythm)

  4. 4

    Full overhead abduction is impossible without this final scapular contribution, even with a fully intact deltoid and cuff

08

Hip Flexion (Sprint Drive / Knee Drive)

Lumbar plexus direct branches (L1-L3) for psoas; Femoral nerve (L2-L4) for iliacus/rectus femoris/sartorius
  1. 1

    Psoas major (direct lumbar plexus branches, L1-L3) and iliacus (femoral nerve, L2-L4) fire together as the iliopsoas — the dominant hip flexor, especially past 90° of flexion where the single-joint hip flexors have the greatest mechanical advantage

  2. 2

    Rectus femoris (femoral nerve, L2-L4) contributes hip flexion torque up to about 90°, but loses mechanical advantage beyond that as it simultaneously shortens over the flexing knee (active insufficiency)

  3. 3

    Sartorius and TFL (femoral and superior gluteal nerves) provide secondary assistance and help control the accompanying slight abduction/rotation of the swinging leg

09

Knee Extension (Kicking Motion)

Femoral nerve — L2, L3, L4
  1. 1

    Femoral nerve (L2-L4) innervates all four quadriceps heads essentially simultaneously

  2. 2

    Vastus intermedius and vastus lateralis/medialis provide pure knee extension torque regardless of hip position

  3. 3

    Rectus femoris adds extra extension torque specifically when the hip is extending at the same time (as in the follow-through of a kick), because its two-joint action lets it transfer power efficiently between the hip and knee

  4. 4

    The patella acts as a mechanical pulley, increasing the quadriceps tendon's lever arm through the terminal 30° of extension — this is why patellar pathology (chondromalacia, patellar tendinopathy) preferentially causes pain in this terminal-extension range

10

Forearm Pronation/Supination (Turning a Screwdriver)

Musculocutaneous (C5-C6, biceps) + Posterior interosseous (C5-C6, supinator) for supination; Median nerve (C6-T1) for both pronators
  1. 1

    Supination sequence: biceps brachii (musculocutaneous nerve, C5-C6) provides the POWERFUL supination torque (especially with the elbow flexed — the mechanical basis for driving a screw with the dominant right arm clockwise, exploiting biceps power) while supinator (posterior interosseous nerve, C5-C6) provides fine, unresisted supination at any elbow angle

  2. 2

    Pronation sequence: pronator teres (median nerve, C6-C7) initiates and dominates against resistance near full elbow extension, while pronator quadratus (anterior interosseous nerve, C7-C8) is the true prime mover across the FULL range and at all elbow angles, since it acts purely at the distal radioulnar joint

  3. 3

    Brachioradialis (radial nerve, C5-C6) assists returning the forearm to neutral (mid-prone) from either full pronation or full supination, exploiting its resting length advantage from the extreme positions

11

Neck Rotation

Spinal accessory (CN XI) + C2-C3 for SCM; Dorsal rami of cervical nerves for splenius/suboccipitals
  1. 1

    To rotate the head toward the RIGHT: the LEFT sternocleidomastoid (CN XI + C2-C3) contracts, pulling the mastoid process across and around toward the sternum, which turns the face to the right

  2. 2

    Simultaneously, the RIGHT splenius capitis/cervicis (dorsal rami of middle cervical nerves) contracts, pulling the same-side mastoid/nuchal line posteriorly — an ipsilateral (same-side) rotator working synergistically with the contralateral SCM

  3. 3

    The suboccipital group, especially obliquus capitis inferior (suboccipital nerve, dorsal ramus C1), fine-tunes the terminal rotation specifically at the atlanto-axial joint, which contributes roughly 50% of total cervical rotation range

  4. 4

    Roughly half the total ~80-90° of head rotation occurs at this single C1-C2 joint; the remaining rotation is distributed across the C2-C7 segments

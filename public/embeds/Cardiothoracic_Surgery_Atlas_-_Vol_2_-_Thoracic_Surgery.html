01 / Thoracic Surgery

Pneumothorax & Chest Tubes

The 2023 British Thoracic Society guideline is a genuine paradigm shift — read this even if pneumothorax management feels like settled knowledge from your training.

Tension Physiology (animated)

A one-way valve mechanism lets air enter the pleural space on inspiration but prevents its escape — pressure accumulates, the mediastinum shifts, venous return falls, and obstructive shock follows.
Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis

Hypotension, tracheal deviation, absent breath sounds, and distended neck veins in the right clinical context (trauma, positive-pressure ventilation, recent central line attempt) should prompt immediate needle or finger thoracostomy decompression — do not wait for a chest X-ray to confirm what the clinical picture already tells you. The time cost of imaging confirmation is exactly the time that can't be afforded in true tension physiology.

The 2023 BTS Paradigm Shift

The headline change: pneumothorax size is no longer the deciding factor for whether to intervene — symptom burden is. A large but minimally symptomatic primary spontaneous pneumothorax can now reasonably be managed conservatively.

What This Means in Practice

Conservative management, regardless of size

An asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic primary spontaneous pneumothorax — even a large one — can be observed rather than automatically drained, provided appropriate follow-up is arranged. This directly overturns older teaching that used a fixed size threshold (commonly 2-3cm) as an automatic trigger for intervention.

Ambulatory management as a first-line option

For symptomatic primary spontaneous pneumothorax in patients with good support and access to follow-up, a one-way ambulatory valve device (allowing the patient to be treated and often discharged rather than admitted for traditional tube drainage) should be considered as initial therapy — a meaningfully different default than "everyone symptomatic gets an admission and a chest tube."

Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax is still handled more cautiously

Underlying lung disease (COPD, interstitial lung disease) means less physiologic reserve — these patients generally still warrant a lower threshold for intervention than the primary spontaneous pneumothorax pathway above, and chemical pleurodesis is a reasonable consideration to prevent recurrence in patients who've already decompensated significantly from a secondary pneumothorax.

Chest Tube Basics Worth Reinforcing

Size still matters for procedural safety even though it no longer independently dictates whether to intervene — a very large pneumothorax changes insertion technique considerations and raises awareness for re-expansion pulmonary edema risk with rapid large-volume drainage. Standard triangle-of-safety landmarks (5th intercostal space, anterior to mid-axillary line) remain the standard insertion site regardless of the size-vs-symptom management question above.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Immediate intervention
Any tension physiology — needle/finger decompression now, do not wait for confirmation or transfer.
Consult now
Thoracic surgery/pulmonology for any pneumothorax requiring drainage, persistent air leak, or recurrent pneumothorax being considered for pleurodesis or surgical intervention.
Transfer if no thoracic surgery on-site
Any pneumothorax with a persistent air leak beyond several days, recurrent pneumothorax, or secondary spontaneous pneumothorax in a patient with limited pulmonary reserve who may need surgical intervention.
Transport considerations
Any patient being transported with a chest tube in place needs a functioning, properly secured drainage system throughout transport, with a plan for what to do if the system is disrupted en route; needle decompression equipment should be immediately available for any patient at risk of tension physiology during transport.
Who to call
Thoracic surgery for procedural intervention beyond simple tube drainage, or for any persistent/complex air leak; pulmonology for ambulatory valve management and outpatient follow-up planning.
02 / Thoracic Surgery

Esophageal Emergencies

Mortality in esophageal perforation is driven almost entirely by time to diagnosis — this is a condition where the differential needs to come to mind early, since the classic triad often doesn't fully present.

Boerhaave Syndrome vs. Iatrogenic Perforation

Both mechanisms produce the same downstream emergency — esophageal contents contaminating the mediastinum — but differ in typical location, mechanism, and how quickly they're usually recognized.
Don't wait for Mackler's triad

Vomiting, chest pain, and subcutaneous emphysema together are present in only a minority of Boerhaave syndrome cases at presentation. A patient with severe chest/epigastric pain after forceful vomiting or retching deserves esophageal perforation on the differential even without the full triad — waiting for subcutaneous emphysema to develop before considering the diagnosis costs valuable time in a condition where every hour of delay measurably worsens outcomes.

Expandable: Working Through Suspected Perforation

Step 1 — Clinical suspicion based on history and exam

Severe chest/epigastric/back pain after vomiting, retching, forceful straining, or recent esophageal instrumentation should trigger this diagnosis as a real consideration, not an exotic afterthought. Physical exam findings (subcutaneous emphysema, Hamman's crunch — a crunching sound synchronous with heartbeat from mediastinal air) support but their absence doesn't exclude the diagnosis.

Step 2 — CT chest/abdomen with oral contrast

First-line imaging in most centers — identifies extraluminal air, fluid collections, mediastinal widening, and often the contrast leak itself. Faster and more readily available than a formal esophagram in most emergency settings, and provides additional information about the surrounding mediastinum and pleural spaces.

Step 3 — Esophagram if CT is equivocal or precise localization is needed

A water-soluble contrast swallow study (not barium, which causes significant mediastinal inflammation if it extravasates) can precisely localize the leak site — useful information for surgical or endoscopic planning even after CT has established the diagnosis.

Step 4 — Stratify management by leak characteristics and stability

A small, well-contained leak in a clinically stable patient (increasingly the case with earlier iatrogenic recognition during endoscopy) may be managed with endoscopic therapy — covered stent placement or endoscopic vacuum therapy — plus adequate drainage and broad-spectrum antibiotics, avoiding open surgery entirely. A larger, uncontained leak, or any hemodynamically unstable patient, needs surgical repair, typically with wide mediastinal drainage regardless of whether primary repair is feasible.

Broad-spectrum coverage from the start

Esophageal perforation contaminates the mediastinum with a mixed oral/gastric flora — broad-spectrum antibiotics covering gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms (plus antifungal coverage in many protocols, given the frequency of Candida in esophageal flora) should start empirically as soon as the diagnosis is suspected, not held pending culture results or definitive source control.

Anastomotic Leaks — A Related But Distinct Problem

Post-esophagectomy anastomotic leaks share the same "contamination of the mediastinum/pleural space" endpoint but occur in a different clinical context (recent major surgery, often with surrounding fibrosis and altered anatomy from the reconstruction). Similar diagnostic principles apply (CT with oral contrast, esophagram), but management planning benefits enormously from direct involvement of the original surgical team given the altered post-esophagectomy anatomy.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Thoracic surgery emergently for any confirmed or strongly suspected esophageal perforation — time to source control is the single biggest modifiable factor in outcome.
ICU admission
Any confirmed perforation, given the risk of rapid progression to mediastinitis and sepsis, regardless of initial management approach (endoscopic vs. surgical).
Transfer if no thoracic surgery/advanced endoscopy on-site
Any suspected esophageal perforation at a facility without thoracic surgical capability — this is a true surgical emergency and should be transferred with the same urgency as other immediately life-threatening thoracic conditions.
Transport considerations
NPO status maintained strictly during transport; broad-spectrum antibiotics started before or during transport, not delayed until arrival; hemodynamic monitoring given the risk of rapid septic decompensation.
Who to call
Thoracic surgery directly and urgently; gastroenterology in parallel if endoscopic stent/vacuum therapy is being considered as the management approach.
03 / Thoracic Surgery

Mediastinal Disease

Compartment location is the organizing principle for the mediastinal mass differential — and mediastinitis is the acute emergency that ties this section to two others in this atlas series.

The Three Compartments

The "4 Ts" mnemonic organizes the anterior compartment differential; middle and posterior compartments have their own characteristic lesions driven by the structures that actually live there.
Thymoma and myasthenia gravis — a two-way relationship worth remembering

Roughly 10-15% of myasthenia gravis patients have a thymoma, and conversely a meaningful proportion of thymoma patients have or will develop myasthenia gravis — any newly identified anterior mediastinal mass consistent with thymoma warrants at least screening questions about fatigable weakness, ptosis, or diplopia, since the finding changes both surgical planning (anesthesia considerations in myasthenic patients) and long-term follow-up.

Neurogenic Tumors — The Posterior Compartment Default

Schwannomas, neurofibromas, and ganglioneuromas arising from the sympathetic chain or intercostal nerves make up the majority of posterior mediastinal masses. Most are benign and slow-growing, often discovered incidentally on imaging obtained for unrelated reasons — but any posterior mediastinal mass still warrants tissue characterization, since malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors and metastatic disease remain on the differential.

Mediastinitis

This is the section's acute emergency, and it connects directly to two other parts of this atlas series: post-sternotomy mediastinitis (see Vol. 1's Post-Cardiac Surgery Complications) and esophageal-perforation-associated mediastinitis (see the prior section in this volume). A third, less common pathway — descending necrotizing mediastinitis — deserves its own attention here.

Descending necrotizing mediastinitis starts in the mouth

A deep neck or dental infection (Ludwig's angina, retropharyngeal abscess, odontogenic infection) can track along cervical fascial planes directly into the mediastinum — a rapidly progressive, high-mortality infection that's easy to underestimate if the initial presentation looks like "just" a dental or neck infection. Any deep neck space infection with systemic toxicity, chest pain, or imaging findings suggesting mediastinal extension needs emergent surgical involvement (typically both ENT/oral surgery for the primary source and thoracic surgery for mediastinal drainage) — this is not a condition for antibiotics and observation alone.

Surgical debridement, not just antibiotics

Regardless of the underlying cause (post-surgical, esophageal, or descending infection), mediastinitis requires aggressive surgical debridement of infected/necrotic tissue alongside broad-spectrum antibiotics — this is one of the clearest examples in thoracic surgery where source control is not optional, and antibiotic therapy alone without debridement carries very high mortality.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Thoracic surgery for any suspected mediastinitis regardless of source; cardiac surgery specifically if post-sternotomy; ENT/oral surgery in parallel for descending necrotizing mediastinitis given the primary neck source.
Emergent evaluation
Any deep neck infection with systemic toxicity or chest symptoms — treat as potential descending mediastinitis until proven otherwise given how rapidly this can progress.
ICU admission
Any confirmed mediastinitis given sepsis risk and the need for close monitoring around surgical debridement.
Transfer if no thoracic surgery on-site
Any mediastinitis requiring surgical debridement capability not available locally — emergent transfer given the mortality risk of delayed source control.
Who to call
Thoracic surgery for mediastinal drainage/debridement; ENT/oral surgery for descending necrotizing mediastinitis source control; infectious disease for antibiotic optimization in complex or resistant organisms.

Pulmonary contusion, not the flail segment, usually drives respiratory failure

The paradoxical motion of a flail chest segment looks dramatic, but the underlying pulmonary contusion (the lung tissue injury beneath the fractured ribs) is typically the actual driver of hypoxemia and respiratory failure — management focuses on pain control (enabling adequate ventilation and cough), pulmonary toilet, and judicious fluid management to avoid worsening the contusion, rather than fixating on stabilizing the flail segment mechanically in most cases.

Blunt Cardiac Injury Workup

A normal ECG plus a normal troponin has a high negative predictive value for clinically significant blunt cardiac injury — this combination reasonably excludes the need for further cardiac-specific workup in most patients. An abnormal ECG or elevated troponin, or hemodynamic instability without another clear source, warrants echocardiography to evaluate for wall motion abnormalities, valve injury, or pericardial effusion.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Immediate intervention
Any immediately life-threatening finding from the checklist above gets addressed in the primary survey itself — needle/tube decompression, occlusive dressing, or pericardiocentesis as indicated, before further workup.
Consult now
Trauma surgery and thoracic surgery jointly for any significant thoracic trauma; cardiothoracic surgery specifically for suspected tracheobronchial injury, traumatic aortic injury, or significant blunt cardiac injury.
Emergent evaluation
Any suspected tracheobronchial injury (bronchoscopy) or traumatic aortic injury (CT angiography) — both carry high mortality if the diagnosis is delayed.
Transfer if no thoracic/trauma surgery on-site
Any of the immediately life-threatening or easily-missed injuries above requiring surgical capability not available locally — stabilize what can be stabilized locally (tube thoracostomy, occlusive dressing) and transfer emergently with clear communication of findings.
Transport considerations
Continuous monitoring for any thoracic trauma patient given the risk of evolving tension physiology or hemodynamic decompensation en route; ensure any chest tube in place has a properly functioning, secured drainage system for the duration of transport.
Who to call
Trauma surgery as the primary contact, with cardiothoracic/vascular surgery involved directly for the specific injury pattern (tracheobronchial, cardiac, or aortic) once identified.

Lung Resection Complications

From the routine (persistent air leak) to the catastrophic (bronchopleural fistula) — and a late complication that only makes sense once you understand how much the mediastinum shifts after a pneumonectomy.

Bronchopleural Fistula & Post-Pneumonectomy Syndrome

Bronchopleural fistula is an early-to-intermediate complication with a dramatic presentation; post-pneumonectomy syndrome is a late complication that requires remembering a surgical history from months to years prior.
The positional-drainage clue for bronchopleural fistula

A classic (though not universal) presentation is sudden expectoration of serous or serosanguinous fluid when the patient changes position — fluid that has accumulated in the post-resection pleural space drains through the fistula into the airway and gets coughed up. This finding in a post-lobectomy or post-pneumonectomy patient should prompt urgent evaluation rather than being attributed to a more benign respiratory process.

Why Right Pneumonectomy Specifically Predisposes to Post-Pneumonectomy Syndrome

After a pneumonectomy, the remaining lung and mediastinum shift to fill the empty hemithorax over time. Following a right pneumonectomy, this shift and rotation can compress the distal trachea or left mainstem bronchus against the spine or aorta — the anatomy specifically predisposes right-sided resections to this compression pattern more than left-sided ones. The result is a slowly progressive airway obstruction that can take months to years to become symptomatic, which is exactly why the diagnosis is easy to miss if the surgical history isn't specifically elicited.

Think of it in anyone with unexplained dyspnea and a pneumonectomy history

New, progressive dyspnea or stridor in a patient with a pneumonectomy history — regardless of how long ago the surgery occurred — deserves consideration of post-pneumonectomy syndrome, particularly if the timeline doesn't fit better with more common explanations (recurrent malignancy, new cardiac disease, or a new pulmonary process). Cross-sectional imaging demonstrating the mediastinal shift and airway compression confirms the diagnosis.

Persistent Air Leak — The Everyday Problem

Far more common than either complication above: an air leak that persists beyond roughly 5-7 days after a lobectomy or wedge resection is the leading cause of prolonged chest tube duration and extended hospital stay following lung resection. Most eventually resolve with continued drainage, ambulation, and time as the visceral pleural defect heals. A genuinely refractory leak may be managed with chemical pleurodesis, endobronchial valve placement (blocking airflow to the leaking segment to allow it to seal), or surgical revision in persistent cases.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Thoracic surgery for any suspected bronchopleural fistula or post-pneumonectomy syndrome — both require surgical expertise for definitive diagnosis and management planning.
Emergent evaluation
Suspected bronchopleural fistula with any respiratory compromise or signs of empyema/mediastinal contamination — this can progress to a life-threatening infectious process quickly.
Urgent evaluation
New or progressive dyspnea/stridor in any patient with a pneumonectomy history, regardless of how remote the surgery — don't let time since surgery lower your suspicion for post-pneumonectomy syndrome.
Transfer if no thoracic surgery on-site
Confirmed or strongly suspected bronchopleural fistula, or post-pneumonectomy syndrome requiring surgical mediastinal repositioning.
Who to call
Thoracic surgery directly — ideally the original surgical team if available, given their specific knowledge of the original resection and any technical factors relevant to the current complication.

Pleural Space Disease

Closing this volume with empyema's staged progression and a distinctive milky effusion that points to one specific injury — the thoracic duct.

Empyema Staging

Each stage narrows toward the same endpoint (infected pleural fluid) but requires progressively more invasive management as fibrin deposition and eventual organization make simple drainage insufficient.
Catching the transition from Stage 1 to Stage 2 matters

The window for successful treatment with antibiotics and simple drainage alone narrows considerably once loculations form (Stage 2) — this is why parapneumonic effusions of any significant size generally warrant sampling rather than watchful waiting, since identifying and draining a Stage 1 exudative effusion early can prevent progression to the more invasive management Stage 2-3 disease requires.

Light's Criteria — Getting the Basic Workup Right

Any new pleural effusion of unclear etiology should be sampled (thoracentesis) and classified using Light's criteria before assuming a systemic cause. An exudate (any one of: pleural/serum protein ratio >0.5, pleural/serum LDH ratio >0.6, or pleural fluid LDH >2/3 the upper limit of normal serum LDH) points toward a local process requiring its own workup — infection, malignancy, or an inflammatory condition — while a transudate points back toward a systemic driver (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) where the pleural fluid itself isn't the primary problem to chase.

Intrapleural Fibrinolytics and DNase

For Stage 2 (fibrinopurulent) empyema with loculations that impair adequate drainage, intrapleural instillation of a fibrinolytic (tPA) combined with DNase has become a well-established strategy to break down loculations and improve drainage without proceeding directly to surgery — this combination therapy has meaningfully reduced the proportion of empyema cases that ultimately require surgical decortication.

Chylothorax

A milky-appearing pleural effusion should immediately raise suspicion for chylothorax from thoracic duct injury — esophagectomy and other extensive mediastinal or thoracic surgery are the leading causes given the thoracic duct's course through this territory, though trauma and malignant lymphatic obstruction are also recognized causes.

Confirm with a triglyceride level, not just appearance

A pleural fluid triglyceride level above 110 mg/dL is diagnostic of chylothorax; levels below 50 mg/dL make it unlikely, and intermediate values warrant lipoprotein electrophoresis to look for chylomicrons directly. Don't rely on visual appearance alone — a milky effusion can occasionally represent a pseudochylothorax (cholesterol-rich fluid from a different chronic process) rather than true chyle, and the distinction changes the entire management approach.

Management escalation

Initial management is typically dietary — a medium-chain triglyceride diet (which bypasses lymphatic absorption) or complete bowel rest with total parenteral nutrition, allowing the thoracic duct injury time to heal spontaneously while drainage continues. A persistently high-output leak (thresholds vary by protocol, but sustained high volume despite conservative measures) that doesn't respond to dietary management within roughly 1-2 weeks warrants surgical thoracic duct ligation.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Thoracic surgery for any empyema requiring drainage beyond simple thoracentesis, or any confirmed chylothorax to establish a management plan.
Urgent evaluation
Any new significant pleural effusion in a septic or systemically unwell patient — sample promptly rather than deferring, since early identification of empyema changes the management trajectory substantially.
Transfer if no thoracic surgery on-site
Stage 2-3 empyema requiring intrapleural fibrinolytic/DNase therapy or surgical decortication capability not available locally; persistent high-output chylothorax requiring surgical duct ligation.
Transport considerations
Any patient with an active chest tube for empyema or chylothorax drainage needs a properly functioning, secured drainage system maintained throughout transport.
Who to call
Thoracic surgery for both empyema requiring advanced intervention and chylothorax requiring surgical management; interventional radiology as an alternative for image-guided drainage procedures depending on local practice patterns.

Foundations · Signaling Biology

Principles of Endocrine Signaling

The shared logic underneath every axis in this atlas: how hormones are classed, how they reach a receptor, how that receptor converts a signal into a cell response, and how the whole system holds itself in check.

01Modes of Chemical Signaling

Not everything a gland releases is "endocrine" in the strict sense. The distinction matters clinically — it's why a paracrine factor can have massive local effects with an undetectable serum level.

Endocrine

Secreted into blood, acts on distant target cells with the appropriate receptor. Classic hormones: insulin, cortisol, T4, PTH.

Paracrine

Diffuses locally to act on neighboring cells without entering systemic circulation. Example: somatostatin from islet delta cells suppressing adjacent alpha/beta cells; testicular testosterone acting on adjacent Sertoli cells at concentrations ~100x serum level.

Autocrine

Cell secretes a factor that acts on itself via its own surface receptors. Common in growth factor signaling and some cytokine loops (e.g., IL-2 on activated T cells — not endocrine per se but illustrates the principle used by IGF-1 in some tissues).

Intracrine

Hormone acts inside the same cell that produced it, without ever leaving the cell. Relevant to peripheral androgen synthesis — some tissues (prostate, skin) convert DHEA to potent androgens intracellularly, contributing to local androgenicity not reflected in serum testosterone.

03Receptor Mechanisms

G-Protein Coupled Receptors (GPCRs)

Seven-transmembrane receptors coupled to heterotrimeric G-proteins. The G-protein subtype determines the second messenger — this is the mechanistic reason drugs and toxins that hit one pathway (cholera toxin locking Gs "on", pertussis toxin locking Gi "off") produce predictable syndromes.

Receptor Tyrosine Kinases (RTKs)

Ligand binding causes receptor dimerization and cross-autophosphorylation of intracellular tyrosine residues, creating docking sites for signaling proteins. Two major downstream arms:

  • PI3K/Akt arm — drives the metabolic effects: GLUT4 vesicle translocation, glycogen synthase activation, lipogenesis. Dominant arm for insulin's metabolic actions.
  • Ras/MAPK (ERK) arm — drives mitogenic/growth effects: gene transcription, cell proliferation. Dominant arm for IGF-1 and for insulin's growth-promoting (rather than metabolic) actions.

Hormones using this mechanism: insulin, IGF-1. Growth hormone uses a related but distinct mechanism — a cytokine receptor (no intrinsic kinase activity) paired with associated JAK2, activating STAT5, which drives hepatic IGF-1 transcription.

Nuclear / Intracellular Hormone Receptors

Lipophilic hormones (steroids, thyroid hormone, vitamin D) cross the plasma membrane directly. Receptor location differs by hormone class and matters for kinetics:

  • Cytoplasmic receptors (glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid receptors) — held inactive by heat-shock protein (HSP90) chaperone complexes in the cytoplasm; hormone binding releases HSP90, exposes a nuclear localization signal, and the complex translocates to the nucleus.
  • Nuclear-resident receptors (thyroid hormone receptor, vitamin D receptor, retinoid receptors) — already bound to DNA response elements even in the unliganded state, often as a repressor complex with corepressors; hormone binding swaps corepressors for coactivators.

Both converge on binding a hormone response element (HRE) — a specific DNA sequence — as a receptor dimer (homodimer for steroid receptors; heterodimer with RXR for thyroid hormone receptor and vitamin D receptor), recruiting coactivator/corepressor complexes that remodel chromatin and alter transcription. This is why steroid and thyroid hormone effects take hours, not seconds: they require new protein synthesis.

Every axis in this atlas is a variation on the same control loop: hypothalamus → pituitary → peripheral gland → peripheral hormone, with feedback acting at one or more levels.

05Transport, Binding Proteins, and the Free Hormone Hypothesis

Lipophilic hormones can't travel free in aqueous plasma in clinically useful quantities — they're overwhelmingly protein-bound. Only the unbound (free) fraction is biologically active and available to enter cells; the bound fraction acts as a circulating reservoir that buffers against rapid swings and extends half-life.

Clinical takeaway
Whenever a binding protein concentration shifts (pregnancy, oral estrogen, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, critical illness), total hormone levels move even though the patient is physiologically euhormonal. Free hormone assays (free T4, free testosterone, calculated free cortisol) — or an understanding of which direction the binding protein moved — are what keep you from chasing a lab abnormality that isn't real.

06Clearance and Secretory Patterns

Metabolic clearance

Peptide hormones: primarily renal filtration and proteolysis (kidney handles a large share of insulin, PTH, GH clearance — renal failure prolongs their half-lives). Steroid/thyroid hormones: hepatic Phase I (hydroxylation, e.g., cytochrome P450) and Phase II (glucuronidation, sulfation) conjugation, biliary/urinary excretion, with some enterohepatic recirculation (relevant to estrogen).

Secretory patterns

Pulsatile — GnRH, LH, GH, cortisol (pulses superimposed on circadian rhythm). Pulse frequency, not just amount, carries information — GnRH pulse frequency determines LH vs FSH predominance. Circadian — cortisol (peak ~0600–0800, nadir ~midnight), TSH (mild nocturnal peak). Continuous non-pulsatile GnRH agonist exposure paradoxically suppresses LH/FSH by downregulating pituitary GnRH receptors — the basis for GnRH agonist therapy in prostate cancer and central precocious puberty.

Verify before relying
Reference ranges, half-life values, and receptor-subtype assignments above reflect standard physiology teaching (Guyton, Williams Textbook of Endocrinology–level consensus) and are stable core science, not shifting guideline content — but always confirm assay-specific reference ranges against your own lab before applying to a patient.

Why this single pathway explains so much pathology
Every classic congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) syndrome is a block at one named enzyme in this cascade. Knowing the pathway lets you predict the biochemistry of a CAH variant you've never memorized: whatever accumulates proximal to the block goes up, whatever is downstream goes down, and the clinical picture follows directly from which downstream hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, androgens) are deficient or excessive.

11β-Hydroxylase Deficiency CYP11B1 · 8q24
Autosomal recessive · ~5–8% of CAH

Block converting 11-deoxycortisol → cortisol and DOC → corticosterone. 11-deoxycorticosterone (DOC) accumulates — DOC has significant intrinsic mineralocorticoid activity, so unlike 21-hydroxylase deficiency, this variant causes hypertension and hypokalemia (from DOC-driven mineralocorticoid excess) rather than salt-wasting, alongside virilization from shunted androgen precursors.

3β-HSD2 Deficiency HSD3B2 · 1p12
Autosomal recessive · Rare

Blocks the Δ5→Δ4 conversion needed at multiple branch points (pregnenolone→progesterone, 17-OH-pregnenolone→17-OH-progesterone, DHEA→androstenedione). Both cortisol and aldosterone are deficient; because the block is before the potent androgen branch, males can be under-virilized (ambiguous genitalia) while females may be mildly virilized from accumulated DHEA (a weak androgen) — the reverse pattern from 21-hydroxylase deficiency.

StAR Deficiency (Lipoid CAH) STAR · 8p11
Autosomal recessive · Very rare, most severe form

Blocks cholesterol transport into the mitochondrion — the step before CYP11A1. Essentially no steroid hormones can be made by either adrenal or gonad. Cholesterol accumulates and becomes cytotoxic to steroidogenic cells over time, worsening the defect. Presents with severe salt-wasting adrenal crisis in infancy and, in genetic males, female-appearing external genitalia (absent fetal testosterone).

Cortisol and its inactive keto-metabolite cortisone are interconverted by two tissue-specific isoenzymes running in opposite directions — this local, pre-receptor regulation is as clinically important as circulating cortisol level itself.

Apparent Mineralocorticoid Excess
Glycyrrhizic acid (licorice, some chewing tobaccos) and the rare genetic 11β-HSD2 deficiency both inhibit 11β-HSD2. Cortisol then floods the mineralocorticoid receptor unopposed → hypertension, hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, with suppressed renin and aldosterone (distinguishing it from primary hyperaldosteronism, where aldosterone is high). Always ask about licorice/chewing tobacco use in a hypokalemic hypertensive patient with a suppressed renin-aldosterone panel.

Beyond the 11β-HSD shuttle, cortisol is ultimately cleared by hepatic reduction (5α/5β-reductase, 3α-HSD) to tetrahydrocortisol/tetrahydrocortisone, conjugated by glucuronidation, and excreted in urine — the basis of 24-hour urine free cortisol and urinary steroid profiling.

Cortisol secretion is pulsatile and layered on a strong circadian rhythm driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus: peak ~0600–0800, nadir ~midnight. This is why a single random cortisol is hard to interpret and why dynamic testing (dexamethasone suppression, ACTH stimulation) exists.

TSH is a heterodimeric glycoprotein sharing its α-subunit with LH, FSH, and hCG — only the β-subunit confers receptor specificity. This shared subunit is why, in rare TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas or severe primary hypothyroidism, extreme TRH drive can cross-stimulate the structurally related gonadotropin machinery, and why hCG (structurally closest to TSH) has weak intrinsic thyrotropic activity — relevant to gestational transient thyrotoxicosis and hyperemesis gravidarum, where very high hCG mildly stimulates the TSH receptor.

Somatostatin and dopamine both tonically inhibit TSH release — relevant when interpreting a low TSH in a critically ill patient on dopamine infusion or octreotide, independent of true thyroid status.

SynthesisFollicular Cell Machinery — Step by Step

Thyroid hormone synthesis is unique in the endocrine system: it's the only hormone stored in bulk extracellularly, in the follicular colloid, bound to a massive glycoprotein scaffold (thyroglobulin) — giving the thyroid a built-in 2–3 month hormone reserve.

  1. Iodide trapping — the follicular cell basolateral membrane sodium-iodide symporter (NIS) actively concentrates iodide 20–40x above plasma. This is the step blocked by perchlorate and exploited diagnostically/therapeutically by radioactive iodine uptake and ablation.
  2. Oxidation & organification — thyroid peroxidase (TPO), at the apical membrane, oxidizes iodide and attaches it to tyrosine residues on thyroglobulin, forming monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT). TPO is the target of Hashimoto's autoantibodies and the site of action of thionamide antithyroid drugs (methimazole, PTU).
  3. Coupling — TPO also catalyzes coupling of iodotyrosines: DIT + DIT → T4; MIT + DIT → T3. This explains the ~13:1 T4:T3 secretion ratio — coupling favors T4 formation.
  4. Storage — iodinated thyroglobulin is stored extracellularly in the follicular colloid.
  5. Endocytosis & proteolysis — TSH-stimulated pinocytosis retrieves colloid, lysosomal proteases cleave thyroglobulin, releasing free T4 and T3 into the bloodstream. MIT/DIT released in the process are recycled by dehalogenase (DEHAL1) to conserve iodide — a deficiency of this enzyme causes iodide wasting and goitrous hypothyroidism.
Wolff-Chaikoff Effect & Escape
A large acute iodide load transiently and completely shuts down organification (protective autoregulation, preventing hormone overproduction) — the Wolff-Chaikoff effect. Normal thyroids "escape" this block within ~10–14 days by downregulating NIS. Patients with underlying autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's) or prior partial thyroidectomy often fail to escape, developing iodine-induced hypothyroidism — relevant after iodinated contrast administration or amiodarone initiation. Amiodarone can also cause iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis (type I: iodine-induced excess synthesis in an abnormal gland; type II: destructive thyroiditis releasing preformed hormone) — the two require opposite management and are distinguished largely on clinical grounds and thyroidal blood flow on Doppler ultrasound.

ActivationPeripheral Conversion — the Deiodinase System

T4 is essentially a circulating prohormone. Tissue-level control over T3 availability — the true active hormone — happens locally, via three deiodinase enzymes that add or remove iodine atoms from specific ring positions.

Non-thyroidal illness syndrome
In acute severe illness, D1 activity falls and D3 activity rises — T4 is preferentially shunted to inactive reverse-T3 instead of active T3. Result: low T3, low-to-normal T4, normal or low TSH, and a high reverse-T3 — a physiologic adaptive response, not true hypothyroidism, and treating it with thyroid hormone replacement has not shown benefit in critical illness.

TransportPlasma Binding

>99% of circulating T4 and T3 is protein-bound (mostly TBG, plus transthyretin and albumin) — only the free fraction is active. T4's much higher binding affinity gives it a long half-life (~7 days) versus T3 (~1 day), which is why levothyroxine can be dosed once daily and why a missed dose or two rarely causes symptomatic swings.

Mutant thyroid hormone receptor-β has reduced T3 affinity, blunting negative feedback at the pituitary. Peripheral tissues respond variably depending on local receptor-β dependence, so patients are often clinically euthyroid despite the biochemical picture.

BreakdownHepatic Clearance

Beyond deiodination, thyroid hormone is inactivated by hepatic glucuronidation and sulfation, with biliary excretion and some enterohepatic recirculation. Drugs that induce hepatic glucuronidation (phenytoin, rifampin, carbamazepine) accelerate T4 clearance and can increase levothyroxine dosing requirements.

GnRH pulse frequency is itself a signal: fast pulses (~every 60–90 min) favor LH synthesis/release; slower pulses favor FSH. Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH receptor exposure — whether from a long-acting agonist or a disease process — paradoxically desensitizes pituitary GnRH receptors and shuts LH/FSH down. CompartmentsLeydig & Sertoli Cells — Two Separate Jobs

Leydig cells (interstitial)

LH target. Full steroidogenic machinery (StAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1, 3β-HSD, 17β-HSD3) → testosterone via the Δ5 pathway (dominant in testis): cholesterol → pregnenolone → 17-OH-pregnenolone → DHEA → androstenedione → testosterone.

Sertoli cells (within seminiferous tubules)

FSH target. No steroidogenesis of their own — instead: nurse spermatogenesis, make androgen-binding protein (ABP) to concentrate testosterone locally (intratubular testosterone ~50–100x serum level, required for spermatogenesis), secrete inhibin B (selective FSH suppression) and, in fetal life, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) (regresses Müllerian ducts).

This two-compartment design is why isolated FSH deficiency impairs fertility (spermatogenesis) with preserved libido/virilization (Leydig-driven testosterone intact), while isolated LH deficiency does the reverse.

Yes — men make meaningful estradiol from testosterone, and it's essential: estradiol (not testosterone) is the primary driver of epiphyseal closure and bone mineral density maintenance in males. This is why aromatase-deficient men have tall stature, unfused epiphyses, and low bone density despite normal-to-high testosterone.

TransportSHBG Binding

~44% of circulating testosterone is SHBG-bound, ~50% albumin-bound (loosely, considered part of the "bioavailable" fraction), and only ~2% is free. SHBG binds DHT > testosterone > estradiol in affinity. See the Foundational Principles section for the full SHBG regulation table — it's identical machinery shared with the female axis.

FeedbackNegative Feedback — Two Independent Arms

● Testosterone / estradiol ⊣ GnRH + LH ● Inhibin B ⊣ FSH (selectively)

This dual-arm design means LH and FSH can be suppressed independently: testosterone (and its aromatized estradiol) provides the dominant negative feedback on LH, while Sertoli-derived inhibin B independently and selectively suppresses FSH — which is exactly why a man with impaired spermatogenesis but intact Leydig function can show a selectively elevated FSH with normal LH and normal testosterone.

Exogenous testosterone / anabolic steroid use

Exogenous androgen (testosterone replacement, anabolic-androgenic steroids) suppresses hypothalamic GnRH and pituitary LH/FSH through the same long-loop negative feedback as endogenous testosterone — the axis cannot distinguish the source. Consequences: (1) endogenous testosterone production collapses (Leydig cells go quiescent from LH withdrawal — this is why men on TRT often have low intratesticular but normal-to-high serum testosterone), and (2) spermatogenesis is suppressed independently via FSH withdrawal and loss of the very high intratesticular testosterone concentration Sertoli cells require locally (serum-level testosterone from injection does not reconstitute the ~50–100x intratesticular gradient). This is the physiologic basis for exogenous-androgen-induced hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and infertility, and for using hCG (LH-receptor agonist) or selective agents rather than testosterone itself when fertility preservation matters. A deeper dive into androgen effects on insulin sensitivity and metabolic parameters is covered in the Volume 3 interaction matrix and clinical scenario cards.

SynthesisThe Two-Cell, Two-Gonadotropin Theory

Ovarian estradiol synthesis requires cooperation between two follicular cell types, each responding to a different gonadotropin — neither cell alone can complete the pathway.

Theca cells (LH target)

Full steroidogenic enzyme complement (StAR, CYP11A1, CYP17A1, 3β-HSD) but no aromatase. LH drives cholesterol → androstenedione (and some testosterone), which then diffuses across the basement membrane to granulosa cells.

Granulosa cells (FSH target)

Express aromatase (CYP19A1), FSH-induced, but lack CYP17A1 — cannot make androgens from scratch. They take theca-derived androstenedione and aromatize it to estrone, then 17β-HSD converts it to estradiol.

Why this matters clinically
This division of labor is exactly why PCOS biochemistry makes sense: chronically elevated LH (relative to FSH) over-drives theca androgen production, while relatively lower FSH limits granulosa aromatization capacity to convert it all to estrogen — net result is androgen excess. Insulin independently amplifies theca androgen synthesis and suppresses hepatic SHBG, both of which raise free/bioactive androgen further. The full PCOS mechanism and management framework is developed as a dedicated scenario card in Volume 3.

The SwitchWhere Negative Feedback Flips to Positive

Estradiol is the single clearest example of context-dependent feedback direction in the entire endocrine system.

Estradiol levelFeedback directionResult
Low-to-moderate, early-mid follicular phaseNEGATIVESuppresses GnRH pulse amplitude and pituitary LH/FSH sensitivity — keeps the cycle from firing early

The threshold-and-duration requirement (high enough, for long enough) is a safety mechanism — it's why exogenous estrogen given briefly or at moderate doses (early combined oral contraceptives, for instance) doesn't trigger a surge, while it's also the physiologic vulnerability that ovulation-induction protocols exploit deliberately.

Luteal PhaseCorpus Luteum & Progesterone

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle's granulosa and theca cells luteinize under LH support, gaining full CYP11A1/3β-HSD steroidogenic capacity but relatively limited CYP17A1 activity — so the corpus luteum's dominant secretory product is progesterone, not androgen or estrogen (though it makes meaningful estradiol too). Without fertilization, the corpus luteum regresses (luteolysis) at ~14 days independent of cycle length, progesterone falls, and withdrawal triggers menses. With fertilization, hCG (structurally LH-like, binds the same LH/hCG receptor) rescues the corpus luteum and sustains progesterone production until the placenta takes over at ~8–10 weeks.

BreakdownHepatic Metabolism & the 2-OH/16α-OH Fork

Estrogens are conjugated hepatically (glucuronidation, sulfation) with meaningful enterohepatic recirculation — gut bacterial β-glucuronidase can deconjugate excreted estrogen, allowing reabsorption (relevant to why broad-spectrum antibiotics can occasionally reduce oral contraceptive reliability, though the magnitude of this effect is debated and rifampin remains the antibiotic with the best-established interaction via CYP induction rather than this route).

A separate hepatic hydroxylation fork has drawn research interest for cancer-risk implications: 2-hydroxylation (via CYP1A1/1A2) produces relatively weak, less proliferative metabolites, while 16α-hydroxylation produces 16α-hydroxyestrone, a metabolite with stronger estrogenic/proliferative activity. This ratio is influenced by diet, exercise, and genetics and has been studied as a modifiable breast cancer risk marker, though it isn't yet standard clinical practice to measure it.

Turner Syndrome 45,X (or mosaic)
Chromosomal · ~1:2,500 female births

Accelerated germ cell atresia leads to streak gonads and estrogen deficiency. Classic labs: low estradiol with markedly elevated LH/FSH (primary ovarian insufficiency/hypergonadotropic hypogonadism), short stature, and a range of somatic features (webbed neck, coarctation, horseshoe kidney).

Aromatase (CYP19A1) Deficiency CYP19A1 · 15q21
Autosomal recessive · Very rare

Cannot convert androgens to estrogens. In a female fetus, unconverted androgens virilize external genitalia and cause maternal virilization during pregnancy (fetal androgens cross to mother, unable to be aromatized by the deficient placenta either). Postnatally: primary amenorrhea, absent breast development, elevated gonadotropins, hyperandrogenism, and — because estrogen (not androgen) closes growth plates in both sexes — tall stature with delayed bone age and osteoporosis if untreated.

Aromatase Excess Syndrome CYP19A1 gain-of-function (gene fusion/rearrangement) · 15q21
Autosomal dominant · Rare (few dozen kindreds reported)

The mirror image of aromatase deficiency: chromosomal rearrangements fuse CYP19A1 to a neighboring gene's promoter, causing extraglandular overexpression of aromatase (fat, skin, bone). In affected males: pre/peripubertal gynecomastia (often the presenting feature), advanced bone age, short adult stature from early epiphyseal fusion, and mild hypogonadotropic hypogonadism from estrogen-driven feedback suppression of LH/FSH. In affected females: macromastia, precocious isosexual puberty, irregular menses. Diagnosis requires CYP19A1 molecular analysis; aromatase inhibitors are used therapeutically.

Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS) Polygenic — not a single-gene disorder
Complex trait · ~6–13% of reproductive-age women

Not a classic Mendelian disorder, but included here because its physiology maps directly onto the two-cell theory above: elevated LH:FSH ratio, theca-cell androgen overproduction, insulin-mediated SHBG suppression, and impaired granulosa aromatization capacity relative to androgen substrate load. Full diagnostic criteria (Rotterdam), differential (including nonclassic CAH — see HPA section), and management are developed as a Volume 3 clinical scenario card.

Why C-peptide matters clinically
Because insulin and C-peptide are cleaved in equimolar amounts and packaged together, C-peptide is a direct proxy for endogenous insulin secretion — and it is not present in commercial insulin preparations. A hypoglycemic patient with high insulin but suppressed C-peptide points to exogenous insulin administration (accidental or factitious); high insulin with high C-peptide points to an endogenous source (insulinoma, sulfonylurea use — sulfonylureas stimulate the patient's own beta cells, so C-peptide stays elevated).

TriggerGlucose-Stimulated Insulin Secretion — the K-ATP Mechanism

  1. Glucose enters the beta cell via GLUT2 (high-capacity, low-affinity — lets intracellular glucose track plasma glucose faithfully without saturating).
  2. Glucokinase phosphorylates glucose — the beta cell's glucose sensor (rate-limiting, sets the glucose threshold for insulin release around ~90 mg/dL).
  3. Glycolysis/oxidative metabolism raises the intracellular ATP:ADP ratio.
  4. Rising ATP closes the K-ATP channel (SUR1/Kir6.2 subunits) → membrane depolarizes.
  5. Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → Ca²⁺ influx.
  6. Ca²⁺ triggers exocytosis of pre-formed insulin granules.

Sulfonylureas (glipizide, glyburide) bind SUR1 directly and close the K-ATP channel independent of glucose — the pharmacologic reason they can cause hypoglycemia even in the absence of a meal, unlike glucose-dependent secretagogues (GLP-1 agonists).

Insulin receptor is a receptor tyrosine kinase (see Foundational Principles). Autophosphorylation recruits IRS-1/IRS-2, which branches into two distinct signaling arms with different physiologic jobs:

Counter-regulationGlucagon

Made from proglucagon — the same precursor gene expressed differently by tissue-specific prohormone convertases, a good example of one gene producing entirely different hormones depending on cleavage context:

Glucagon acts on hepatocyte Gs-coupled receptors → cAMP/PKA → activates glycogen phosphorylase (glycogenolysis) and gluconeogenic enzymes (PEPCK, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase), and promotes ketogenesis by favoring fatty acid oxidation over re-esterification in the liver. Its receptor is essentially liver-restricted (unlike insulin's near-ubiquitous receptor distribution) — glucagon's job is specifically hepatic glucose output, not peripheral glucose disposal.

Local brakeSomatostatin & Amylin

Somatostatin (delta cells)

Broad paracrine inhibitor — suppresses insulin and glucagon release locally, plus (systemically, from hypothalamus and elsewhere) GH, TSH, and gastrin. This is why octreotide can blunt both hyper- and hypoglycemic counter-regulation and requires careful glucose monitoring when used clinically.

Amylin (co-secreted with insulin)

Delays gastric emptying, suppresses (inappropriately elevated) postprandial glucagon, and promotes satiety via the area postrema. Deficient in insulin-deficient states (T1DM) alongside insulin — the physiologic rationale for pramlintide as adjunct therapy.

BreakdownInsulin Clearance

Roughly half of portal-vein insulin is extracted by the liver on first pass (hepatic insulin-degrading enzyme) — meaning endogenous pancreatic insulin never reaches systemic circulation at the concentration it was secreted. This is one reason subcutaneously injected insulin (which bypasses first-pass hepatic clearance, entering systemic circulation directly) behaves pharmacokinetically differently from endogenous insulin and can produce relatively more peripheral than hepatic effect for a given dose. The kidney handles most of the remainder of insulin clearance — renal impairment prolongs insulin half-life and is a common cause of increasing hypoglycemia risk as CKD progresses.

Axis 6 · Calcium-Phosphate-Bone

PTH, Vitamin D, Calcitonin & FGF23

A three-hormone system (plus a fourth counter-player) regulating serum calcium and phosphate through coordinated action on bone, kidney, and gut — with genetics that map cleanly onto each node.

SensorThe Calcium-Sensing Receptor (CaSR)

Chief cells of the parathyroid gland express CaSR, a Gq-coupled receptor that directly senses ionized (free, not total) serum calcium. Rising calcium activates CaSR → suppresses PTH release; falling calcium removes that brake → PTH rises. This is an inverse sigmoidal relationship, not linear — small changes near the physiologic set-point produce large PTH swings, while the system is relatively insensitive to calcium changes far from set-point.

Why 25-OH-D, not calcitriol, is the clinical screening test
25-OH-vitamin D has a long half-life (~2–3 weeks) and directly reflects substrate availability (sun + diet), making it the correct screening measure for vitamin D status. Calcitriol (1,25-(OH)₂-D) has a short half-life (~4–6 hours), is tightly regulated by PTH/phosphate/FGF23 rather than substrate, and can be normal or even elevated in vitamin D deficiency with secondary hyperparathyroidism — measuring it to assess overall vitamin D status is a common ordering error.

Calcitriol's actions: intestine (↑ calbindin → ↑ active transcellular Ca²⁺ and phosphate absorption), bone (supports mineralization at physiologic levels; at very high levels can promote resorption alongside PTH), kidney (modest Ca²⁺ reabsorption), and — importantly — direct negative feedback on the parathyroid chief cell to suppress PTH transcription.

Counter-playersCalcitonin & FGF23

Calcitonin

From parafollicular (C) cells of the thyroid, released in response to rising calcium. Directly inhibits osteoclast activity, opposing PTH. Physiologic role in normal human calcium homeostasis is modest (thyroidectomized and medullary-thyroid-cancer patients with absent/excess calcitonin show only mild calcium derangement) — its main clinical utility is as a tumor marker and surveillance tool for medullary thyroid carcinoma, notably in MEN2 syndromes.

FGF23

Secreted by osteocytes in response to rising phosphate and calcitriol. Requires Klotho as an obligate co-receptor for high-affinity FGFR binding in the kidney. Actions: phosphaturic (inhibits proximal tubule NaPi cotransporters — same direction as PTH) and suppresses CYP27B1 (opposite direction from PTH — lowers active vitamin D). Net effect: FGF23 is the body's dedicated phosphate-lowering hormone, willing to sacrifice vitamin D activation to do it.

Oncology · Supportive Care

Oncology Side Effects Atlas

A complete, exhaustive reference of chemotherapy and radiation side effects — organized by organ system, each with the pathology behind it, likely causative agents, CTCAE-style mild/moderate/severe grading, and the specific agents/interventions that actually work (magic mouthwash for mucositis, ondansetron/aprepitant/dexamethasone for CINV, mesna for hemorrhagic cystitis, and more). Cited to ASCO, NCCN, MASCC, and ASTRO guidance per topic. Doses and protocols should be verified against current guidelines before clinical use.

01

Chemotherapy-Induced Nausea/Vomiting (CINV)

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Highly emetogenic: cisplatin, cyclophosphamide (high-dose), anthracyclines (esp. combined with cyclophosphamide). Moderately emetogenic: carboplatin, oxaliplatin, irinotecan.

Pathology / Mechanism

Chemotherapy triggers serotonin (5-HT3) release from enterochromaffin cells in the gut, activating vagal afferents to the brainstem chemoreceptor trigger zone (acute phase, first 24h); a delayed phase (24h-5 days) is more substance P/NK1-receptor-mediated. Anticipatory nausea is a separate, classically conditioned phenomenon from prior poorly-controlled cycles.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
Mild1-2 episodes/24h, tolerating oral intake
Moderate3-5 episodes/24h or IV fluids indicated <24h
Severe≥6 episodes/24h or IV fluids/tube feeding/TPN indicated ≥24h, or life-threatening consequences
Management & Most Effective Agents

First-line prophylaxis is risk-stratified per MASCC/ESMO and ASCO antiemetic guidelines: highly emetogenic regimens get a 4-drug combination (5-HT3 antagonist [ondansetron/granisetron], NK1 antagonist [aprepitant], dexamethasone, ± olanzapine); moderately emetogenic regimens typically use a 5-HT3 antagonist + dexamethasone. Olanzapine is specifically effective for breakthrough/refractory CINV and delayed nausea. Ondansetron (Zofran) remains the most recognizable and widely used 5-HT3 antagonist for acute-phase control.

Clinical Pearl

Anticipatory nausea does not respond to antiemetics started on the day of treatment — it requires anxiolytic/behavioral intervention (e.g., lorazepam, relaxation techniques) BEFORE the next cycle, and the best prevention is aggressive control of nausea during the first cycle so the conditioned response never develops.

References

ASCO Antiemetics Guideline Update (2020); MASCC/ESMO Antiemetic Guideline; NCCN Antiemesis guideline.

02

Oral Mucositis / Stomatitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

5-fluorouracil, methotrexate, high-dose melphalan (HCT conditioning), doxorubicin; risk amplified by concurrent head/neck radiation.

Pathology / Mechanism

Direct cytotoxic injury to rapidly-dividing basal oral mucosal epithelium, followed by an inflammatory cascade (pro-inflammatory cytokine release, NF-κB activation) that amplifies tissue damage beyond the initial insult — ulceration typically peaks around day 7-14 post-exposure as the epithelium fails to regenerate fast enough.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildErythema/soreness, able to eat a soft/regular diet
ModeratePatchy ulceration, pain that limits oral intake, requiring a modified diet
SevereConfluent ulceration, unable to tolerate any oral intake — may require IV hydration, feeding tube, or parenteral nutrition
Management & Most Effective Agents

"Magic mouthwash" (compounded combination — typically viscous lidocaine + diphenhydramine ± an antacid such as magnesium/aluminum hydroxide, formulations vary by institution) is the classic topical symptomatic agent for pain control. Basic oral care protocols (bland rinses, soft toothbrush) are foundational per MASCC/ISOO guidelines. Oral cryotherapy (ice chips held in the mouth during and shortly after bolus 5-FU infusion) is a specifically evidence-supported PREVENTIVE measure for 5-FU-induced mucositis. Palifermin (recombinant keratinocyte growth factor) is reserved for high-dose chemotherapy/autologous HCT conditioning settings given cost/narrow indication.

Clinical Pearl

Cryotherapy timing only works for bolus 5-FU (vasoconstriction reduces drug delivery to oral mucosa during the brief infusion) — it is NOT effective for continuous-infusion 5-FU or most other mucotoxic agents, so don't extrapolate the intervention beyond its evidence base.

References

MASCC/ISOO Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Mucositis; NCCN Supportive Care guidelines.

03

Chemotherapy-Induced Diarrhea

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Irinotecan (both an early cholinergic and a later, more severe secretory mechanism), 5-fluorouracil/capecitabine, some targeted therapies (EGFR/TKI class).

Pathology / Mechanism

Irinotecan's active metabolite (SN-38) causes direct mucosal injury to the intestinal epithelium (delayed, secretory-type diarrhea, typically >24h after dosing); a separate EARLY cholinergic mechanism (within hours) results from irinotecan's acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting activity, distinct in timing and treatment from the delayed type.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildIncrease of <4 stools/day over baseline
ModerateIncrease of 4-6 stools/day over baseline; IV fluids indicated <24h
SevereIncrease of ≥7 stools/day; incontinence; hospitalization; or life-threatening hemodynamic collapse
Management & Most Effective Agents

Early cholinergic (irinotecan, within hours of infusion): atropine (anticholinergic) is specifically effective and should be given promptly, sometimes prophylactically in patients with a prior episode. Delayed/secretory diarrhea: high-dose loperamide (higher and more frequent dosing than OTC labeling) is first-line; octreotide is used for loperamide-refractory or severe cases. Aggressive oral/IV hydration and electrolyte monitoring are essential regardless of cause given the risk of rapid dehydration.

Clinical Pearl

Don't give loperamide for irinotecan's EARLY cholinergic diarrhea — it won't address the underlying acetylcholinesterase-mediated mechanism the way atropine will, and recognizing which phase you're treating changes the entire management approach.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline (chemotherapy-induced diarrhea); ASCO/ONS guidance on irinotecan-specific management.

04

Chemotherapy-Induced Constipation

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Vincristine and other vinca alkaloids (autonomic/enteric neuropathy); opioid analgesics used for cancer pain compound the risk.

Pathology / Mechanism

Vinca alkaloids disrupt microtubule function in enteric neurons (the same mechanism causing peripheral neuropathy), impairing normal peristaltic autonomic signaling — this is a genuine neuropathic constipation, distinct from simple opioid-induced slowed transit, though the two commonly coexist and compound each other.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildOccasional symptoms, stool softener/laxative sufficient
ModeratePersistent symptoms requiring regular laxative use, limiting instrumental ADLs
SevereObstipation/fecal impaction requiring manual evacuation, or bowel obstruction requiring intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

Prophylactic bowel regimen (senna ± docusate) started AT THE SAME TIME as vincristine or opioids, rather than waiting for symptoms, is standard practice. Escalate to osmotic laxatives (polyethylene glycol) for breakthrough symptoms; avoid bulk-forming fiber laxatives in patients with reduced motility (can worsen obstruction risk).

Clinical Pearl

Unrecognized vincristine-related ileus can progress to a true bowel obstruction picture — a cancer patient on vincristine with worsening abdominal distension and no bowel movements needs prompt evaluation, not just an increased laxative dose.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline; ONS Putting Evidence Into Practice — constipation.

05

Chemotherapy-Associated Hepatotoxicity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Methotrexate (cumulative dose-related hepatic fibrosis with long-term/chronic low-dose use), many oral targeted therapies/TKIs (transaminitis), high-dose regimens generally.

Pathology / Mechanism

Methotrexate depletes hepatic folate stores and directly promotes hepatic stellate cell activation with chronic exposure, leading to periportal fibrosis over years of cumulative dosing (most classically described with chronic low-dose use in rheumatologic disease, but relevant to oncologic dosing schedules as well); many TKIs cause idiosyncratic or dose-related hepatocellular injury via direct/metabolic mechanisms specific to each agent.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildAST/ALT >1-3x ULN, asymptomatic
ModerateAST/ALT >3-5x ULN, or symptomatic (fatigue, jaundice)
SevereAST/ALT >5-20x ULN or evidence of hepatic decompensation/failure
Management & Most Effective Agents

Routine LFT monitoring at defined intervals is the primary preventive strategy, allowing dose interruption/reduction before progression to fibrosis or acute liver injury. Established hepatic fibrosis from chronic methotrexate may warrant periodic liver biopsy or noninvasive fibrosis staging (elastography) in long-term use; acute hepatocellular injury from TKIs generally requires holding the agent and, for higher grades, permanent discontinuation.

Clinical Pearl

Baseline hepatitis B/C screening before starting many chemotherapy and especially immunotherapy/monoclonal antibody regimens is important not just for hepatotoxicity risk but for viral reactivation risk (see rituximab/anti-CD20 hepatitis B reactivation) — don't conflate the two mechanisms when a patient's LFTs rise.

References

NCCN Supportive Care/hepatotoxicity monitoring guidance; methotrexate long-term hepatic monitoring literature.

01

Chemotherapy-Induced Neutropenia / Myelosuppression

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Nearly all cytotoxic chemotherapy classes, most severely alkylating agents, anthracyclines, and high-dose combination regimens; less pronounced with vincristine and bleomycin specifically.

Pathology / Mechanism

Cytotoxic agents preferentially kill rapidly-dividing bone marrow progenitor cells alongside tumor cells (the basis of the whole therapeutic-index concept in chemotherapy) — the neutrophil lineage has the shortest half-life of the major cell lines, so neutropenia is typically the first and most clinically significant cytopenia to manifest, generally nadiring 7-14 days after treatment.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildANC 1000-1500/µL
ModerateANC 500-1000/µL
SevereANC <500/µL (Grade 4) — see the Oncology Emergencies Atlas for febrile neutropenia management specifically
Management & Most Effective Agents

Primary G-CSF prophylaxis (filgrastim/pegfilgrastim) for regimens with >20% febrile neutropenia risk; secondary prophylaxis after a prior febrile neutropenia episode. Dose reduction/delay for recurrent significant neutropenia. See the Oncology Emergencies Atlas for the full febrile neutropenia management pathway once fever occurs.

Clinical Pearl

G-CSF is prevention/recovery support, not a treatment FOR an active febrile neutropenia episode's infection — antibiotics remain the primary treatment once fever occurs; don't let growth factor administration create false reassurance that delays empiric antibiotics.

References

ASCO/ASH Growth Factor Guideline; NCCN Hematopoietic Growth Factors guideline (see also the Oncology Interventions Atlas).

02

Chemotherapy-Induced Anemia

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Platinum agents (especially carboplatin), and cumulative myelosuppression from any multi-cycle regimen.

Pathology / Mechanism

Direct marrow suppression of erythroid progenitors plus, in some cases, relative erythropoietin deficiency or blunted marrow response to endogenous erythropoietin — anemia typically develops more gradually than neutropenia given the red cell's much longer (~120 day) lifespan, so it often becomes clinically apparent only after several cycles.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildHgb 10-11.9 g/dL
ModerateHgb 8-9.9 g/dL, or transfusion indicated
SevereHgb <8 g/dL or life-threatening consequences requiring urgent intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

RBC transfusion for symptomatic or severe anemia. ESAs (epoetin/darbepoetin) are restricted to palliative-intent chemotherapy per FDA black-box labeling given mortality/VTE/tumor-progression signals — see the Oncology Interventions Atlas for full detail. Iron studies should be checked, since functional iron deficiency blunts any ESA response and iron repletion alone may improve mild anemia.

Clinical Pearl

Always confirm chemotherapy intent (curative vs. palliative) before offering an ESA for chemotherapy-induced anemia — this single distinction is the crux of the FDA safety label discussed in depth in the Oncology Interventions Atlas.

References

See Oncology Interventions Atlas (Iron/ESA/G-CSF section) for full ESA safety data and references.

03

Chemotherapy-Induced Thrombocytopenia

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Carboplatin (notably more myelosuppressive to platelets than cisplatin), gemcitabine, high-dose combination regimens.

Pathology / Mechanism

Direct marrow suppression of megakaryocyte progenitors; platelets have a short circulating lifespan (~7-10 days) similar to neutrophils, so thrombocytopenia often nadirs on a similar timeline to neutropenia within a treatment cycle.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildPlatelets 75,000-99,000/µL
ModeratePlatelets 50,000-74,000/µL
SeverePlatelets <25,000/µL or any bleeding requiring transfusion/intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

Platelet transfusion for severe thrombocytopenia with bleeding or before invasive procedures (typical threshold <10,000-20,000/µL prophylactically, higher threshold before procedures). Dose reduction/delay of the causative agent for recurrent severe thrombocytopenia. Thrombopoietin-receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag) have a more limited, situation-specific oncologic role (e.g., chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia in specific trial-supported settings) rather than routine use.

Clinical Pearl

Don't anchor on chemotherapy as the automatic cause of a falling platelet count without considering heparin-induced thrombocytopenia or DIC in the right clinical context (e.g., a hospitalized patient on prophylactic heparin) — the workup differs substantially.

References

ASCO Platelet Transfusion Guideline; NCCN Supportive Care guideline.

01

Alopecia

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Taxanes (paclitaxel/docetaxel), cyclophosphamide, anthracyclines (doxorubicin) — essentially any agent targeting rapidly dividing cells, including hair follicle matrix cells.

Pathology / Mechanism

Hair follicle matrix cells are among the most rapidly dividing cells in the body (turnover rivaling bone marrow and GI epithelium), making them collaterally vulnerable to the same cytotoxic mechanisms targeting tumor cells — hair loss is nearly always reversible after treatment ends, though regrowth texture/color can transiently differ.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildHair loss <50% of normal, not requiring a wig if patient chooses to camouflage
ModerateHair loss ≥50%, readily apparent, patient may elect a wig for camouflage
SevereNot typically graded beyond moderate — psychosocial impact can still be severe even without a higher CTCAE grade
Management & Most Effective Agents

Scalp cooling devices (e.g., DigniCap, Paxman) worn during and after infusion reduce follicular blood flow/drug delivery and have reasonable evidence for reducing (not eliminating) hair loss with specific regimens, most established in breast cancer taxane/anthracycline protocols. No pharmacologic agent reliably prevents chemotherapy alopecia. Wigs, scarves, and psychosocial support/counseling remain the mainstay of management.

Clinical Pearl

Set expectations before treatment starts — alopecia is one of the most psychologically distressing side effects despite being medically minor, and proactive counseling (timing of onset, expected regrowth, scalp cooling eligibility) meaningfully improves the patient experience even when little can be pharmacologically prevented.

References

NCCN Survivorship guideline; scalp cooling device clinical trial literature (breast cancer taxane regimens).

02

Hand-Foot Syndrome (Palmar-Plantar Erythrodysesthesia)

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Capecitabine (classic association), 5-fluorouracil, liposomal doxorubicin.

Pathology / Mechanism

Drug is concentrated and slowly eliminated through eccrine sweat glands in the palms/soles (high density in these areas), producing direct local cytotoxic injury to the skin — a distinct entity from the hand-foot SKIN REACTION seen with multi-kinase inhibitors (a different, hyperkeratotic mechanism, seen with agents like sorafenib/regorafenib).

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMinimal skin changes/dermatitis without pain
ModerateSkin changes with pain, limiting instrumental ADLs
SevereSevere skin changes with pain, limiting self-care ADLs (ulceration, blistering, severe desquamation)
Management & Most Effective Agents

Dose modification/interruption of the causative agent for moderate-severe symptoms is the primary effective intervention. Supportive measures: emollients/moisturizers, avoiding heat/friction/tight footwear, cooling. Evidence for pyridoxine (vitamin B6) prophylaxis is inconsistent/limited despite common use. Topical corticosteroids may help symptomatically but don't address the underlying mechanism.

Clinical Pearl

Hand-foot SYNDROME (capecitabine/5-FU) and hand-foot SKIN REACTION (multi-kinase TKIs) look similar but have different mechanisms, different typical distributions (skin reaction is more localized to pressure points/friction areas), and different management nuances — don't use the terms interchangeably in documentation or when researching management.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline; capecitabine/5-FU prescribing information dermatologic toxicity sections.

03

EGFR-Inhibitor Acneiform Rash

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Cetuximab, panitumumab (monoclonal antibodies), erlotinib, gefitinib (TKIs) — any EGFR-pathway-targeted agent.

Pathology / Mechanism

EGFR is normally expressed in the epidermal basal layer and is essential for normal keratinocyte proliferation/differentiation — blocking this pathway paradoxically produces an inflammatory, acne-like follicular eruption (not a true infectious/hormonal acne) typically on the face/scalp/upper trunk within the first 1-2 weeks of treatment.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildPapules/pustules covering <10% BSA, may or may not have associated symptoms
ModeratePapules/pustules covering 10-30% BSA, with mild symptoms and psychosocial impact
SeverePapules/pustules covering >30% BSA with severe symptoms, limiting self-care ADLs, or superinfection
Management & Most Effective Agents

Prophylactic treatment starting BEFORE or at the onset of therapy (rather than waiting for the rash to develop) is specifically supported by trial evidence: oral doxycycline or minocycline plus a topical steroid/moisturizer reduces rash severity. Established rash is managed with the same combination, escalating topical steroid potency for higher grades, and dose modification only for severe/refractory cases since the rash itself has been correlated with better treatment response in some studies.

Clinical Pearl

This rash correlating with better tumor response in several EGFR-inhibitor trials is a genuinely useful (if counterintuitive) reassurance point for patients distressed by a prominent rash — but it doesn't mean the rash should go untreated, since severe cases still meaningfully impair quality of life and can superinfect.

References

MASCC Skin Toxicity Study Group guidelines for EGFR-inhibitor dermatologic toxicity.

04

Extravasation / Vesicant Injury

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Anthracyclines (doxorubicin, daunorubicin — severe vesicants), vinca alkaloids (vincristine, vinblastine — severe vesicants), and other vesicant/irritant classes to a lesser degree.

Pathology / Mechanism

Accidental infiltration of a vesicant drug into the subcutaneous tissue (rather than the vein) causes direct cytotoxic tissue injury; unlike a typical irritant, vesicants continue to cause progressive tissue necrosis over days-to-weeks after the event because the drug binds locally and is not readily cleared, unlike a simple chemical irritant reaction.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildErythema, swelling <5cm without ulceration
ModerateErythema/swelling ≥5cm, or associated pain/induration
SevereUlceration, tissue necrosis, or need for surgical intervention/debridement
Management & Most Effective Agents

STOP the infusion immediately upon suspicion, aspirate residual drug from the line before removal, and elevate the limb. Agent-specific antidotes matter: dexrazoxane (IV, systemic) is FDA-approved specifically for anthracycline extravasation and should be given within 6 hours; hyaluronidase (local injection) is used for vinca alkaloid extravasation to promote drug dispersion/absorption. Cold compresses are generally used for anthracycline extravasation (vasoconstriction limits spread) while WARM compresses are used for vinca alkaloid extravasation (vasodilation promotes local drug clearance) — using the wrong temperature for the wrong drug class is a recognized, avoidable error.

Clinical Pearl

The cold-vs-warm compress distinction is the single most commonly missed detail in extravasation management — always confirm which drug class you're dealing with before applying either, since the wrong choice can worsen the outcome rather than help.

References

ONS/ESMO Extravasation Management Guidelines; ISMP guidance on vesicant chemotherapy safe handling.

01

Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN)

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Platinum agents (cisplatin, oxaliplatin — oxaliplatin has a distinct acute cold-triggered variant), taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel), vinca alkaloids (vincristine), bortezomib, thalidomide.

Pathology / Mechanism

Multiple convergent mechanisms depending on drug class: platinum agents accumulate in dorsal root ganglia (outside the blood-brain barrier) causing direct sensory neuron cell body injury; taxanes and vinca alkaloids disrupt axonal microtubule transport; bortezomib affects mitochondrial function in sensory neurons — the common endpoint is a length-dependent, predominantly sensory (occasionally motor/autonomic) axonal neuropathy.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildAsymptomatic or mild symptoms, not limiting ADLs
ModerateModerate symptoms limiting instrumental ADLs
SevereSevere symptoms limiting self-care ADLs, or disabling
Management & Most Effective Agents

Duloxetine is the only agent with ASCO-endorsed moderate-quality evidence for established CIPN treatment (based on a randomized trial in mixed platinum/taxane-induced neuropathy) — other commonly tried agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclics) have weaker or extrapolated evidence from other neuropathic pain conditions. Dose reduction, delay, or discontinuation of the causative agent remains the most effective PREVENTIVE strategy once significant symptoms emerge, since no agent has strong evidence for true prevention.

Clinical Pearl

Oxaliplatin's acute neuropathy is specifically triggered/worsened by cold exposure (cold drinks, air conditioning, touching cold objects) within days of infusion — patient counseling to avoid cold exposure around infusion days is a simple, evidence-informed intervention distinct from the chronic cumulative neuropathy seen with continued dosing.

References

ASCO Guideline: Prevention and Management of Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy.

02

Ifosfamide-Related Encephalopathy

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Ifosfamide (risk increased with renal impairment, low albumin, prior/concurrent CNS disease, and higher doses).

Pathology / Mechanism

Chloroacetaldehyde, a neurotoxic ifosfamide metabolite, accumulates and is thought to impair mitochondrial oxidative metabolism in the CNS, producing a reversible metabolic encephalopathy ranging from mild confusion to coma — distinct from and easily mistaken for CNS disease progression or an unrelated delirium.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild confusion/somnolence, no intervention needed beyond monitoring
ModerateModerate confusion requiring holding the next dose and closer monitoring
SevereSevere confusion, seizure, or coma requiring ICU-level care and pharmacologic reversal
Management & Most Effective Agents

Methylene blue is the specific treatment for established ifosfamide encephalopathy and is also used prophylactically in patients with risk factors or a prior episode who require continued ifosfamide therapy. Supportive care (holding the causative dose, ensuring adequate hydration/renal function) is essential alongside methylene blue.

Clinical Pearl

Before assuming a confused patient on ifosfamide has disease progression or an unrelated cause of delirium, always check the medication list specifically for ifosfamide — this is one of the more reversible and specifically treatable causes of altered mental status in oncology, and methylene blue response can be diagnostic as well as therapeutic.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline; ifosfamide prescribing information neurotoxicity section.

03

Cancer-Treatment-Related Cognitive Impairment ("Chemo Brain")

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Broadly reported across many regimens, most studied in breast cancer chemotherapy (anthracycline/cyclophosphamide-based regimens) but not isolated to any single drug class.

Pathology / Mechanism

Proposed mechanisms include direct neurotoxicity, treatment-related neuroinflammation, hormonal changes (especially with concurrent endocrine therapy), and secondary contributors (fatigue, sleep disruption, depression/anxiety, anemia) — the relative contribution of direct chemotherapy neurotoxicity versus these confounding factors remains an active area of research and is likely multifactorial in most patients.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild cognitive changes noticed by patient, not affecting work/daily function
ModerateModerate changes affecting work performance or instrumental ADLs
SevereSevere changes significantly impairing independence/self-care
Management & Most Effective Agents

Cognitive rehabilitation/training programs and structured aerobic exercise have the best current evidence for improving symptoms. Addressing reversible contributors (treating anemia, sleep disorders, depression/anxiety) is a critical and often underemphasized first step. Pharmacologic options (methylphenidate and other stimulants) have inconsistent trial evidence and are not first-line.

Clinical Pearl

Before attributing cognitive complaints entirely to chemotherapy, systematically screen for and address the reversible contributors (anemia, poor sleep, mood symptoms, hypothyroidism) — meaningful improvement is often achievable by treating these rather than accepting cognitive decline as an inevitable, untreatable consequence of chemotherapy.

References

NCCN Survivorship guideline (cognitive function); ASCO/ONS cognitive impairment supportive care literature.

01

Anthracycline Cardiotoxicity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Doxorubicin, daunorubicin, epirubicin, idarubicin — risk is cumulative-dose-dependent (see the Oncology Interventions Atlas for specific dose thresholds).

Pathology / Mechanism

Anthracyclines generate reactive oxygen species and inhibit topoisomerase-2β specifically in cardiomyocytes (which have limited antioxidant reserve and essentially no regenerative capacity compared to most other tissues), producing progressive, often irreversible myocardial injury that is dose-cumulative rather than idiosyncratic.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildAsymptomatic LVEF decline (e.g., 10-19% drop from baseline or to 50-59%), no intervention beyond monitoring
ModerateSymptomatic heart failure responsive to medical management, or asymptomatic LVEF decline to 40-49%
SevereSevere/refractory heart failure, LVEF <40%, or life-threatening cardiac decompensation
Management & Most Effective Agents

See the Oncology Interventions Atlas for full detail — dexrazoxane for high cumulative-dose patients, baseline/serial LVEF monitoring, and standard heart failure medical therapy (ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta-blockers) for established cardiotoxicity, often in cardio-oncology co-management.

Clinical Pearl

Unlike trastuzumab cardiotoxicity (often reversible), anthracycline cardiotoxicity is frequently only partially reversible or irreversible — this distinction should shape how aggressively cumulative dose limits and cardioprotective strategies are enforced from the start of treatment rather than reactively after damage appears.

References

See Oncology Interventions Atlas (Antitumor Antibiotics section) for full cardiotoxicity dose data and references.

02

Bleomycin Pulmonary Toxicity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Bleomycin (risk increases with cumulative dose, age >70, renal impairment, and — critically — any subsequent supplemental oxygen exposure at any future time point).

Pathology / Mechanism

Bleomycin generates reactive oxygen species preferentially in lung tissue (which has relatively low levels of the detoxifying enzyme bleomycin hydrolase compared to other tissues), producing progressive pulmonary fibrosis; supplemental oxygen at ANY point after bleomycin exposure — including remote future surgeries — can trigger acute lung injury by amplifying this same oxidative mechanism, regardless of how long ago treatment occurred.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildAsymptomatic, radiographic changes only or mild dyspnea on exertion
ModerateSymptomatic dyspnea limiting instrumental ADLs, requiring intervention
SevereSevere dyspnea limiting self-care ADLs, hypoxia requiring oxygen, or life-threatening respiratory failure
Management & Most Effective Agents

Discontinue bleomycin promptly at the first sign of toxicity (declining DLCO on serial pulmonary function testing is often the earliest detectable change, sometimes preceding symptoms). Corticosteroids are used for symptomatic pneumonitis. Critically, minimize supplemental oxygen exposure indefinitely after treatment when clinically feasible (e.g., during future anesthesia) — this is a lifelong consideration the patient should carry forward, not just a concern during active treatment.

Clinical Pearl

Every patient who has ever received bleomycin should be counseled to inform ALL future anesthesia/surgical teams of this exposure, indefinitely — this single communication gap (a patient not mentioning remote bleomycin exposure before an unrelated future surgery) is a recognized, preventable cause of serious harm.

References

Bleomycin prescribing information pulmonary toxicity section; anesthesia literature on bleomycin-associated oxygen toxicity risk.

01

Cisplatin Nephrotoxicity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Cisplatin (dose-related and cumulative; carboplatin is comparatively much less nephrotoxic).

Pathology / Mechanism

Cisplatin is actively transported into proximal renal tubular cells (via organic cation transporters) at concentrations exceeding plasma levels, causing direct tubular cell injury, oxidative stress, and inflammation — the proximal tubule's role in concentrating/reabsorbing filtrate is exactly what concentrates the drug there in the first place.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildCreatinine 1.5-2x baseline, no intervention needed
ModerateCreatinine 2-3x baseline, or intervention indicated
SevereCreatinine >3x baseline, life-threatening consequences, or dialysis indicated
Management & Most Effective Agents

Aggressive IV pre- and post-hydration (6-8 hour infusion protocols) ± mannitol is the primary preventive strategy — see the Oncology Interventions Atlas for full detail. Magnesium and other electrolyte repletion (cisplatin causes concurrent renal magnesium wasting) is important supportive care. Renal function must return to baseline before redosing; substitution with carboplatin is considered if nephrotoxicity limits continued cisplatin use.

Clinical Pearl

Cisplatin-induced renal magnesium wasting can cause a refractory hypomagnesemia that persists for months after treatment ends — see the Electrolyte Correction Atlas for magnesium replacement strategies, and remember that this specific electrolyte disturbance can outlast the acute nephrotoxicity itself.

References

See Oncology Interventions Atlas (Platinum-Based Chemotherapy section) for full nephrotoxicity data and references.

02

Hemorrhagic Cystitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide (via their shared toxic metabolite, acrolein).

Pathology / Mechanism

Acrolein, a metabolite of both cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide, is excreted in the urine and directly damages the bladder urothelium, causing mucosal inflammation, ulceration, and hemorrhage — the bladder is essentially exposed to a concentrated toxic metabolite bath during urination after these agents.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMicroscopic hematuria only
ModerateGross hematuria without clots
SevereGross hematuria with clots, requiring transfusion, or urinary tract obstruction requiring intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

Mesna (a thiol compound that binds/neutralizes acrolein directly in the bladder) given concurrently with cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide is the primary preventive agent — essential with ifosfamide and higher-dose cyclophosphamide regimens. Aggressive hydration and frequent bladder emptying reduce urothelial contact time with acrolein. Established severe hemorrhagic cystitis may require bladder irrigation, and refractory/severe cases can require hyperbaric oxygen (borrowing from the radiation cystitis literature) or cystoscopic intervention.

Clinical Pearl

Mesna prevents hemorrhagic cystitis but has NO effect on the antitumor efficacy or other toxicities of cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide — it is a purely protective adjunct targeted at one specific toxic metabolite pathway, not a general chemoprotectant.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline; mesna/cyclophosphamide/ifosfamide prescribing information.

01

Chemotherapy-Associated SIADH

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Vincristine, cyclophosphamide (especially high-dose IV), cisplatin.

Pathology / Mechanism

These agents can directly stimulate hypothalamic/pituitary antidiuretic hormone release or, in cyclophosphamide's case, potentiate ADH's effect at the renal collecting duct — the result is water retention and dilutional hyponatremia despite normal or expanded volume status, the hallmark of SIADH as opposed to hypovolemic hyponatremia.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildSodium 130-134 mEq/L, asymptomatic
ModerateSodium 121-129 mEq/L, or mild symptoms (nausea, headache)
SevereSodium ≤120 mEq/L or symptomatic (seizure, altered mental status) — see the Electrolyte Correction Atlas for full severity/correction protocol
Management & Most Effective Agents

Fluid restriction is first-line for mild-moderate cases. Hold/avoid the causative agent if the pattern is clearly drug-related and recurrent. See the Electrolyte Correction Atlas for the full sodium correction protocol (including the critical ≤8 mEq/L/24h chronic correction cap) for moderate-severe cases.

Clinical Pearl

Cyclophosphamide-induced SIADH classically occurs within hours of a HIGH-DOSE IV pulse — if a patient is also receiving aggressive IV hydration around the same high-dose cyclophosphamide dose (common in conditioning regimens), the combination can produce rapid, clinically significant hyponatremia that's easy to miss if sodium isn't checked proactively.

References

See Electrolyte Correction Atlas (Sodium section) for full correction protocol; NCCN Supportive Care guideline.

02

Hormonal Therapy Tumor Flare Phenomenon

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

LHRH/GnRH agonists (leuprolide, goserelin) in prostate cancer without an anti-androgen bridge; tamoxifen (rarely) in breast cancer.

Pathology / Mechanism

GnRH agonists initially cause a paradoxical SURGE in LH/FSH and consequently testosterone (or estrogen, mechanistically analogous) before the negative feedback/receptor downregulation that produces their intended suppressive effect takes hold days later — this transient surge can stimulate hormone-sensitive tumor growth, worsening bone pain, urinary obstruction, or spinal cord compression symptoms in patients with existing metastatic burden.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildTransient increase in existing symptoms (bone pain, urinary symptoms) without acute complication
ModerateSymptom flare requiring additional analgesia or urgent evaluation
SevereFlare precipitating an oncologic emergency (spinal cord compression, urinary obstruction, pathologic fracture) — see the Oncology Emergencies Atlas
Management & Most Effective Agents

Co-administer an anti-androgen (e.g., bicalutamide) starting a few days BEFORE and continuing for a couple weeks after starting an LHRH agonist in prostate cancer patients with significant metastatic burden — this bridging strategy blocks the peripheral effect of the testosterone surge and is standard practice specifically to prevent this phenomenon, not an optional add-on.

Clinical Pearl

Tumor flare is entirely predictable and preventable with the anti-androgen bridging strategy — a patient who develops worsening bone pain or new urinary retention shortly after starting an LHRH agonist WITHOUT a bridging anti-androgen should prompt immediate consideration of this mechanism rather than assumed disease progression.

References

NCCN Prostate Cancer guideline (androgen deprivation therapy initiation).

01

Chemotherapy-Related Infertility / Gonadal Toxicity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Alkylating agents (cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, busulfan, melphalan) carry the highest risk among chemotherapy classes; risk is dose- and age-dependent.

Pathology / Mechanism

Alkylating agents damage the ovarian follicle pool (a fixed, non-renewable reserve depleted faster than natural aging would deplete it) and testicular germ cells (Sertoli/spermatogonial cells specifically, which have high proliferative turnover) — the degree of damage is both cumulative-dose-dependent and age-dependent, with older patients having less reserve to begin with.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildTemporary oligospermia/menstrual irregularity, expected recovery
ModerateProlonged amenorrhea/oligospermia (>1 year), uncertain recovery
SeverePermanent infertility/premature ovarian failure or azoospermia
Management & Most Effective Agents

Fertility preservation counseling and referral BEFORE treatment starts is the single most important intervention — sperm banking (straightforward, quick) and oocyte/embryo cryopreservation (requires more lead time, ovarian stimulation) should be discussed with every reproductive-age patient regardless of how urgent treatment initiation feels, since even a short delay for banking rarely meaningfully changes oncologic outcomes. GnRH agonist co-administration during chemotherapy for ovarian protection has mixed/inconsistent trial evidence and should be framed as adjunctive at best, not a substitute for fertility preservation.

Clinical Pearl

Fertility preservation counseling is frequently skipped or rushed in the urgency of starting cancer treatment — but for most regimens, the few days needed for a sperm bank referral (and often feasible timelines for oocyte/embryo banking) do not meaningfully delay oncologic treatment, and patients consistently report this counseling as something they wish had been offered even when they ultimately decline it.

References

ASCO Fertility Preservation Guideline; NCCN Adolescent and Young Adult Oncology guideline.

02

Infusion Reactions / Hypersensitivity

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Taxanes (paclitaxel — related to the cremophor solvent vehicle, less common with albumin-bound nab-paclitaxel), platinum agents (cumulative-exposure-related, more common with re-treatment), monoclonal antibodies (rituximab especially with the first infusion, given high circulating target-cell burden).

Pathology / Mechanism

Mechanisms vary by cause: taxane reactions are often a direct/non-IgE-mediated reaction to the cremophor EL solvent vehicle itself rather than the drug; platinum hypersensitivity is a true IgE-mediated reaction that develops and intensifies with repeated exposure (unlike most drug allergies that are stable or present from first exposure); rituximab's first-infusion reactions relate to rapid cytokine release from a large burden of CD20+ cells being targeted simultaneously (a cytokine-release-like phenomenon more than classic anaphylaxis).

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild transient flushing/rash, intervention not indicated, infusion can continue
ModerateSymptoms requiring interruption of infusion, responsive to symptomatic treatment (antihistamine, brief pause)
SevereSevere reaction (bronchospasm, hypotension, urticaria with systemic symptoms) requiring urgent intervention and infusion discontinuation
Management & Most Effective Agents

Premedication (corticosteroids, H1/H2 antihistamines) before taxane and platinum infusions is standard and specifically reduces reaction incidence/severity. Slowing the infusion rate at the first sign of reaction, followed by symptomatic treatment, allows many mild-moderate reactions to resolve without full discontinuation. For patients who need a specific agent despite a prior hypersensitivity reaction (common with platinum re-treatment in ovarian cancer), formal desensitization protocols (structured, graded step-up re-exposure under close monitoring) allow continued safe treatment in many cases rather than abandoning an effective agent outright.

Clinical Pearl

Platinum hypersensitivity classically WORSENS with cumulative exposure/retreatment (unlike most drug reactions) — a patient tolerating carboplatin fine for 4 cycles can still develop a significant reaction on cycle 6 or a later retreatment course after a treatment-free interval, so ongoing vigilance shouldn't fade just because prior cycles were uneventful.

References

NCCN Supportive Care guideline (infusion reaction management); desensitization protocol literature for platinum hypersensitivity.

03

Cancer-Related Fatigue

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Virtually universal across chemotherapy and radiation, and often persists independent of any single causative agent.

Pathology / Mechanism

Multifactorial and incompletely understood — proposed contributors include treatment-related inflammatory cytokine release, anemia, sleep disruption, deconditioning, and psychological distress, with most patients experiencing a combination rather than one dominant cause; unlike ordinary fatigue, cancer-related fatigue is characteristically disproportionate to recent activity and not fully relieved by rest.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildFatigue relieved by rest, not limiting ADLs
ModerateFatigue not fully relieved by rest, limiting instrumental ADLs
SevereFatigue not relieved by rest, limiting self-care ADLs, or requiring bed rest
Management & Most Effective Agents

Structured aerobic exercise has the strongest and most consistent evidence of any intervention for cancer-related fatigue — counterintuitively more effective than rest. Systematically screen for and treat reversible contributors (anemia, hypothyroidism, sleep disorders, depression/anxiety, pain, deconditioning) as the second pillar of management. Pharmacologic stimulants (methylphenidate) have inconsistent evidence and are considered only after the above have been addressed.

Clinical Pearl

The instinct to recommend more rest for a fatigued cancer patient is usually wrong — structured, supervised exercise programs (even modest ones) outperform rest-focused recommendations in the trial literature, and this counterintuitive point is worth actively explaining to patients who assume rest is the obvious answer.

References

NCCN Cancer-Related Fatigue guideline; ASCO Cancer-Related Fatigue Guideline.

01

Radiation Dermatitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Any external beam field, most prominently breast, head & neck, and skin-surface-proximal treatment areas; risk increases with higher total dose, larger fraction size, and concurrent chemotherapy (radiosensitization).

Pathology / Mechanism

Ionizing radiation damages basal keratinocyte stem cells in the epidermis, impairing normal skin turnover/repair — combined with direct microvascular injury and an inflammatory response, this produces a spectrum from erythema to moist desquamation as the skin's regenerative capacity is progressively overwhelmed over the course of a multi-week treatment course.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildFaint erythema or dry desquamation
ModerateModerate to brisk erythema, or patchy moist desquamation mostly confined to skin folds
SevereMoist desquamation in areas other than skin folds, bleeding with minor trauma, or life-threatening skin necrosis/ulceration
Management & Most Effective Agents

Gentle skin care (fragrance-free moisturizers, avoiding harsh soaps/adhesives/sun exposure to the field) is foundational preventive care throughout treatment. Topical corticosteroids (mid-potency) reduce severity/symptoms for higher-grade reactions. Silver sulfadiazine or other moist wound-care dressings are used for established moist desquamation to promote healing and reduce infection risk.

Clinical Pearl

Avoid recommending most topical antiperspirants/deodorants and certain metallic-ingredient creams in the treatment field during active radiation — some can increase surface dose (a "bolus effect") and worsen the reaction, though evidence on which specific products matter is more nuanced than older blanket restrictions suggested; check current institutional skin care protocols.

References

ONS/MASCC skin toxicity management guidelines; ASTRO patient education materials on radiation dermatitis.

01

Radiation Mucositis / Esophagitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Head & neck radiation (oral/pharyngeal mucositis), thoracic radiation for lung cancer or mediastinal lymphoma (esophagitis) — risk amplified substantially by concurrent chemotherapy (chemoradiation).

Pathology / Mechanism

Same fundamental basal epithelial injury mechanism as chemotherapy mucositis (rapidly-dividing mucosal stem cells are radiosensitive), but radiation mucositis is more localized to the treatment field and tends to worsen progressively and more severely as cumulative fractionated dose accumulates over a multi-week course, rather than peaking and resolving within a single chemotherapy cycle.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildErythema of the mucosa, minimal pain, able to eat a regular diet
ModeratePatchy mucositis, or moderate pain limiting to a soft/liquid diet
SevereConfluent mucositis with severe pain preventing adequate oral intake — often requiring a feeding tube or IV nutrition support
Management & Most Effective Agents

"Magic mouthwash" and other topical analgesic rinses provide symptomatic relief; opioid analgesia (including, for severe head/neck mucositis, sometimes a fentanyl patch or IV PCA) is frequently needed given the severity possible with concurrent chemoradiation. Proactive nutrition support planning (dietitian involvement, sometimes prophylactic feeding tube placement before treatment starts in high-risk head/neck chemoradiation) prevents the weight loss/dehydration spiral that can otherwise interrupt treatment.

Clinical Pearl

Unlike chemotherapy mucositis (which typically peaks and resolves within one cycle), radiation mucositis tends to worsen progressively through the ENTIRE multi-week treatment course and often peaks AFTER treatment ends — proactive, escalating pain and nutrition management planned from the start of treatment (not reactive management once severe) is essential for treatment completion.

References

MASCC/ISOO Mucositis Guidelines (radiation-specific sections); NCCN Head and Neck Cancers guideline supportive care section.

02

Radiation Proctitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Pelvic radiation for prostate, cervical, endometrial, anal, or rectal cancer.

Pathology / Mechanism

Direct radiation injury to the rectal mucosa and its microvasculature — acute proctitis (during/shortly after treatment) reflects direct mucosal inflammation, while CHRONIC/late proctitis (months to years later) results from progressive vascular fibrosis/ischemia and characteristically produces fragile, bleeding telangiectasias rather than active inflammation.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildRectal discomfort, intermittent bleeding not requiring intervention
ModerateModerate symptoms, intermittent bleeding requiring occasional intervention (e.g., topical treatment)
SevereSevere bleeding requiring transfusion, or bowel obstruction/fistula/perforation requiring surgical intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

Acute proctitis is typically managed with dietary modification (low-residue diet), topical/rectal anti-inflammatory agents, and sucralfate enemas (mucosal protectant). CHRONIC radiation proctitis with recurrent bleeding from telangiectasias is specifically well-treated with argon plasma coagulation (endoscopic cauterization of the bleeding vessels) and, for more diffuse/refractory chronic injury, hyperbaric oxygen therapy (promotes angiogenesis/tissue healing in chronically ischemic irradiated tissue).

Clinical Pearl

Chronic radiation proctitis bleeding often does NOT respond to the same anti-inflammatory approach used for acute proctitis, because the underlying pathology (vascular telangiectasia/fibrosis, not active inflammation) is fundamentally different — recognizing this distinction is what correctly points toward argon plasma coagulation or hyperbaric oxygen rather than escalating anti-inflammatory therapy that won't help.

References

ASTRO/ASCRS guidance on chronic radiation proctitis management; hyperbaric oxygen therapy literature for radiation-induced tissue injury.

03

Radiation Enteritis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Abdominal or pelvic radiation fields encompassing small/large bowel (cervical, endometrial, rectal, some GU cancers).

Pathology / Mechanism

Analogous mechanism to proctitis but affecting small/large bowel mucosa broadly within the treatment field — acute enteritis reflects direct mucosal injury and altered motility/absorption, while chronic enteritis results from progressive submucosal fibrosis and vascular injury that can eventually cause stricture, malabsorption, or bowel obstruction years after treatment.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildIncrease of <4 stools/day, mild cramping
ModerateIncrease of 4-6 stools/day, or moderate cramping/pain requiring medication
SevereSevere diarrhea/pain, bowel obstruction, or fistula/perforation requiring surgical intervention
Management & Most Effective Agents

Low-residue/low-fat diet and loperamide for acute symptoms; octreotide for more severe or refractory acute diarrhea. Chronic enteritis with stricture/obstruction may require surgical intervention if severe; hyperbaric oxygen has supporting evidence for some chronic radiation-induced GI injury, similar to its role in proctitis.

Clinical Pearl

Chronic radiation enteritis can present YEARS after treatment completion with vague symptoms (bloating, intermittent obstruction-like symptoms) that get misattributed to unrelated GI disease or cancer recurrence — a remote radiation history should stay on the differential for GI symptoms indefinitely in these patients, not just during the acute post-treatment period.

References

ASTRO supportive care guidance; radiation enteritis chronic management literature.

01

Radiation Pneumonitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Thoracic radiation for lung cancer, breast cancer (especially with internal mammary node coverage), or mediastinal lymphoma; risk increases with larger irradiated lung volume, concurrent chemotherapy, and pre-existing lung disease.

Pathology / Mechanism

Radiation injures alveolar epithelial and capillary endothelial cells within the treatment field, triggering an inflammatory cascade (cytokine-mediated) that typically manifests 1-6 months after completing radiation — a distinctly delayed timeline compared to most acute radiation effects, reflecting the time needed for the inflammatory/fibrotic cascade to become clinically apparent.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildRadiographic changes only, asymptomatic, no intervention needed
ModerateSymptomatic (cough, dyspnea) requiring medical intervention (e.g., corticosteroids)
SevereSevere symptoms with hypoxia requiring oxygen support, or life-threatening respiratory failure
Management & Most Effective Agents

Corticosteroids (typically prednisone, started at a moderate dose) are first-line for symptomatic pneumonitis, with a SLOW taper over weeks to months — tapering too quickly risks a rebound flare of inflammation. Rule out infection and pulmonary embolism before attributing new respiratory symptoms to pneumonitis, since the clinical/radiographic picture can overlap.

Clinical Pearl

The slow corticosteroid taper is not a minor detail — rebound pneumonitis flares from tapering too quickly are a well-recognized pitfall, and patients/providers should be counseled that the taper itself may take considerably longer than the initial treatment course.

References

ASTRO clinical guidance on radiation pneumonitis; NCCN Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer guideline supportive care section.

01

Radiation Cystitis

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Pelvic radiation for prostate, bladder, cervical, or endometrial cancer.

Pathology / Mechanism

Similar mechanism to radiation proctitis but affecting bladder urothelium and its microvasculature — acute cystitis reflects direct mucosal irritation (urgency, frequency, dysuria during/shortly after treatment), while chronic/late radiation cystitis results from vascular telangiectasia and fibrosis, presenting months to years later with recurrent hematuria and, in severe cases, reduced bladder capacity/compliance.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild urinary frequency/urgency, not requiring medication
ModerateSymptoms requiring medication, or intermittent hematuria not requiring intervention
SevereGross hematuria requiring transfusion/intervention, or severe bladder dysfunction (contracted bladder, obstruction) requiring surgical management
Management & Most Effective Agents

Acute symptoms respond to hydration, urinary analgesics (phenazopyridine), and anticholinergics for urgency/frequency. Chronic hemorrhagic radiation cystitis is a primary indication for hyperbaric oxygen therapy (promotes healing in chronically hypoxic, fibrotic irradiated tissue) and can also be managed with intravesical agents or, for severe refractory bleeding, cystoscopic fulguration.

Clinical Pearl

New hematuria in a patient with a distant history of pelvic radiation should prompt consideration of radiation cystitis, but should NOT be assumed without first excluding a new bladder malignancy (radiation itself is a risk factor for secondary bladder cancer) — cystoscopy to directly visualize the bladder is usually warranted rather than presuming the radiation history explains the bleeding.

References

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy literature for hemorrhagic radiation cystitis; AUA guidance on hematuria evaluation.

01

Radiation Necrosis (Brain)

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Brain/CNS radiation, particularly stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) for brain metastases and high-dose conventional radiation for primary brain tumors; risk increases with treated volume, total dose, and re-irradiation.

Pathology / Mechanism

Progressive radiation-induced vascular injury (endothelial damage, vessel wall thickening, eventual vascular occlusion) leads to localized tissue ischemia and necrosis, typically presenting months to a few years after treatment — critically, radiation necrosis is often radiographically difficult to distinguish from TUMOR RECURRENCE/progression on standard MRI, since both can show a new or enlarging enhancing lesion with surrounding edema.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildAsymptomatic, radiographic finding only
ModerateSymptomatic (headache, focal deficit) responsive to corticosteroids
SevereSevere symptoms/mass effect requiring surgical intervention, or life-threatening herniation
Management & Most Effective Agents

Corticosteroids reduce the vasogenic edema associated with radiation necrosis and are first-line for symptomatic disease. Bevacizumab (anti-VEGF) has good evidence specifically for radiation necrosis (targeting the VEGF-mediated vascular permeability/edema driving symptoms) and is used for corticosteroid-refractory or steroid-dependent cases. Surgical resection is reserved for cases with significant mass effect or when tissue diagnosis is needed to distinguish necrosis from recurrent tumor.

Clinical Pearl

Advanced imaging (MR perfusion, PET, or sometimes biopsy) is often needed to distinguish radiation necrosis from tumor recurrence when standard MRI is ambiguous — this distinction fundamentally changes management (bevacizumab/steroids vs. further oncologic treatment for recurrence), so don't assume a new enhancing lesion after brain radiation is automatically progression.

References

NCCN Central Nervous System Cancers guideline (radiation necrosis section); bevacizumab radiation necrosis trial literature.

02

Lhermitte's Sign & Radiation Myelopathy

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Radiation fields including the cervical or thoracic spinal cord — head & neck radiation, mediastinal radiation (lymphoma), or any field with the cord in the beam path.

Pathology / Mechanism

Lhermitte's sign (an electric-shock sensation radiating down the spine/limbs with neck flexion) reflects TRANSIENT demyelination of spinal cord white matter tracts — a self-limited phenomenon typically appearing 1-6 months after radiation and resolving over weeks to months as remyelination occurs. TRUE radiation myelopathy (permanent spinal cord injury) is a separate, much rarer, dose-dependent late effect from vascular/white matter injury exceeding the cord's tolerance, generally avoided by respecting established spinal cord dose constraints during treatment planning.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildLhermitte's sign only — transient sensory phenomenon, no weakness/other deficit
ModerateNot typically applicable to transient Lhermitte's; escalate to "severe" categorization only if a persistent deficit emerges
SevereTrue radiation myelopathy with persistent motor/sensory deficit or paralysis — a rare, largely preventable late effect
Management & Most Effective Agents

Lhermitte's sign requires reassurance and symptomatic management only — it is self-limited and does NOT predict or progress to true myelopathy. True radiation myelopathy has no established restorative treatment beyond supportive/rehabilitative care, which is precisely why strict adherence to spinal cord dose tolerance limits during radiation planning is the operative "treatment" — prevention rather than cure.

Clinical Pearl

Don't let a patient's Lhermitte's sign cause undue alarm about impending paralysis — explicitly counseling that this transient, self-limited phenomenon does NOT predict permanent myelopathy (a separate, dose-limited, largely prevented complication) meaningfully reduces unnecessary anxiety.

References

ASTRO spinal cord dose tolerance guidance; radiation myelopathy/Lhermitte's sign clinical literature.

01

Xerostomia (Radiation-Induced Dry Mouth)

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Head & neck radiation with parotid/salivary gland exposure in the treatment field.

Pathology / Mechanism

Salivary gland acinar cells are relatively radiosensitive; radiation causes progressive, often permanent glandular atrophy and fibrosis with cumulative dose — modern IMRT (intensity-modulated radiation therapy) techniques that specifically spare at least one parotid gland below its functional dose threshold have substantially reduced (though not eliminated) the severity of this once near-universal complication.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild dryness, minimal dietary alteration
ModerateModerate dryness requiring frequent sipping of liquids/oral lubricants, altered diet
SevereSevere dryness with inability to adequately swallow food, requiring feeding tube support
Management & Most Effective Agents

Parotid-sparing IMRT planning is the primary PREVENTIVE strategy and should be prioritized during treatment planning whenever oncologically feasible. Pilocarpine (a cholinergic sialogogue, stimulates residual functioning gland tissue) is the primary pharmacologic treatment for established xerostomia. Saliva substitutes/artificial saliva products provide symptomatic relief. Amifostine (a radioprotectant given before each fraction) has evidence for reducing xerostomia but has fallen out of routine use given its own significant side effect burden (hypotension, nausea) relative to the benefit of modern IMRT sparing techniques.

Clinical Pearl

Xerostomia severity has meaningfully DECREASED in the IMRT era specifically because parotid sparing is now a standard planning goal, not just an aspiration — this is a good example of a technical radiation planning advance directly translating into reduced chronic toxicity, worth explaining to patients anxious about historical accounts of severe radiation dry mouth.

References

ASTRO/IMRT parotid-sparing planning literature; NCCN Head and Neck Cancers guideline supportive care section.

02

Radiation Caries & Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) of the Jaw

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Head & neck radiation including the mandible/maxilla and salivary glands in the field.

Pathology / Mechanism

Radiation-induced xerostomia removes saliva's protective antibacterial/buffering function, dramatically accelerating dental caries; separately, radiation causes direct bone hypovascularity/hypocellularity in the mandible specifically, so that even minor dental trauma or extraction after treatment can fail to heal, exposing necrotic bone (osteoradionecrosis) — a distinct, serious complication from radiation caries though related through the same field.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildDental caries managed with routine dental care, no bone exposure
ModerateLimited bone exposure not requiring surgical intervention, managed conservatively
SevereExtensive osteoradionecrosis requiring surgical debridement/resection, or pathologic fracture
Management & Most Effective Agents

Comprehensive dental clearance and extraction of any high-risk teeth BEFORE radiation begins is the essential preventive step — extractions performed AFTER radiation carry substantially higher ORN risk than the same extraction done first. Fluoride treatment and meticulous oral hygiene reduce ongoing caries risk from radiation-induced xerostomia. Established ORN is managed with conservative measures first (antibiotics, minor debridement) and, for refractory cases, the PENTOCLO protocol (pentoxifylline + tocopherol/vitamin E + clodronate) or hyperbaric oxygen therapy, with surgical resection reserved for severe/refractory disease.

Clinical Pearl

Pre-radiation dental clearance is a genuinely time-sensitive, easy-to-overlook step — a head/neck cancer patient who needs extractions should ideally have them completed and allow some healing time BEFORE radiation starts, not after, and this coordination between oncology and dentistry should happen at the very beginning of treatment planning, not as an afterthought.

References

NCCN Head and Neck Cancers guideline (dental evaluation prior to radiation); PENTOCLO protocol literature for osteoradionecrosis.

01

Lymphedema

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Radiation to lymph node basins (axillary, pelvic, inguinal), especially when COMBINED with surgical lymph node dissection in the same region — the combination carries substantially higher risk than either alone.

Pathology / Mechanism

Radiation causes fibrosis of lymphatic channels and remaining lymph nodes within the treatment field, impairing lymphatic drainage capacity — when combined with surgical removal of nodes (e.g., axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer, inguinal/pelvic dissection for melanoma or gynecologic cancers), the residual lymphatic system has substantially reduced reserve capacity to compensate, and swelling can develop months to even years after treatment.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild swelling, noticeable but not significantly limiting function, minimal limb size discrepancy
ModerateModerate swelling limiting instrumental ADLs, or interfering with function
SevereSevere swelling causing significant disability, or recurrent cellulitis/lymphangitis
Management & Most Effective Agents

Complete decongestive therapy (manual lymphatic drainage performed by a certified lymphedema therapist, combined with compression bandaging/garments and specific exercises) is the evidence-based standard of care. Compression garments for long-term maintenance after the initial decongestive phase. Patient education on infection prevention (meticulous skin care, prompt treatment of any cellulitis in the affected limb) is essential since lymphedematous tissue has impaired immune surveillance and infection risk is both higher and more consequential.

Clinical Pearl

A cancer survivor with a history of nodal dissection + radiation who develops sudden limb swelling, redness, and warmth should be treated urgently for possible cellulitis (lymphedematous tissue is highly infection-prone and can progress rapidly) rather than assumed to simply be a lymphedema flare — infection in this setting is a genuine, sometimes limb-threatening emergency, not a routine chronic symptom exacerbation.

References

NCCN Survivorship guideline (lymphedema section); ASCO/ONS lymphedema management guidance.

01

Radiation-Associated Secondary Malignancy

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Any radiation field, with risk proportional to dose, treated volume, and — critically — younger age at treatment (longer subsequent lifespan for a malignancy to manifest).

Pathology / Mechanism

Radiation-induced DNA damage in cells at the margin of the treatment field (receiving a meaningful but sub-therapeutic dose) can, over a long latency period (often 10-20+ years), result in a new, radiation-induced malignancy distinct from the original cancer — classic examples include secondary sarcomas in the radiation field, breast cancer after mantle radiation for Hodgkin lymphoma, and thyroid cancer after childhood head/neck radiation.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildNot applicable as an acute severity scale — this is a long-term risk-counseling and surveillance issue rather than an acute toxicity
ModerateNot applicable
SevereDiagnosis of a secondary malignancy — managed as its own new cancer diagnosis
Management & Most Effective Agents

There is no acute "treatment" for this risk — management consists entirely of PREVENTION (using the lowest effective dose/volume, proton therapy or other normal-tissue-sparing techniques when appropriate, especially in children/young adults with long expected survivorship) and long-term surveillance per survivorship guidelines for the specific field/organ at risk.

Clinical Pearl

This risk is precisely why proton therapy is preferentially used in pediatric CNS tumors (see the Oncology Interventions Atlas radiation modalities section) — the entire clinical rationale is reducing integral dose to normal tissue specifically to lower this long-latency secondary malignancy risk over a child's full expected lifespan.

References

See Oncology Interventions Atlas (Radiation Modalities section); NCCN Survivorship guideline (secondary malignancy surveillance by prior treatment exposure).

02

Radiation Fibrosis Syndrome

Likely Causative Agents / Modality

Any radiation field, with risk increased by higher cumulative dose, larger treated volume, and re-irradiation of previously treated tissue.

Pathology / Mechanism

Chronic, progressive fibrotic transformation of irradiated soft tissue (muscle, fascia, blood vessels, sometimes nerve) driven by ongoing low-grade inflammation and fibroblast activation that can continue evolving for years after treatment — unlike acute radiation effects that resolve, fibrosis is a genuinely progressive late effect that can cause pain, restricted range of motion, and, when nerves are involved, radiation-induced plexopathy or neuropathy.

Severity (CTCAE-style)
MildMild stiffness/tightness, not limiting function
ModerateModerate restriction limiting instrumental ADLs or causing chronic pain requiring management
SevereSevere contracture/dysfunction significantly limiting self-care ADLs, or disabling plexopathy/neuropathy
Management & Most Effective Agents

Physical therapy (stretching, range-of-motion exercises) started early and maintained long-term is the primary management strategy to slow functional decline, though it does not reverse established fibrosis. Pentoxifylline combined with vitamin E has some evidence for improving established radiation fibrosis (similar rationale to its use in osteoradionecrosis) though effect sizes are modest. There is no agent that reliably reverses established fibrosis, which is why early, sustained physical therapy engagement matters more than waiting for a pharmacologic fix.

Clinical Pearl

Radiation fibrosis syndrome can continue to progress for YEARS after treatment ends, unlike most other radiation toxicities which stabilize — a survivor reporting new, gradually worsening stiffness/pain long after treatment completion should be evaluated for this rather than assumed to have an unrelated new musculoskeletal problem, especially in a previously irradiated field.

References

Radiation fibrosis syndrome clinical review literature; pentoxifylline/vitamin E trial literature for radiation-induced fibrosis.

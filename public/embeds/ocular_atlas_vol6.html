Neonatal Conjunctivitis (Ophthalmia Neonatorum)

Timing of onset after birth is the single fastest way to narrow the differential before any lab result comes back — chemical, gonococcal, and chlamydial causes each occupy a distinct, largely non-overlapping window.

Ophthalmia neonatorum refers to conjunctivitis presenting within the first 30 days of life, acquired either from maternal genital tract organisms during vaginal delivery (gonococcal, chlamydial, herpetic) or from prophylactic topical medication itself (chemical conjunctivitis from erythromycin ointment). Gonococcal conjunctivitis is the most urgent to recognize given Neisseria gonorrhoeae's unique capacity to penetrate an intact corneal epithelium, risking corneal ulceration and perforation within days if untreated.

Risk Factors
  • Untreated or unscreened maternal gonococcal or chlamydial infection at the time of delivery — prenatal screening and treatment is the single most effective prevention strategy, more effective than any neonatal prophylaxis
  • Vaginal delivery (versus cesarean, though transmission can rarely still occur with cesarean if membranes ruptured before organisms were controlled)
  • Lack of, or use of an agent with reduced efficacy for, neonatal ocular prophylaxis (note that none of the routinely available prophylactic agents in the US reliably prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis specifically)
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable to conjunctivitis itself; if herpetic (HSV) etiology is suspected given the risk of disseminated neonatal disease, a broader systemic and CNS evaluation (not a fundus-focused one) becomes the priority.

History
  • Precise timing of onset relative to birth — chemical conjunctivitis (from prophylaxis itself) typically presents within 24 hours and is mild/self-limited; gonococcal conjunctivitis classically presents 2-5 days after birth with acute, severe, copious purulent discharge; chlamydial conjunctivitis presents later, typically 5-14 days after birth, and can be milder and more indolent initially.
  • Maternal prenatal screening and treatment history for gonorrhea and chlamydia, and any known maternal infection status at delivery.
Exam
  • Assess discharge character and severity, lid edema, and specifically examine the cornea given gonococcal disease's capacity for rapid corneal involvement.
  • Full systemic assessment for signs of disseminated infection (fever, lethargy, poor feeding, respiratory distress suggesting chlamydial pneumonia or disseminated gonococcal/herpetic disease).
Imaging/Labs
  • Conjunctival Gram stain and culture (looking specifically for gram-negative intracellular diplococci suggesting gonococcal disease) and chlamydial testing (NAAT/PCR where available) should be obtained for any neonate with conjunctivitis beyond the immediate chemical-prophylaxis window.
  • Consider HSV PCR/culture if vesicular lesions, a dendritic corneal pattern, or any history/risk factor for maternal genital herpes is present, given the urgency of disseminated neonatal HSV.
1
Triage by timing and severity first

Mild conjunctivitis within the first 24 hours, without severe purulence, is likely chemical and self-limited. Any conjunctivitis presenting acutely with copious, severe purulent discharge (classically day 2-5) should be treated as presumed gonococcal until proven otherwise, given the stakes of delayed treatment.

⏰ At initial presentation
2
Hospitalize and treat presumed/confirmed gonococcal conjunctivitis emergently

A single dose of ceftriaxone 25-50 mg/kg (max 125mg) IV/IM (or cefotaxime 100mg/kg as an alternative, notably preferred over ceftriaxone in neonates with hyperbilirubinemia or on calcium-containing IV fluids given ceftriaxone-associated biliary/cardiopulmonary adverse event reports in neonates), plus frequent saline eye irrigation until discharge resolves. Evaluate for disseminated gonococcal infection (sepsis, arthritis) and extend antibiotic duration accordingly if found.

⏰ Immediately on strong clinical suspicion, before confirmatory culture results
3
Treat confirmed chlamydial conjunctivitis systemically

Oral erythromycin 50 mg/kg/day divided into 4 doses for 14 days is the treatment of choice, since systemic therapy is needed given the substantial risk of concurrent nasopharyngeal infection and subsequent chlamydial pneumonia; topical therapy alone is inadequate. Efficacy is only around 80%, so a second course may be needed, and counsel parents specifically about the reported association between oral erythromycin in infants under 6 weeks and infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.

⏰ Once confirmed or strongly suspected based on timing/testing
4
Treat suspected herpetic conjunctivitis as a systemic emergency

Initiate systemic (IV) acyclovir urgently given the risk of disseminated neonatal HSV disease, in addition to topical ophthalmic antiviral therapy and close ophthalmology follow-up for corneal involvement.

⏰ Urgently, on suspicion
5
Manage chemical conjunctivitis supportively

Lubrication and observation; this is self-limited and does not require antibiotic therapy.

⏰ Supportive only
6
Address the broader prevention/public health layer

Ensure the mother (and, as relevant, partner) is linked to treatment for any newly identified gonococcal or chlamydial infection, and confirm the birthing facility's prenatal screening protocols are being followed, since screening and treating pregnant patients is a more effective prevention strategy than any neonatal prophylactic regimen.

⏰ As part of the overall care episode

Complications to Anticipate
  • Corneal ulceration/perforation, essentially unique to gonococcal disease among the bacterial causes given its epithelium-penetrating capacity
  • Chlamydial pneumonia, occurring in a substantial proportion of infants with chlamydial conjunctivitis given shared nasopharyngeal colonization
  • Disseminated gonococcal or herpetic infection (sepsis, arthritis, meningitis, or disseminated HSV) if systemic evaluation and treatment are delayed

  • None of the routinely available prophylactic agents (erythromycin ointment in the US) reliably prevent chlamydial conjunctivitis — prenatal maternal screening and treatment remains the more effective prevention strategy for this specific pathogen.
Evidence Notes
  • Current AAO clinical guidance continues to support universal neonatal erythromycin prophylaxis specifically in regions where gonorrhea prevalence is high and routine prenatal screening/treatment cannot be ensured, while emphasizing that prenatal screening and treatment of the pregnant patient remains the most effective overall prevention strategy.

Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)

The screening timing itself is a clinical calculation, not a fixed calendar date — postmenstrual age, not simply days since birth, drives when the retina is developmentally ready to show disease.

Premature birth interrupts the normal centripetal growth of retinal blood vessels from the optic nerve outward, leaving the peripheral retina (particularly zone I, the posterior pole, and zone II) incompletely vascularized. A two-phase process follows: an initial phase of vascular arrest/growth cessation (relative hyperoxia compared to the intrauterine environment suppresses VEGF), followed after a latent period of roughly 4-6 weeks by a second phase of abnormal, VEGF-driven proliferative vessel growth at the junction between vascularized and non-vascularized retina, which can progress to traction retinal detachment if severe.

Risk Factors
  • Low gestational age at birth (the dominant risk factor; the most immature infants, born before 24 weeks, carry the highest risk of severe, treatment-requiring, and recurrent disease)
  • Low birth weight (typically ≤1500g, or ≤2000g with an unstable clinical course, as a combined screening criterion)
  • Prolonged supplemental oxygen exposure and overall clinical instability (need for cardiorespiratory support) in the neonatal course
Fundoscopic Findings

Classified by zone (I: posterior pole, the highest-risk location; II: mid-periphery; III: far periphery, lowest risk), stage (1: a flat demarcation line between vascularized and avascular retina; 2: a raised ridge; 3: extraretinal fibrovascular proliferation; 4: partial retinal detachment; 5: total retinal detachment), and the presence or absence of 'plus disease' (dilation and tortuosity of the posterior retinal vessels, indicating more active/aggressive disease). Aggressive posterior ROP (AP-ROP) is a distinct, rapidly progressive variant occurring in zone I with prominent plus disease and often minimal demarcation, carrying the worst prognosis if not treated promptly.

History
  • Gestational age at birth, birth weight, and overall neonatal course (oxygen requirement/duration, cardiorespiratory instability, other major neonatal morbidities such as intraventricular hemorrhage) — all relevant to risk stratification and screening urgency.
Exam
  • Dilated indirect ophthalmoscopy remains the gold standard screening technique, performed by an ophthalmologist experienced in ROP examination.
  • Wide-field digital imaging (e.g., RetCam) and teleophthalmology are increasingly used, particularly in resource-limited settings, to document findings and facilitate remote expert grading, though direct exam remains the reference standard.
Imaging/Labs
  • Not applicable beyond the fundus exam itself for staging; this is a clinical, exam-based diagnosis and classification system.
1
Confirm screening eligibility and calculate the correct first exam timing

Standard criteria: birth weight ≤1500g or gestational age ≤30-32 weeks (per local guideline), or selected larger/older infants with an unstable clinical course. The first dilated exam should occur at 4-6 weeks postnatal age OR 31-33 weeks postmenstrual age, WHICHEVER IS LATER — this postmenstrual-age anchor is what prevents screening a very premature infant too early, before the retina is developmentally capable of manifesting disease.

⏰ Calculated at NICU admission, first exam per the above formula
2
Stage and classify at each screening exam

Document zone, stage, and plus disease status at every exam per the International Classification of ROP (ICROP); this classification directly determines both follow-up interval and treatment threshold.

⏰ At every screening exam
3
Determine follow-up interval based on findings

Findings close to treatment threshold (e.g., zone I disease of any stage, or stage 2-3 in zone II) require re-examination in roughly 1 week or sooner; less concerning findings (zone II stage 1, or zone III) can be followed at longer intervals (1-2 weeks or more) per ophthalmology judgment, always accounting for the latent 4-6 week window before proliferative disease can emerge.

⏰ Ongoing, interval individualized to findings
4
Treat 'type 1' (treatment-requiring) ROP promptly

Per ETROP-derived criteria, treatment-requiring disease includes: Zone I ROP with plus disease (any stage), Zone I stage 3 without plus disease, and Zone II stage 2 or 3 with plus disease, or aggressive posterior ROP (AP-ROP) at any zone/stage. Treatment should not be delayed once these thresholds are met, given the risk of progression to retinal detachment.

⏰ Promptly once treatment-requiring criteria are met
5
Choose between laser photocoagulation and intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy

Laser photocoagulation (ablating the avascular peripheral retina to reduce VEGF drive) is effective for stages 1-3 and AP-ROP but carries a real risk of inducing significant myopia (reported in 17-40% of treated children) and permanent peripheral field loss from the ablated retina. Intravitreal anti-VEGF (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept) is increasingly used, particularly for zone I/posterior disease, with a different risk-benefit profile including the need for prolonged follow-up given documented late recurrence risk.

⏰ At the time treatment-requiring criteria are confirmed
6
Extend follow-up significantly longer after anti-VEGF therapy specifically

Given a recognized risk of late reactivation/recurrence after intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment (distinct from the laser experience), current guidance emphasizes 3-5 years of structured follow-up after anti-VEGF therapy rather than assuming treatment marks a definitive endpoint.

⏰ Extended, structured follow-up for 3-5 years post-treatment
7
Escalate to vitreoretinal surgery for progression to retinal detachment

Stage 4 (partial) or stage 5 (total) retinal detachment requires vitreoretinal surgical intervention (vitrectomy, scleral buckle) by a specialized pediatric retina surgeon, with visual prognosis declining substantially at these more advanced stages.

⏰ As soon as detachment is identified
🔧 Procedure: Laser Photocoagulation for ROP
  1. Performed by a pediatric retina specialist, typically under sedation or general anesthesia given the infant's size and the need for a stable, precise procedure.
  2. The avascular peripheral retina (anterior to the ridge/demarcation line) is ablated with laser to reduce the ongoing VEGF drive from ischemic tissue.
  3. Both eyes are typically treated in the same session if both meet treatment criteria.
Contraindications:
  • Active ocular or systemic infection precluding safe sedation/anesthesia.

Clinical Pearls
  • The first screening exam timing formula (4-6 weeks postnatal age OR 31-33 weeks postmenstrual age, whichever is LATER) is calculated, not simply counted from the birth date — getting this calculation right prevents both unnecessarily early exams and dangerously delayed ones.
  • Anti-VEGF therapy is not a 'one and done' treatment the way laser traditionally was thought of — the recognized late recurrence risk means these infants need meaningfully longer structured follow-up (3-5 years) than the treatment episode alone might suggest.
  • Even infants who never develop treatment-requiring ROP remain at elevated long-term risk for myopia, strabismus, and amblyopia and warrant ongoing pediatric ophthalmology follow-up beyond the acute screening period.
Evidence Notes
  • The Early Treatment for Retinopathy of Prematurity (ETROP) study established the 'type 1' treatment-requiring criteria (Zone I any stage with plus, Zone I stage 3 without plus, Zone II stage 2-3 with plus) that remain the current treatment threshold standard.
  • Multiple RCTs (RAINBOW, and aflibercept-vs-laser trials) have established intravitreal anti-VEGF agents as an effective alternative to laser for type 1 ROP, particularly favored for zone I/posterior disease, while documented late recurrence has driven current recommendations for extended post-treatment follow-up.

Abusive Head Trauma: Retinal Hemorrhage Patterns

Getting this exam and its documentation right protects children twice over: it identifies abuse that would otherwise continue, and it protects families from false accusation when the pattern genuinely doesn't fit — both outcomes depend entirely on precise, unhurried, expert documentation.

Abusive head trauma (AHT, the term that has replaced 'shaken baby syndrome' since injury can result from shaking, impact, or both) causes retinal hemorrhage through vitreoretinal traction generated by rapid acceleration-deceleration forces, with a predilection for hemorrhages at sites of maximal vitreous-retina adherence (the vitreous base, and around the macula, where the distinctive traumatic macular retinoschisis described by Greenwald et al. occurs). Retinal hemorrhages occur in roughly 85% of AHT victims, but their number, layer distribution, and peripheral extent — not their mere presence — are what carry diagnostic weight.

In approximately two-thirds of AHT cases, hemorrhages are too numerous to count, multilayered (spanning the nerve fiber layer, deeper intraretinal layers, and sub-internal-limiting-membrane space), and extend from the posterior pole all the way to the peripheral ora serrata — a pattern research has shown to be highly specific for severe abusive trauma and rare in accidental injury, including witnessed short-distance household falls, where hemorrhages (when present at all) are typically sparse and confined to the posterior pole.

Risk Factors
  • Infants under 1 year old are at highest risk, most commonly between 3 and 8 months of age, though the CDC's AHT definition extends to children up to 5 years
  • This is a diagnosis about the perpetrator's actions, not the child's characteristics — there is no patient-side risk factor beyond age; the relevant 'risk factors' are caregiver/environmental (inconsistent or absent history explaining the injury severity, delayed presentation for care, prior child protective services involvement)
Fundoscopic Findings

See pathophysiology above for the full diagnostic pattern. Key exam elements: bilaterality (though hemorrhages can be asymmetric or rarely unilateral), number (few/scattered vs. too-numerous-to-count), layer (single-layer intraretinal vs. multilayered spanning nerve fiber layer/intraretinal/sub-ILM), peripheral extent (confined to posterior pole vs. extending to the ora serrata), and specific severe findings: traumatic retinoschisis (a split within the retinal layers creating a cavity, often with a surrounding hemorrhagic ring) and retinal folds — both considered highly specific for abusive injury when present.

History
  • Obtain the caregiver's explanation independently and in detail; the central diagnostic tension in AHT is a mismatch between the severity of injury found and the trauma history offered (e.g., a history of a minor fall inconsistent with severe multilayered hemorrhages, encephalopathy, and/or fractures).
  • Timing of symptom onset relative to when the child was last reportedly well and in whose care.
Exam
  • A dilated fundus exam by an ophthalmologist with pediatric expertise is essential — direct ophthalmoscopy alone provides only a limited view even with dilation, and indirect ophthalmoscopy is required for an adequate peripheral assessment all the way to the ora serrata.
  • Perform the exam as promptly as possible and consider repeat examination, since retinal hemorrhages can resolve within 7-10 days — a delayed single exam risks missing or under-characterizing the full pattern.
  • Full skeletal survey and skin exam for other inflicted-injury findings: classic metaphyseal lesions, rib fractures (especially posterior/lateral and in different stages of healing), and bruising patterns inconsistent with the developmental stage of the child (e.g., bruising in a pre-mobile infant).
  • Assess pupillary function and mental status; anisocoria, poor pupillary reaction, or mydriasis can indicate severe concurrent CNS injury/depression.
Imaging/Labs
  • Neuroimaging (CT initially, often followed by MRI) to assess for subdural hemorrhage and parenchymal injury — the presence of retinal hemorrhages correlates strongly with positive neuroimaging findings; retinal hemorrhages are rare in children with both normal neuroimaging and no other evidence of head trauma.
  • High-quality fundus photography (e.g., RetCam wide-field imaging) for objective documentation; OCT, where available, can further characterize hemorrhage depth/layer and has been used in research settings to assist with timing/age estimation of hemorrhages, though this remains an evolving area.
  • Exclude alternative medical causes of multilayered retinal hemorrhage before attributing findings to abuse: blood dyscrasias (leukemia, severe chronic anemia, coagulopathy), recent vaginal delivery (birth-related retinal hemorrhage, which is typically limited and resolves within the first days to couple of weeks of life), sepsis, and (rarely) other severe accidental trauma or aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage.
1
Ensure prompt, expert ophthalmologic examination

Involve ophthalmology with pediatric expertise as early as possible given the 7-10 day window in which hemorrhages may resolve; a delayed exam can understate the true severity/pattern of injury.

⏰ As soon as AHT is a clinical consideration
2
Document with maximal precision

A detailed written description remains essential and should not be replaced by photographs alone; a schematic drawing supporting the written description is useful. Document number (or 'too numerous to count'), layer(s) involved, laterality, peripheral extent (specifically noting extension to the ora serrata), and any traumatic retinoschisis or retinal folds.

⏰ At the time of exam, and at any repeat exam
3
Grade overall severity using a structured framework

A three-tier severity framework is used in practice: mild (few intraretinal hemorrhages limited to the posterior pole — suspicious but not diagnostic alone), moderate (falls between mild and severe — highly suspicious), and severe (widespread, multilayered hemorrhages, possibly with retinal folds/schisis cavities — strongly indicative of AHT in the absence of another adequate explanation).

⏰ At the time of documented findings
4
Correlate with neuroimaging and the full-body exam

The overall diagnosis of AHT rests on the constellation of findings (subdural hemorrhage, encephalopathy, retinal hemorrhage pattern, and often skeletal findings) interpreted together with the history, not on the retinal findings in isolation.

⏰ As part of the coordinated multidisciplinary workup
5
Actively exclude medical mimics before attributing findings to abuse

Send appropriate labs (CBC, coagulation studies) to exclude a blood dyscrasia or coagulopathy, and consider the birth history (recent vaginal delivery can cause retinal hemorrhage, though this is typically less severe and resolves within days to roughly 2 weeks) before finalizing the assessment.

⏰ As part of the same evaluation
6
Engage the hospital's child protection team and comply with mandatory reporting requirements

Any reasonable suspicion of abuse triggers mandatory reporting obligations to child protective services per local/state law; this is a parallel, non-optional process alongside the medical workup, not something deferred until diagnostic certainty is reached.

⏰ As soon as reasonable suspicion is present, per mandatory reporting law
7
Communicate findings clearly for medicolegal purposes

Documentation should be thorough enough to stand on its own in legal proceedings, given the frequent involvement of child protective services and, in many cases, the criminal justice system; written notes, digital photographs, and annotated diagrams together support both clinical care and legal review.

⏰ Throughout the evaluation and at its conclusion

Complications to Anticipate
  • Permanent visual impairment from macular involvement, traumatic retinoschisis, or optic atrophy in severe cases
  • The devastating neurologic complications of the underlying abusive head trauma itself (which typically drive mortality/major morbidity far more than the ocular findings)
  • Missed diagnosis with return of the child to an unsafe environment if the retinal findings and full clinical picture are not recognized and appropriately reported
Clinical Pearls
  • The finding that carries diagnostic weight is the PATTERN (multilayered, too numerous to count, extending to the ora serrata), not simply the presence of any retinal hemorrhage — isolated, sparse, posterior-pole-only hemorrhages are a different, less specific finding requiring the rest of the clinical picture to interpret.
  • The absence of retinal hemorrhages does NOT exclude abusive head trauma — a negative eye exam is not reassuring on its own when other clinical findings (subdural hemorrhage, unexplained encephalopathy, skeletal survey findings) are concerning.
  • This exam and its documentation genuinely protects children in both directions: accurate recognition protects a child from returning to an abusive environment, and an equally rigorous, precise exam protects a family from a false accusation when the pattern doesn't actually fit — both outcomes depend on the same careful, unhurried, expert-level examination and documentation.
Evidence Notes
  • Multiple large case series (Morad et al., and others cited in the 2025 AAP/AAO technical report on abusive head trauma) establish that too-numerous-to-count, multilayered retinal hemorrhages extending to the ora serrata are highly specific for severe head trauma including AHT, while cautioning that these features in isolation, without considering overall number and distribution, are not by themselves diagnostic.
  • A study of 190 children evaluated for AHT found all 15 children with retinal hemorrhages had positive neuroimaging, and none of the 85 children with negative neuroimaging had retinal hemorrhages, supporting the strong correlation between retinal findings and underlying structural brain injury in this evaluation.

Retinoblastoma & Leukocoria Differential

Every case of leukocoria is retinoblastoma until an ophthalmologist proves otherwise — the differential is genuinely broad, but the asymmetric stakes (a missed malignancy vs. an unnecessary urgent referral) should always resolve in favor of urgent same-week ophthalmology evaluation.

Retinoblastoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy of childhood, arising from biallelic mutation of the RB1 tumor suppressor gene — either two somatic ('sporadic') hits confined to a single retinal cell line (typically unilateral disease), or one germline plus one somatic hit (heritable disease, which is nearly always bilateral, though roughly 15% of apparently unilateral cases also carry an underlying germline mutation). Leukocoria (a white pupillary reflex, replacing the normal red reflex) is the most common presenting sign, produced by the white tumor mass reflecting light back through the pupil; strabismus is the second most common presenting sign, sometimes preceding leukocoria.

**Leukocoria differential:** A white pupillary reflex in a child is not specific to retinoblastoma, and the differential is genuinely broad: congenital cataract, persistent fetal vasculature (PFV, formerly persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous), Coats disease (an exudative retinal vascular anomaly), advanced retinopathy of prematurity, ocular toxocariasis (parasitic infection), coloboma, congenital CMV chorioretinitis, and rarely orbital cellulitis with secondary changes. Several of these entities (Coats disease, PFV, advanced ROP, toxocariasis) can closely mimic retinoblastoma on both clinical exam and imaging, and misdiagnosis in either direction carries real consequences: an unnecessary enucleation for a benign mimic, or, far more dangerously, a missed malignancy treated as a benign condition.

Risk Factors
  • Family history of retinoblastoma (relevant to the heritable form, though the majority of cases occur without a known family history, as sporadic new germline or somatic mutations)
  • Most affected children are diagnosed before age 5, with heritable/bilateral disease typically presenting earlier (often before age 1) than sporadic unilateral disease
  • For the broader leukocoria differential specifically: prematurity/ROP history, prenatal toxocara (dog/cat roundworm) exposure risk, and congenital CMV infection as relevant alternative-diagnosis risk factors
Fundoscopic Findings

Retinoblastoma classically appears as a white-to-cream, often calcified, mass arising from the retina, sometimes with associated vitreous seeding (tumor cells dispersed in the vitreous) or subretinal fluid/seeding. Unusual presenting fundus/anterior findings can include glaucoma, hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage, or a picture mimicking uveitis or orbital cellulitis — atypical presentations are more common in older children and can delay diagnosis if retinoblastoma isn't kept on the differential for these less classic pictures.

History
  • Age at presentation, family history of retinoblastoma or childhood eye enucleation, and how the finding was first noticed (often by a parent noticing an abnormal 'white reflection' in flash photography, or by a pediatrician/family member observing strabismus).
  • Prematurity/ROP history, known toxocara exposure risk (young child with a dog or cat, pica/geophagia), and congenital infection history relevant to the differential.
Exam
  • Red reflex testing (Bruckner test) is the standard pediatric screening tool and should prompt urgent same-week ophthalmology referral for any asymmetric or absent red reflex, regardless of how confident the primary examiner feels about a benign explanation.
  • Definitive evaluation requires examination under anesthesia (EUA) by an ophthalmologist, given the need for a completely still, fully dilated, detailed exam of the entire retina in a young child — this is standard practice, not an escalation reserved for uncertain cases.
  • Assess for strabismus, and for other physical exam clues to the specific alternative diagnosis (e.g., known prematurity/ROP history, signs of orbital cellulitis).
Imaging/Labs
  • B-scan ultrasound is a key diagnostic tool: retinoblastoma classically shows a retinal mass with characteristic calcifications, a finding that helps distinguish it from most other entries in the leukocoria differential.
  • MRI orbits/brain (avoiding CT when possible to limit radiation exposure in a population at elevated future malignancy risk, particularly with heritable disease) to assess extraocular/optic nerve extension and screen for pinealoblastoma in bilateral/heritable cases (historically termed 'trilateral retinoblastoma' when a pinealoblastoma co-occurs).
  • Genetic testing (RB1 mutation analysis) is pursued for prognostic and family-counseling purposes; tissue biopsy of the intraocular mass itself is generally AVOIDED given the risk of seeding tumor cells outside the eye, so diagnosis relies on the clinical exam plus imaging correlation rather than histologic confirmation prior to any definitive treatment.
  • Emerging technique: molecular analysis of cell-free DNA from aqueous humor can identify RB1 mutations without removing the eye, though this is not yet in widespread clinical use for excluding heritable predisposition.
1
Treat any leukocoria or asymmetric red reflex as urgent until proven otherwise

Same-week (not routine, months-later) ophthalmology referral for definitive examination under anesthesia; the asymmetric consequence of a missed retinoblastoma (metastatic disease, loss of life) versus an unnecessary urgent referral for a benign mimic should always resolve toward urgency.

⏰ Immediately upon identifying an abnormal red reflex or witnessed leukocoria
2
Perform examination under anesthesia with B-scan ultrasound

Confirms the diagnosis, characterizes tumor size/location/seeding, and importantly evaluates the fellow eye and screens for early bilateral involvement.

⏰ Promptly, coordinated with pediatric ophthalmology/oncology
3
Stage the tumor and determine eye-salvage potential

Staging (per the International Classification of Retinoblastoma and/or the AJCC system) and assessment of visual potential in the affected eye determine whether an eye-salvage approach (chemotherapy, focal therapies) or enucleation is pursued.

⏰ At diagnosis, guiding the treatment plan
4
Choose treatment modality based on laterality, size, and eye-salvage potential

Options include systemic chemotherapy, intra-arterial chemotherapy (delivered directly to the ophthalmic artery), intravitreal chemotherapy (for vitreous seeding specifically), focal therapies (cryotherapy, laser, brachytherapy) for smaller tumors, and enucleation for large tumors with poor visual potential or when eye-salvage isn't safely achievable. Modern eye-salvage chemotherapy approaches have meaningfully reduced enucleation rates compared to historical practice.

⏰ Individualized, coordinated by pediatric ophthalmology and oncology
5
Pursue genetic counseling and testing

Determine heritable (germline) versus sporadic (somatic-only) status, since this has major implications for the risk of second primary malignancies (particularly osteosarcoma and other sarcomas, and pinealoblastoma), the recurrence risk in future children, and screening recommendations for at-risk siblings.

⏰ As part of the overall diagnostic/treatment process
6
Establish long-term oncologic and ophthalmologic surveillance

  • A parent noticing an abnormal white reflection in a flash photograph is a classic, real-world way this disease first comes to attention — take this history seriously and refer urgently rather than reassuring based on a single normal-appearing office exam.

  • GeneReviews and multiple case series confirm leukocoria as the most common presenting sign of retinoblastoma, with strabismus as the second most common; roughly 15% of apparently unilateral cases still carry an underlying germline RB1 mutation, underscoring the importance of genetic counseling even in seemingly sporadic unilateral presentations.

Ocular Emergencies in Pregnancy

Preeclampsia-associated vision loss is one of the rare situations in ophthalmology where the correct treatment for the eye is entirely obstetric — delivery of the placenta, not any ocular intervention, is what resolves the underlying process.

Pregnancy induces hormonal, hemodynamic, and metabolic changes affecting nearly every ocular structure, most clinically relevant when preeclampsia/eclampsia is present. Severe preeclampsia can cause choroidal ischemia and vascular dysfunction leading to serous (exudative) retinal detachment — fluid separating the neurosensory retina from the retinal pigment epithelium without any retinal break — via a fundamentally different mechanism than rhegmatogenous detachment covered elsewhere in this atlas. Cortical blindness from posterior cerebral vasospasm/ischemia is a separate, more severe preeclampsia/eclampsia-associated complication with an entirely normal eye exam, analogous to the bilateral occipital vision loss pattern discussed with sudden painless vision loss.

Risk Factors
  • Preeclampsia/eclampsia and HELLP syndrome (hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets) — the dominant driver of pregnancy-specific ocular emergencies, affecting roughly 2-8% of pregnancies with visual symptoms reported in an estimated 25-40% of affected women
  • Pre-existing diabetic retinopathy, which can accelerate/worsen during pregnancy independent of preeclampsia
  • Pre-existing retinal vein/artery occlusion risk factors compounded by pregnancy's relatively hypercoagulable state
Fundoscopic Findings

Serous retinal detachment from preeclampsia/eclampsia: subretinal fluid with an otherwise typically unremarkable-appearing retina (no break, no traction), sometimes with associated subretinal fibrin deposits or intraretinal hemorrhage; choroidal thickening is demonstrable on OCT and correlates with disease severity, decreasing as the systemic process resolves postpartum. Hypertensive retinopathy findings (arteriolar narrowing/spasm, present in a large majority of preeclampsia/eclampsia/HELLP cases, cotton-wool spots, flame hemorrhages) may accompany more severe disease. Cortical blindness presents with an entirely normal fundus and pupil exam despite severe visual symptoms — the mismatch itself is the finding.

History
  • Gestational age, known preeclampsia/eclampsia/HELLP diagnosis or symptoms (headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain, edema), and blood pressure trend.
  • Visual symptom character: blurred vision, scotoma, photopsia, amaurosis fugax, and diplopia are all reported with preeclampsia/eclampsia and don't necessarily localize to a single mechanism without further exam.
Exam
  • Visual acuity, pupil exam (cortical blindness classically shows a NORMAL pupillary response despite severe vision loss, since the reflex arc bypasses the visual cortex), and dilated fundus exam for serous detachment or hypertensive retinopathy changes.
  • Blood pressure measurement is essential and directly relevant to both the ophthalmic and obstetric urgency of the presentation.
Imaging/Labs
  • OCT, where available, to characterize serous retinal detachment and choroidal thickening.
  • This workup runs in parallel with, and is subordinate to, the obstetric evaluation: preeclampsia labs (platelets, liver enzymes, renal function, proteinuria), fetal monitoring, and blood pressure management are the primary and most time-critical priorities.
  • Brain MRI if cortical blindness or any focal neurologic finding is present, to evaluate for posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) or other structural correlate.
1
Recognize that this is fundamentally an obstetric emergency with ocular manifestations

Any new visual symptom in a pregnant patient, especially beyond 20 weeks gestation, should prompt urgent obstetric evaluation for preeclampsia/eclampsia/HELLP alongside the ophthalmic exam — the eye findings are a marker of the systemic disease severity, not a separate problem to solve independently.

⏰ Immediately upon presentation with new visual symptoms in pregnancy
2
Prioritize blood pressure control and obstetric management

Definitive treatment of preeclampsia-associated serous retinal detachment or cortical blindness is delivery of the placenta (timing per obstetric judgment based on gestational age and maternal/fetal status) and blood pressure control — there is no ocular-specific treatment that addresses the underlying process.

⏰ As the primary, time-critical intervention
3
Manage serous retinal detachment conservatively

No specific ocular/topical treatment is required or effective; the detachment characteristically resolves over days to weeks following delivery and blood pressure normalization, generally with an excellent visual prognosis.

⏰ Observed through the postpartum course
4
Reassure regarding cortical blindness prognosis while treating the underlying process

Most cases of preeclampsia/eclampsia-associated cortical blindness resolve, though some patients retain residual visual field deficits; this remains fundamentally an obstetric/neurologic management problem (blood pressure control, seizure prophylaxis/management if eclampsia) rather than an ophthalmic one.

⏰ Through the acute and postpartum course
5
Select ocular medications with pregnancy safety in mind for any coexisting ocular disease

For patients with pre-existing glaucoma or other ocular conditions requiring medication during pregnancy, coordinate with obstetrics on agent selection; some topical glaucoma medications and systemic agents used elsewhere in this atlas (e.g., certain oral medications) carry pregnancy category considerations that should be reviewed specifically rather than defaulting to the non-pregnant standard regimen.

⏰ Throughout pregnancy for any coexisting chronic ocular disease
6
Monitor and manage worsening diabetic retinopathy proactively

Pre-existing diabetic retinopathy can progress during pregnancy independent of preeclampsia; patients with known diabetic retinopathy should have dedicated ophthalmology follow-up scheduled proactively during pregnancy rather than waiting for symptomatic worsening.

⏰ Proactively scheduled early in pregnancy for at-risk patients

  • New visual symptoms in pregnancy, especially in the second half, should trigger preeclampsia screening reflexively — this is one of the clearest examples in this entire atlas of an eye finding whose correct workup and treatment live almost entirely outside ophthalmology.
  • A normal pupil exam with severe bilateral vision loss in a pregnant patient (cortical blindness) is not reassuring — it's the specific pattern pointing toward a posterior cortical/PRES-type process requiring neurologic and obstetric evaluation, not an eye-focused workup.
  • Serous retinal detachment from preeclampsia generally has an excellent prognosis and resolves with delivery and blood pressure control — avoid unnecessary invasive ocular intervention for a process that self-resolves once the systemic disease is addressed.
Evidence Notes
  • Case series and reviews describe serous retinal detachment occurring in roughly 1% of severe preeclampsia and at higher rates in eclampsia, with OCT-documented choroidal thickening that decreases as the systemic disease resolves, and consistently good visual outcomes with conservative management focused on the underlying obstetric process.

Ocular Emergencies in the Immunocompromised Patient

CMV retinitis and progressive outer retinal necrosis look similar on a quick glance but demand almost opposite management urgency — PORN can destroy the retina within days, while CMV retinitis, though still sight-threatening, generally allows a more measured treatment initiation.

Immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS with low CD4 counts, solid organ or stem cell transplant recipients, and patients on significant immunosuppressive therapy for autoimmune disease) are susceptible to a distinct set of opportunistic ocular infections rarely seen in immunocompetent hosts. CMV retinitis, the most common vision-threatening ocular opportunistic infection in advanced AIDS, produces a slowly progressive granular retinal necrosis with hemorrhage and minimal vitreous inflammation, classically following the retinal vasculature. Progressive outer retinal necrosis (PORN), caused by VZV (most common) or HSV, is a distinct and far more aggressive process: rapid progression with early posterior pole/macular involvement, deep multifocal retinal whitening, and characteristically MINIMAL vitreous inflammatory reaction (a key discriminator from typical acute retinal necrosis in a less severely immunocompromised host) — occurring in the most profoundly immunosuppressed patients (classically very low CD4 counts).

Risk Factors
  • CMV retinitis: advanced HIV/AIDS with low CD4 count (historically the dominant population before antiretroviral therapy scale-up); solid organ or hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients on significant immunosuppression; other severe iatrogenic immunosuppression
  • PORN: profound immunosuppression, classically very low CD4 counts in AIDS; prior or concurrent VZV/HSV infection
  • Fungal endophthalmitis: candidemia/fungemia (especially with indwelling central catheters, prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics, total parenteral nutrition, or injection drug use), neutropenia, and recent intraabdominal surgery
  • Severe/atypical presentations of otherwise-common infections (HZO, bacterial keratitis) tend to run a more aggressive, less typical course in this population and should prompt a lower threshold for aggressive workup and treatment
Fundoscopic Findings

CMV retinitis: granular, often perivascular ('pizza-pie' or 'cottage cheese and ketchup') retinal whitening with associated hemorrhage, following a relatively indolent but relentlessly progressive course if untreated; treated/quiescent lesions show sharp demarcation between necrotic and healthy retina with variable pigmentation, and residual lipid/calcification should not be mistaken for active disease. PORN: deep, multifocal patches of retinal whitening starting posteriorly/at the macula, progressing rapidly (over days) with minimal associated vitreous haze despite the severity — this combination of rapid progression, posterior/macular involvement, and minimal vitritis is what distinguishes it from typical (non-immunocompromised) acute retinal necrosis.

History
  • Underlying immunosuppression etiology and severity (HIV status/CD4 count and viral load, transplant history and current immunosuppressive regimen, other relevant systemic disease), and antiretroviral/antirejection medication adherence.
  • Any systemic symptoms suggesting disseminated infection (fever, sepsis) relevant to fungal endophthalmitis risk.
Exam
  • Dilated fundus exam is essential and should specifically characterize the pattern of retinal whitening, its relationship to the vasculature, degree of hemorrhage, and — critically — the degree of vitreous inflammation, since minimal vitritis with rapid, posterior, multifocal whitening points toward PORN rather than typical CMV retinitis or standard acute retinal necrosis.
  • Full anterior segment exam for any coexisting anterior chamber inflammation, keratic precipitates, or signs suggesting an atypical/severe presentation of a more common entity (severe HZO, aggressive bacterial keratitis).
Imaging/Labs
  • Vitreous or aqueous PCR (CMV, VZV, HSV, toxoplasma) can help distinguish among the viral retinitis syndromes and from toxoplasmosis when the clinical picture is ambiguous, though diagnosis is often made clinically given the frequently short timeline available for treatment decisions.
  • Fluorescein angiography can help characterize retinal non-perfusion/vasculitis patterns.
  • Blood cultures and appropriate systemic infectious workup for suspected fungal endophthalmitis or any systemic source.
  • CD4 count and HIV viral load (if not already known) given direct relevance to both diagnosis and overall immune reconstitution planning.
1
Recognize the urgency differential between CMV retinitis and PORN

PORN can progress to complete retinal destruction within days and represents a more acute ophthalmic emergency than typical CMV retinitis, which, while still vision-threatening and requiring prompt treatment, generally progresses more slowly; the degree of vitritis (minimal in PORN) and the rapidity/pattern of progression are the key bedside discriminators driving how urgently to escalate.

⏰ At initial recognition
2
Initiate CMV retinitis treatment promptly

Combined systemic (oral valganciclovir or IV ganciclovir) and, particularly for sight-threatening or zone 1/posterior-involving lesions, intravitreal ganciclovir injection; foscarnet or cidofovir are alternatives for ganciclovir-resistant or -intolerant cases. Treatment alongside optimization/initiation of antiretroviral therapy (or reduction of iatrogenic immunosuppression where feasible and safe) is essential, since immune reconstitution is what ultimately controls the disease long-term.

⏰ As soon as the diagnosis is made or strongly suspected
3
Treat PORN aggressively and immediately with combination therapy

IV acyclovir (often at higher doses than standard) combined with intravitreal ganciclovir injections (frequently bilateral given the high rate of fellow-eye involvement) is standard; despite aggressive treatment, visual outcomes in PORN remain guarded given how rapidly the retina can be destroyed.

⏰ Emergently, given the days-scale progression risk
4
Consider early vitrectomy for PORN not responding to medical therapy

Pars plana vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade, combined with continued intravitreal ganciclovir, has been used successfully in cases of unfavorable response to medical therapy alone, and some case reports support considering earlier surgical intervention rather than prolonged medical therapy alone in progressive cases.

⏰ If inadequate response to medical therapy, individualized timing
5
Maintain a lower threshold for suspecting fungal endophthalmitis in at-risk patients

Any intraocular inflammation in a patient with candidemia/fungemia risk factors (indwelling lines, recent abdominal surgery, prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotics, neutropenia) warrants urgent ophthalmology evaluation and consideration of the same intravitreal antifungal approach described in the Endophthalmitis entry (Vol 1), adapted for the specific organism once identified.

⏰ Whenever fungemia risk factors coincide with new eye symptoms
6
Coordinate closely with infectious disease and the patient's primary immunosuppression-managing team

Treatment decisions (antiviral/antifungal choice, whether/how to adjust background immunosuppression) require close coordination beyond ophthalmology alone, since the underlying immune status is what ultimately determines both acute treatment response and long-term recurrence risk.

Evidence Notes
  • A systematic review/meta-analysis of CMV retinitis antiviral treatment (59 studies, 4501 patients) found IV ganciclovir reduced retinitis progression rate compared to no ganciclovir, and inflammation recurrence was significantly lower in antiretroviral-therapy-treated versus untreated HIV/AIDS patients, directly supporting the combined antiviral-plus-immune-reconstitution treatment approach.
  • Case reports of early pars plana vitrectomy with silicone oil tamponade for PORN not responding to medical therapy describe subsequent improvement in retinal lesion healing, supporting consideration of earlier surgical intervention in treatment-refractory cases.

Geriatric-Specific Ocular Considerations

Anticoagulation reversal for a spontaneous hyphema or vitreous hemorrhage is a genuinely under-studied decision — there is little direct evidence to guide it, which means this is squarely a shared, individualized decision rather than a protocol you can look up.

Older adults carry a distinct risk profile across several conditions covered elsewhere in this atlas, driven by three converging factors: high rates of anticoagulant/antiplatelet use (raising bleeding-related ocular emergency risk), age-related ocular disease (wet AMD, glaucoma) that can present acutely, and increased fall risk (contributing to orbital trauma patterns). None of these represent a new disease process distinct from what's covered elsewhere in this atlas — rather, this entry addresses how age-related physiology and comorbidity meaningfully change risk, urgency, and management nuance for existing diagnoses.

Risk Factors
  • Anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents), disproportionately prevalent in the geriatric population given atrial fibrillation, coronary disease, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis/treatment indications
  • Age-related macular degeneration, particularly the neovascular ('wet') subtype, as the dominant cause of acute-on-chronic vision loss in this population
  • Fall risk from polypharmacy, gait/balance impairment, and sensory deficits, contributing to a distinct pattern of orbital and ocular trauma compared to younger populations
  • Anticholinergic medication burden (common in this population from various indications) as a contributing angle-closure glaucoma risk factor, covered in more depth in Vol 1's Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma entry
Fundoscopic Findings

Findings depend entirely on the underlying process: submacular/subretinal hemorrhage from acute wet AMD (a hemorrhagic, often disc-adjacent or macular lesion in a patient with known or new neovascular AMD, sometimes with an associated pigment epithelial detachment); vitreous hemorrhage obscuring the view in patients on anticoagulation with an underlying retinal tear or proliferative disease (see Vol 1's Sudden Painless Vision Loss entries for full vitreous hemorrhage workup); and standard hyphema findings (see Vol 2) though with the specific anticoagulation-related considerations addressed below.

History
  • Complete anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication history (specific agent, dose, indication, and last dose timing) is essential for any geriatric patient presenting with a new ocular hemorrhage of any type.
  • Known AMD history (wet vs. dry, prior anti-VEGF treatment) and timeline of any new/worsening visual symptoms (sudden metamorphopsia or central scotoma is the classic presentation of acute wet AMD conversion or hemorrhage).
  • Fall circumstances and mechanism for any trauma-related presentation, since falls-related orbital fracture patterns and associated injury profiles can differ from the higher-energy mechanisms more typical in younger trauma populations.
Exam
  • Standard exam per the specific underlying diagnosis (hyphema grading, vitreous hemorrhage assessment, dilated fundus exam for AMD-related hemorrhage) as detailed in those conditions' dedicated entries elsewhere in this atlas.
  • For any spontaneous (non-traumatic) hyphema or vitreous hemorrhage in an anticoagulated patient, assess IOP carefully given the documented association between anticoagulation and both the underlying hemorrhage and secondary glaucoma risk.
Imaging/Labs
  • INR (for warfarin), and consider point-of-care or laboratory confirmation of DOAC effect where clinically relevant and available, though routine DOAC level testing is not widely available or standardized for this indication.
  • OCT and/or fluorescein angiography for suspected acute wet AMD hemorrhage, per ophthalmology.
  • B-scan ultrasound if a dense vitreous/hyphema hemorrhage precludes a direct view, to assess for an underlying retinal tear/detachment (see Vol 1).

Manage the specific underlying diagnosis per its dedicated entry, with anticoagulation status factored throughout

Hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage, and acute wet AMD hemorrhage each have their own full workup/treatment algorithm elsewhere in this atlas (Vol 1, Vol 2) — this entry adds the anticoagulation- and age-specific layer on top of that base management, rather than replacing it.

⏰ Throughout, alongside the underlying condition's own protocol
2
Approach anticoagulation reversal as an individualized, shared decision

There is limited direct evidence specifically guiding anticoagulation reversal for intraocular hemorrhage (hyphema, vitreous hemorrhage); this decision should weigh the severity/vision-threat of the ocular bleed against the systemic thromboembolic risk of reversal, ideally in conversation with the patient, their prescribing physician, and hematology where the situation is complex or the stakes are high on both sides.

⏰ At the time of diagnosis, individualized
3
Recognize acute wet AMD hemorrhage/conversion as a same-day-to-next-day ophthalmology emergency

New central scotoma, metamorphopsia, or acute vision loss in a patient with known or new AMD should prompt urgent (same day to next day) ophthalmology evaluation for anti-VEGF therapy; delayed treatment allows the neovascular process and any associated hemorrhage to progress, with disease severity and hemorrhage size correlating with worse ultimate visual outcomes.

⏰ Per ophthalmology judgment based on hemorrhage size/location and response to initial anti-VEGF therapy

  • A retrospective study of anti-VEGF injection for AMD-associated submacular hemorrhage identified anticoagulant use, larger hemorrhage size, and the polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy AMD subtype as significant risk factors for breakthrough vitreous hemorrhage after treatment.

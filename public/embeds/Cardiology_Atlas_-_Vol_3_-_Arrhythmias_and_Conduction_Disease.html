01 / Arrhythmias & Conduction Disease

Conduction & Arrhythmia Mechanisms

Every arrhythmia in this volume reduces to one of three mechanisms: enhanced automaticity, triggered activity, or reentry. Get these three right and the rest of the volume is pattern-matching.

Normal Conduction Sequence (animated)

The SA node fires fastest and sets the pace under normal conditions. The AV node's physiologic delay isn't a bug — it's what gives the atria time to finish contracting and fill the ventricles before ventricular systole begins.

Animated pulse follows the normal activation sequence: SA node → AV node (delay) → His bundle → bundle branches → Purkinje network. If your browser doesn't render SVG animation, the dashed lines show the same path statically.
Pearl

Every other pacemaker cell in the heart can fire on its own (Purkinje fibers included) — they just don't get the chance under normal conditions because the SA node's intrinsic rate is faster and overdrive-suppresses everyone downstream. This is exactly why escape rhythms in complete heart block are slow: you're seeing whichever backup pacemaker is closest to the block, and the further from the SA node, the slower and less reliable the intrinsic rate.

Three Mechanisms, One Framework

Enhanced automaticity

A focus (SA node or ectopic) simply fires faster than it should — digoxin toxicity, catecholamine excess, ischemia. Usually presents as a gradual "warm-up" in rate rather than an abrupt onset/offset.

Triggered activity

Afterdepolarizations (early = during repolarization, delayed = just after) reach threshold and fire an extra beat. Early afterdepolarizations are the mechanism behind Torsades de pointes in prolonged-QT states; delayed afterdepolarizations are classically digoxin toxicity.

Reentry (animated)

Reentry is the mechanism behind the majority of clinically significant tachyarrhythmias — AVNRT, AVRT, atrial flutter, and most monomorphic VT. It requires three ingredients: two available pathways with different conduction/refractory properties, a unidirectional block in one of them, and conduction slow enough in the other that the blocked pathway has time to recover excitability.

A premature impulse (e.g., a PAC) finds the fast pathway still refractory, conducts down the slow pathway instead, and by the time it reaches the bottom the fast pathway has recovered — allowing retrograde conduction back up and perpetuating a self-sustaining circuit. This slow-fast pattern is the classic AVNRT mechanism, but the same three-ingredient logic applies to AVRT (via an accessory pathway) and scar-related monomorphic VT (via a fixed anatomic circuit around infarct scar).
Why this matters clinically

Understanding reentry explains why vagal maneuvers and adenosine work for AVNRT/AVRT (transiently blocking the AV node interrupts the circuit) but do nothing for atrial flutter or VT (the circuit doesn't involve the AV node at all — it just transiently reveals the underlying atrial activity by slowing AV conduction). It's also why catheter ablation works: physically interrupting one limb of an anatomically fixed circuit eliminates the arrhythmia permanently.

Bradyarrhythmias & AV Block

Not all bradycardia is equal — the location of the block (nodal vs. infranodal) predicts both natural history and urgency far better than heart rate alone.

AV Block Grades & Pacing Criteria

The single most important distinction: Mobitz I is usually a benign AV-nodal phenomenon, while Mobitz II is infranodal disease that can progress to complete heart block without warning. Confusing the two is the most common bradycardia teaching error.

Top: the four AV block grades with their defining PR-interval behavior. Bottom: 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS pacing indications and sinus node dysfunction.
Don't miss this distinction

Mobitz I (Wenckebach) with a narrow QRS is almost always AV-nodal and rarely needs emergent pacing. Mobitz II — especially with a wide QRS — is infranodal, unpredictable, and can progress to complete heart block within minutes to hours. When in doubt about which type of 2nd-degree block you're looking at, treat it like Mobitz II until proven otherwise.

Interactive: Symptomatic Bradycardia — Immediate Management Steps

Step 1 — Assess stability

Hypotension, altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest pain, or acute heart failure signal instability. Unstable patients skip straight to pacing (transcutaneous first, transvenous to follow) — don't wait on pharmacologic therapy to fully take effect.

Step 2 — Atropine 1mg IV, repeat every 3–5 min (max 3mg)

First-line for symptomatic bradycardia. Be aware atropine is often ineffective or can paradoxically worsen block in infranodal disease (Mobitz II, complete heart block with wide-complex escape) — the intrinsic His-Purkinje tissue doesn't have the vagally-mediated responsiveness that atropine targets. Don't delay pacing while repeating ineffective atropine doses in this setting.

Step 3 — Transcutaneous pacing, or dopamine/epinephrine infusion

Bridge therapy while arranging transvenous pacing. Sedate/analgese if the patient is conscious — transcutaneous pacing is uncomfortable. Confirm mechanical capture (a palpable pulse with each paced beat), not just electrical capture on the monitor.

Step 4 — Transvenous pacing / definitive pacemaker

Transvenous pacing bridges to a permanent pacemaker when the indication is durable (complete heart block, Mobitz II, symptomatic sinus node dysfunction) rather than from a reversible cause (electrolyte disturbance, drug toxicity, ischemia expected to resolve with reperfusion).

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/EP for any new Mobitz II, complete heart block, or symptomatic sinus node dysfunction — even if currently hemodynamically stable, given unpredictable progression risk.
ICU admission
Any patient requiring temporary pacing (transcutaneous or transvenous), hemodynamic instability, or an escape rhythm as the only ventricular rhythm.
Transfer if no EP/cath lab on-site
Complete heart block, Mobitz II, or any bradycardia requiring temporary pacing at a facility without 24/7 EP or pacemaker-implant capability — arrange transfer before the temporary pacing wire fails or dislodges, not after.
Transport considerations
Continuous cardiac monitoring and external pacer pads placed and ready (even if not actively pacing) for the entire transport; a physician or critical care transport team for any patient who is pacer-dependent or hemodynamically borderline; confirm transvenous wire position and capture thresholds are documented before transport if a temporary wire is in place.
Who to call
Electrophysiology is the correct consult for isolated conduction disease requiring device therapy; general cardiology if EP is unavailable emergently. Involve cardiac surgery only if bradycardia is a downstream complication of a surgical issue (e.g., post-TAVR, post-cardiac-surgery heart block).
Reversible causes — always screen first

Hyperkalemia, hypothyroidism, medication effect (beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine CCBs, digoxin), inferior MI (RCA supplies the AV node in ~90% of people — expect transient AV block that often resolves with reperfusion), and Lyme carditis (young patient, endemic area, may need only temporary pacing as the carditis resolves) can all produce AV block that doesn't need a permanent pacemaker once the underlying cause is corrected.

03 / Arrhythmias & Conduction Disease

Supraventricular Tachycardias

Three reentrant circuits, one acute-management framework — and one combination (WPW plus atrial fibrillation) that turns a routine SVT algorithm into a potential resuscitation.

AVNRT vs. AVRT vs. Atrial Flutter

All three are reentrant. AVNRT uses dual pathways within the AV node itself; AVRT uses an accessory pathway plus the AV node; atrial flutter is a macro-reentrant circuit that doesn't involve the AV node at all — which is exactly why adenosine behaves differently in each.
The one combination that changes everything: WPW + AFib

When atrial fibrillation occurs in a patient with a bypass tract, the accessory pathway can conduct atrial impulses to the ventricle far faster than the AV node normally would — producing an irregularly irregular, wide-complex tachycardia often exceeding 200 bpm, with real risk of degeneration to ventricular fibrillation. Do not give AV-nodal blocking agents (adenosine, beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, digoxin) in this setting — blocking the AV node removes its competition with the accessory pathway and can accelerate conduction down the bypass tract. Procainamide or ibutilide (agents that slow the accessory pathway) or synchronized cardioversion if unstable are the correct choices.

Vagal Maneuvers — Technique Matters

The modified Valsalva maneuver (standard 15-second strain followed immediately by passive leg raise and supine repositioning) roughly doubles conversion rates compared to standard Valsalva alone in trial data — worth doing correctly before reaching for adenosine.

Adenosine as a diagnostic and therapeutic tool

Adenosine's ultra-short half-life (~10 seconds) makes it uniquely useful diagnostically: even when it doesn't terminate the rhythm, the transient AV block it produces can unmask flutter waves or reveal the underlying atrial rhythm — turning an ambiguous narrow-complex tachycardia into a clear diagnosis in real time on the monitor.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/EP for any WPW pattern discovered incidentally (even asymptomatic) given the small but real risk of sudden death, and for any SVT recurrent enough to warrant ablation discussion.
ICU admission
Hemodynamically unstable SVT requiring cardioversion, or pre-excited AFib/flutter with rapid ventricular response, particularly if antiarrhythmic infusion (procainamide) is needed.
Transfer if no EP on-site
Pre-excited AFib/flutter with a bypass tract, recurrent/refractory SVT being considered for ablation, or any wide-complex irregular tachycardia where the diagnosis (WPW+AFib vs. VT vs. other) remains uncertain after initial workup.
Transport considerations
Continuous monitoring with defibrillator/pacer pads placed; have procainamide or synchronized cardioversion readily available en route given the risk of degeneration to VF in pre-excited AFib — this is not a "stable enough for basic transport" scenario until the rhythm is controlled.
Who to call
Electrophysiology specifically (not general cardiology) for accessory pathway–related arrhythmias, since catheter ablation of the pathway is both diagnostic and curative.

Rate vs. Rhythm Control — Interactive Decision Points

Is the patient hemodynamically unstable?

Hypotension, active ischemia, acute heart failure, or altered mental status attributable to the arrhythmia → synchronized cardioversion now, regardless of anticoagulation status or duration of AF (in the truly unstable patient, the thromboembolic risk of cardioversion is accepted).

Is early rhythm control appropriate?

2023 guideline upgrade: early rhythm control (within ~1 year of diagnosis) is now Class 1 for suitable patients, based on EARLY-AF and similar trial evidence showing reduced cardiovascular outcomes compared to a rate-control-first strategy. This is a meaningful shift from older "rate control unless symptomatic" thinking — consider referral for rhythm control discussion (antiarrhythmics or catheter ablation) earlier than reflexively defaulting to rate control.

Rate control — what target and which agents?

Lenient rate control (resting HR <110) is generally sufficient and non-inferior to strict control for most patients. Beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (diltiazem, verapamil) are first-line; digoxin is a reasonable add-on especially in HFrEF, but is a poor monotherapy choice given lack of efficacy with exertion. Avoid AV-nodal blockers if WPW/pre-excitation is present (see SVT section).

Anticoagulation before elective cardioversion

If AF duration is <48 hours and thromboembolic risk is low, cardioversion can proceed without pre-anticoagulation in many protocols — but confirm against current institutional/guideline criteria, since this is a frequently misapplied rule. If duration is ≥48 hours or unknown, either 3 weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation before cardioversion, or TEE to exclude LA appendage thrombus followed by anticoagulation at the time of cardioversion, is required — and anticoagulation continues for at least 4 weeks after regardless of restored rhythm, given the "atrial stunning" phenomenon that persists after successful cardioversion.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for new-onset AF with rapid ventricular response not controlled by initial IV agents, or any AF with hemodynamic instability requiring cardioversion.
ICU admission
AF with RVR causing hemodynamic compromise, AF requiring IV antiarrhythmic infusion, or AF in the setting of decompensated heart failure/cardiogenic physiology.
Transfer if no EP/cardiology on-site
Recurrent symptomatic AF being considered for ablation, AF with a mechanical valve or complex anticoagulation question, or WPW-associated AF (see SVT section) requiring specialized management.
Transport considerations
Continuous cardiac monitoring; if on an antiarrhythmic infusion (e.g., amiodarone, diltiazem drip), a critical care transport team capable of titrating the infusion; defibrillator/cardioversion capability available en route for any patient with borderline hemodynamics.
Who to call
General cardiology is appropriate for most new AF management; escalate specifically to EP when ablation, complex device interaction, or accessory-pathway-mediated arrhythmia is in play.

Ventricular Arrhythmias

Monomorphic, polymorphic, and Torsades de pointes are frequently used loosely — but each implies a different mechanism and a different (sometimes opposite) treatment.

VT Morphology, VF, and ICD Criteria

Top: the three VT phenotypes and their treatment logic. Middle: VF and the pulseless arrest algorithm. Bottom: 2017 AHA/ACC/HRS primary-prevention ICD criteria.
The Torsades trap

Amiodarone is a mainstay for most VT — but amiodarone itself prolongs the QT interval, making it the wrong choice for Torsades de pointes and potentially perpetuating the very mechanism causing the arrhythmia. If the QT is prolonged, reach for IV magnesium (give it even with a normal serum level — it works on repolarization independent of measured magnesium status), correct potassium to the high-normal range, and consider overdrive pacing or isoproterenol if the Torsades is bradycardia-dependent (pause-dependent).

Interactive: Is This Monomorphic, Polymorphic, or Torsades?

Is the QRS morphology consistent beat-to-beat?

Yes → monomorphic VT, typically scar-related reentry (post-MI scar is the most common substrate). No → proceed to the QT question below.

Was the baseline (or inter-episode) QT prolonged?

Yes → Torsades de pointes. Look specifically for the "twisting" QRS axis around the isoelectric line, and search for a cause: congenital LQTS, QT-prolonging drugs, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, or profound bradycardia. No → polymorphic VT with a normal QT, which is usually acute ischemia-driven — treat the ischemia (consider emergent catheterization) rather than reflexively loading an antiarrhythmic.

Is the patient pulseless?

Yes → this is a cardiac arrest rhythm (pulseless VT or VF): immediate unsynchronized defibrillation plus ACLS. No → assess hemodynamic stability; unstable but with a pulse gets synchronized cardioversion; stable monomorphic VT can be trialed on procainamide or amiodarone while preparing for cardioversion if it fails.

Secondary Causes to Screen For

Structural substrate

Prior MI scar, nonischemic cardiomyopathy, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC — suspect in young patients with VT of LBBB morphology and a family history of sudden death), and sarcoidosis all create fixed reentry circuits.

Reversible/toxic causes

Severe electrolyte derangement (K+, Mg2+), digoxin toxicity, QT-prolonging drug combinations, cocaine/sympathomimetic toxicity, and severe hypoxia/acidosis should all be actively screened for — some VT is entirely reversible once corrected.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Electrophysiology for any sustained VT, and cardiology/EP jointly for any survived cardiac arrest — ICD candidacy assessment should begin during the index admission, not be deferred to outpatient follow-up.
ICU admission
Any sustained VT (with or without instability), post-arrest care (targeted temperature management candidacy), or need for antiarrhythmic infusion.
Transfer if no EP/cath lab on-site
Recurrent or refractory VT (VT storm), suspected channelopathy or inherited cardiomyopathy needing specialized workup, or any post-arrest patient at a facility without cardiac catheterization and targeted temperature management capability.
Transport considerations
Continuous monitoring with defibrillator pads placed and ready for the entire transport; antiarrhythmic infusion capability if VT is recurrent; a physician or critical-care-level transport team for any patient with recent sustained VT/VF given the real risk of recurrence in transit.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for device therapy and ablation candidacy; interventional cardiology emergently if the VT is ischemia-driven and revascularization is indicated.

Wide Complex Tachycardia

VT vs. SVT with aberrancy is a genuinely hard call — and the base rates matter as much as the criteria: roughly 80% of wide-complex tachycardia is VT, rising even higher in patients with known structural heart disease.

The single most important rule in this section

When uncertain, treat wide-complex tachycardia as VT. This isn't just caution for caution's sake — the base rate genuinely favors VT, and the harm of treating VT as if it were SVT (giving an AV-nodal blocker to a rhythm that doesn't involve the AV node, or worse, giving verapamil to true VT — which can cause hemodynamic collapse via peripheral vasodilation without terminating the arrhythmia) is far worse than the reverse error.

Interactive: Brugada Criteria (Step Through in Order)

Work through each step in sequence — the algorithm stops and calls VT as soon as any step is positive. Only reaching step 4 without a positive finding favors SVT with aberrancy.

Step 1 — Is an RS complex absent in ALL precordial leads (V1–V6)?

Look across every precordial lead for any RS pattern at all. If every single precordial lead shows only monophasic (all R or all S) or biphasic-without-RS morphology — no lead has a clean R-then-S — this strongly favors a ventricular origin, since SVT with aberrancy almost always preserves an RS complex somewhere across the precordium.

If absent everywhere → VT (stop here)
Step 2 — Is the R-to-S interval >100ms in any precordial lead?

Measure from the onset of the R wave to the nadir of the S wave in whichever precordial lead has the longest interval. A markedly delayed intrinsicoid deflection like this reflects slow, non-physiologic conduction through myocardium rather than the His-Purkinje system.

If >100ms in any lead → VT (stop here)
Step 3 — Is AV dissociation present?

Look for P waves marching through independently of the QRS complexes, or their electrical equivalents — capture beats (a normal, narrow QRS interrupting the wide-complex rhythm when a sinus impulse "captures" the ventricle) and fusion beats (a QRS that's a hybrid of the wide and narrow morphology). Both are pathognomonic for VT when present, since they require independent atrial and ventricular activity.

If AV dissociation, capture, or fusion beats present → VT (stop here)
Step 4 — Do morphology criteria for VT match in BOTH V1-2 AND V6?

This step requires knowing the specific morphology criteria (R-wave characteristics in V1, qR/QS patterns, R/S ratio in V6) that are beyond quick bedside recall for most clinicians — this is the step most often skipped in practice in favor of just defaulting to "treat as VT" if steps 1–3 were all negative but clinical suspicion remains.

If VT morphology absent in both leads → favors SVT with aberrancy

Supporting Features (Not Part of Brugada, But Useful)

Favors VT

QRS duration >160ms, extreme (northwest) axis deviation, precordial concordance (all precordial leads pointing the same direction), history of prior MI or structural heart disease, and hemodynamic instability all shift the probability strongly toward VT.

Favors SVT with aberrancy

A QRS morphology identical to a known baseline bundle branch block on prior ECGs, young age with no structural heart disease, and a typical right or left bundle branch block pattern (rather than an atypical/bizarre morphology) lean toward aberrant conduction — but none of these rule out VT on their own.

Practical bedside approach

Many emergency clinicians reasonably skip the full four-step Brugada algorithm under time pressure and instead use a simplified, high-sensitivity approach: any history of structural heart disease, hemodynamic instability, AV dissociation, or extreme QRS widening/axis leads directly to treating as VT (procainamide, amiodarone, or cardioversion) without working through detailed morphology criteria. This undertreats the rare true SVT-with-aberrancy case but essentially never misses true VT — an acceptable trade given the relative harms.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/EP for any diagnostically uncertain wide-complex tachycardia, particularly if it recurs or if the patient has known structural heart disease.
ICU admission
Any wide-complex tachycardia requiring antiarrhythmic infusion or cardioversion, or any episode with hemodynamic compromise.
Transfer if no EP on-site
Recurrent wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain mechanism, or any case where empiric VT treatment fails and the diagnosis needs electrophysiology study to resolve.
Transport considerations
Treat as VT for transport purposes if any uncertainty exists — continuous monitoring, defibrillator pads placed, antiarrhythmic or cardioversion capability immediately available throughout transport.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for definitive diagnosis (EP study) and ablation candidacy once the acute episode is controlled.

Channelopathies

Structurally normal hearts, purely electrical disease, and a shared talent for presenting as unexplained syncope or witnessed arrest in an otherwise healthy young person.

Long QT Syndrome & Brugada Syndrome

Both conditions are ion channelopathies rather than structural disease — LQTS affects repolarization (potassium or sodium channels depending on subtype), Brugada affects sodium channel function and produces a specific, diagnostic ECG pattern.
The genotype-specific trigger pattern (LQTS) is clinically actionable

LQT1 events during swimming/exercise, LQT2 events triggered by sudden noise (an alarm clock, a phone ringing) or emotional stress, and LQT3 events occurring at rest or during sleep aren't just trivia — they directly inform counseling (LQT1 patients should generally avoid competitive swimming) and raise suspicion for the specific subtype even before genetic testing results return.

Fever unmasks Brugada

The type 1 Brugada ECG pattern can appear or worsen during febrile illness, and fever-precipitated arrhythmic events are a recognized phenomenon in this population — aggressive antipyretic management in a known or suspected Brugada patient with fever is a genuine clinical intervention, not just comfort care.

Acquired QT Prolongation — The More Common Bedside Problem

Congenital channelopathies are individually rare; acquired QT prolongation from drug interactions and electrolyte disturbance is the far more common cause of a prolonged QT you'll actually encounter. Antipsychotics, methadone, certain antiemetics (ondansetron at high doses), fluoroquinolones/macrolides, and multiple QT-prolonging drugs used concurrently are frequent culprits — check the specific combination against a current reference (e.g., CredibleMeds' QTdrugs list) rather than relying on memory, since risk is often driven by drug combinations rather than any single agent.

Family screening

Once either condition is identified in a patient, first-degree relatives warrant ECG screening (and often genetic testing once a pathogenic variant is identified) — both are heritable, and asymptomatic carriers exist in both conditions.

ICD as definitive therapy

For Brugada syndrome specifically, ICD placement is the only therapy with proven mortality benefit in high-risk patients (spontaneous type 1 pattern plus syncope or documented VT/VF) — unlike LQTS, where beta-blockers meaningfully reduce events in many patients before ICD becomes necessary.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/EP for any newly identified Brugada pattern (spontaneous or fever-unmasked) or any QTc prolongation with syncope, and clinical genetics for suspected inherited channelopathy once the patient is stabilized.
ICU admission
Any patient with a channelopathy who has had a syncopal event with a documented or highly suspected arrhythmic cause, or any Torsades de pointes episode regardless of resolution.
Transfer if no EP on-site
Newly diagnosed Brugada syndrome or LQTS requiring risk stratification and possible ICD placement, or recurrent syncope with a suspected channelopathy not yet definitively characterized.
Transport considerations
Continuous cardiac monitoring for the duration of transport; avoid administering any further QT-prolonging or sodium-channel-blocking medications en route; have magnesium readily available if Torsades is a concern.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for device therapy and risk stratification; clinical genetics/inherited arrhythmia clinic for family cascade screening once the acute issue is addressed.

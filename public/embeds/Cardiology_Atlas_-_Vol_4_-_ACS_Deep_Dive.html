STEMI Equivalents & Mimics

The patterns that deserve a cath lab activation despite not meeting textbook ST-elevation criteria — and the conduction abnormalities that hide ischemia from a standard read.

ECG Lead–to–Coronary Territory Map (animated)

Before pattern recognition, the foundation: which leads correspond to which artery. This matters as much for reciprocal changes as for the primary findings.

Animated highlight cycles through each territory in sequence (inferior → lateral → septal → anterior). The static legend below shows the same grouping if animation doesn't render in your viewer.
Reciprocal changes confirm the diagnosis

Inferior STEMI (II, III, aVF) classically shows reciprocal ST-depression in I and aVL; anterior STEMI shows reciprocal changes in the inferior leads. The presence of appropriate reciprocal change increases confidence that you're looking at true infarction rather than a mimic like pericarditis (which characteristically shows diffuse concave ST-elevation without reciprocal depression, except aVR).

Patterns That Don't Show Frank ST-Elevation

Each of these represents either an active STEMI-equivalent (treat exactly like STEMI) or a critical warning sign of impending infarction (Wellens) — recognizing them prevents a dangerous discharge.

Top: three patterns that don't show classic ST-elevation but carry STEMI-equivalent or pre-infarction urgency. Bottom: modified Sgarbossa criteria for identifying acute MI in the presence of LBBB or ventricular pacing.
Wellens syndrome — the discharge that kills

Patients with Wellens pattern are frequently pain-free and have normal or minimally elevated troponin at the time you're looking at the ECG — because the pattern represents a critical stenosis that hasn't yet fully occluded, not an ongoing infarction. The trap is treating a pain-free patient with a "reassuring" troponin as low-risk. Do not exercise stress test these patients — provoking ischemia in a critically stenosed proximal LAD can precipitate an acute anterior STEMI on the treadmill. These patients need early angiography.

Other Mimics Worth Distinguishing

Pericarditis vs. STEMI

Diffuse (not territorial) concave ST-elevation, PR depression, and absence of reciprocal changes (except aVR) favor pericarditis. Localized convex ("tombstone") ST-elevation with reciprocal depression favors STEMI. When genuinely ambiguous, treat as STEMI and get emergent cardiology input rather than risk missing an infarction.

Benign early repolarization

J-point elevation with a notch/slur at the QRS-ST junction, concave ST-elevation, and a stable pattern on prior ECGs favor this benign variant — but "benign early repol" should be a diagnosis of exclusion in a patient with concerning symptoms, not a default read.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Activate cath lab now
Any de Winter pattern, posterior MI confirmed on posterior leads, or modified Sgarbossa-positive LBBB/paced rhythm with a compatible clinical picture — treat identically to a standard STEMI activation, don't wait for elevation to "declare" itself.
Urgent (same-admission) cardiology consult
Wellens syndrome — this needs early angiography during the same encounter, not outpatient follow-up, even though it may not meet formal STEMI activation criteria at your institution.
Transfer if no cath lab on-site
Any of the above patterns at a non-PCI-capable facility — activate your STEMI transfer protocol exactly as you would for standard ST-elevation, including pre-notification of the receiving cath lab.
Transport considerations
Continuous 12-lead monitoring en route; repeat ECG if symptoms change; have defibrillator pads placed given the risk of malignant arrhythmia with ongoing ischemia.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology directly for cath lab activation; general cardiology is appropriate for Wellens syndrome if same-day angiography can be arranged without a formal STEMI activation.

Reperfusion Strategy

Primary PCI vs. fibrinolysis is fundamentally a time-and-geography decision — the contraindication checklist below only matters once fibrinolysis is actually on the table.

Interactive: PCI vs. Fibrinolysis Decision

Step 1 — Can primary PCI be performed within 90 minutes of first medical contact?

If yes at a PCI-capable facility, or achievable via transfer, primary PCI is preferred — it has superior outcomes to fibrinolysis when performed promptly. If the anticipated delay to PCI exceeds 120 minutes total (accounting for transfer time), fibrinolysis becomes the preferred initial strategy if no contraindication exists.

Step 2 — If fibrinolysis is being considered, are there contraindications? (see checklist below)

Any absolute contraindication rules out fibrinolysis entirely — arrange emergent transfer for primary PCI regardless of door-to-balloon delay, since fibrinolysis carries unacceptable risk. Relative contraindications require an individualized risk-benefit discussion, generally favoring transfer for PCI when feasible in a reasonable timeframe.

Step 3 — After fibrinolysis: assess for reperfusion at 60-90 minutes

Resolution of ST-elevation by ≥50%, resolution of chest pain, and reperfusion arrhythmias (accelerated idioventricular rhythm) suggest successful reperfusion — plan the pharmacoinvasive strategy (angiography within 2-24 hours even if apparently successful). Failure to meet these signs at 60-90 minutes indicates failed fibrinolysis → emergent rescue PCI is indicated, not a repeat dose of lytic.

Rescue PCI ≠ repeat lytic

If fibrinolysis fails (persistent ST-elevation, ongoing pain, hemodynamic/electrical instability at 60-90 minutes), the answer is emergent rescue PCI, not a second dose of fibrinolytic — repeat lytic administration substantially increases bleeding risk without reliably improving reperfusion in this setting.

Fibrinolytic Agent Notes

Tenecteplase has become the most commonly used agent given its single weight-based IV bolus dosing (versus the more complex weight-based infusion regimens of older agents), simplifying administration in the field and reducing dosing errors — an important practical consideration for EMS-administered fibrinolysis.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Immediate cath lab activation
Any STEMI (or equivalent) at a PCI-capable facility, or any patient who can reach one within the guideline-recommended window.
Immediate transfer
STEMI at a non-PCI-capable facility when transfer can achieve first-medical-contact-to-balloon time within the guideline window, or any absolute fibrinolytic contraindication regardless of transfer time (fibrinolysis is off the table, so PCI — however delayed — becomes the only reperfusion option).
Fibrinolysis + transfer (pharmacoinvasive)
When PCI transfer delay would clearly exceed the acceptable window and no contraindication exists — give the lytic, then transfer for angiography within 2-24 hours (sooner if reperfusion fails).
Transport considerations
Continuous ECG monitoring, defibrillator immediately available (reperfusion arrhythmias are common and usually benign but should be monitored), and a clear plan communicated to the receiving cath lab team before departure — including exact lytic agent, dose, and time given if fibrinolysis was administered.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology at the receiving facility as early as possible — ideally with prehospital ECG transmission to allow direct-to-cath-lab routing, bypassing the emergency department entirely when protocols allow.

Antiplatelet Therapy

The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI guideline sharpened P2Y12 selection considerably — this section is built directly from that document's specific recommendations.

P2Y12 Inhibitor Selection

All three inhibit the same receptor but differ in onset, reversibility, and contraindications. The 2025 guideline's core message: reach for ticagrelor or prasugrel first, not clopidogrel, for most PCI patients.
Prasugrel's hard contraindication

Prasugrel is Class 3 (contraindicated) in patients with a history of stroke or TIA — this is not a relative caution, it's a hard stop per the 2025 guideline. Ticagrelor remains an option in this population when a potent P2Y12 inhibitor is still preferred.

Situational Selection Logic

STEMI treated with fibrinolysis

Give clopidogrel specifically alongside the fibrinolytic — this is a distinct recommendation from PCI-treated ACS, where ticagrelor/prasugrel are preferred. Ticagrelor and prasugrel haven't been established as safe/effective in the fibrinolysis-treated population the way clopidogrel has.

Delayed angiography >24h (NSTE-ACS)

Upstream clopidogrel or ticagrelor may be considered to reduce MACE while awaiting angiography — this differs from the immediate-PCI scenario where the preference is more clearly stated toward ticagrelor/prasugrel.

De-escalation timing matters

Switching from ticagrelor/prasugrel down to clopidogrel is a reasonable bleeding-reduction strategy — but not in the first 30 days after the ACS event. Early de-escalation during the highest-risk window for recurrent ischemic events is specifically not recommended; this is a later (typically post-30-day) adjustment.

Lipid-lowering runs alongside antiplatelet therapy

The 2025 guideline reinforces high-intensity statin for all ACS patients with the option to add ezetimibe concurrently at the outset — and explicitly supports adding a non-statin agent (ezetimibe, a PCSK9 inhibitor, inclisiran, or bempedoic acid) if LDL-C remains ≥70 mg/dL on maximally tolerated statin, with further intensification reasonable even in the 55-70 mg/dL range in this high-risk population. This aligns with the 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline goals covered in Vol. 1a.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for any complex antiplatelet decision — recent stroke/TIA with need for potent P2Y12 inhibition, concurrent anticoagulation requirement, or upcoming non-cardiac surgery requiring perioperative bridging decisions.
Involve cardiac surgery early
Any patient on DAPT who may need CABG — communicate exact agent and last dose time immediately so hold-time planning (5/7/3-5 days for clopidogrel/prasugrel/ticagrelor) can begin without unnecessary delay to revascularization.
Transfer considerations
Ensure the exact P2Y12 agent, loading dose, and administration time travel with the patient in written form — this materially changes downstream antiplatelet and surgical timing decisions at the receiving facility.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for real-time agent selection during active PCI planning; general cardiology or the patient's outpatient cardiologist for long-term DAPT duration and de-escalation timing decisions.

Anticoagulation Strategy

Four agents with overlapping but distinct roles — plus the single most consequential shift in ACS anticoagulation practice over the past decade: getting out from under prolonged triple therapy.

Choosing an Anticoagulant

Top: the four anticoagulants used in ACS and their key differentiators. Bottom: the shift from prolonged triple therapy to early dual-therapy in AFib patients undergoing PCI.
Don't cross over between UFH and enoxaparin

Switching anticoagulant classes mid-treatment (starting UFH then switching to enoxaparin, or vice versa) is associated with increased bleeding risk without a clear offsetting benefit — pick one strategy at the outset and stay with it through the index hospitalization when possible.

Quick Selection Logic

Planned early invasive strategy

UFH is often preferred — its short half-life and full reversibility with protamine make it easy to manage around an imminent catheterization, and it avoids the renal-adjustment complexity of enoxaparin in a patient whose renal function may still be in flux.

Conservative/medical management

Enoxaparin or fondaparinux are reasonable — fondaparinux in particular carries the lowest bleeding risk of the four agents, which matters most in a patient not headed to the cath lab imminently.

Triple Therapy → Dual Therapy: What Changed

For years, standard practice for a patient with AFib undergoing PCI was aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor + oral anticoagulant, often continued for months. Four major trials changed that.

TrialKey finding
WOESTDropping aspirin (warfarin + clopidogrel only) reduced bleeding without increasing thrombotic events
PIONEER AF-PCIRivaroxaban-based dual/reduced-dose strategies reduced bleeding compared to standard triple therapy
RE-DUAL PCIDabigatran + P2Y12 (dual) reduced bleeding vs. warfarin-based triple therapy
AUGUSTUSApixaban + P2Y12 (no aspirin) had the lowest bleeding rates among the strategies tested, without excess ischemic events
Practical takeaway

The current default: brief triple therapy (often just through the hospitalization), then drop aspirin and continue the anticoagulant plus a single P2Y12 inhibitor — preferably clopidogrel — for the remainder of the intended antiplatelet course. A DOAC is generally preferred over warfarin in this combination unless there's a specific reason warfarin is required (mechanical valve, certain antiphospholipid syndrome presentations).

Individualize the exact aspirin discontinuation timing

The 1-4 week window for aspirin discontinuation after PCI isn't one-size-fits-all — a patient with a complex stenting result, recent stent thrombosis history, or very high thrombotic risk may warrant staying closer to the 4-week end (or occasionally longer per specialist judgment), while a straightforward PCI result in a patient at high bleeding risk may warrant stopping aspirin closer to the 1-week end.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for any AFib+PCI patient to establish the anticoagulation/antiplatelet combination and duration plan before discharge — this should be an explicit, documented plan, not an implicit continuation of whatever was started acutely.
Pharmacy/cardiology co-management
Renal function trending in a patient on enoxaparin, or any complex multi-drug bleeding-risk assessment (anticoagulant + DAPT + additional risk factors like recent GI bleed) benefits from structured pharmacy involvement alongside cardiology.
Transfer considerations
Document the exact anticoagulant, dose, and last administration time clearly in transfer paperwork — timing relative to a planned procedure (PCI, surgery) at the receiving facility depends on this information being accurate and immediately available.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for anticoagulant selection around active PCI; general cardiology or the patient's anticoagulation clinic for long-term triple-to-dual therapy transition planning.

Right Ventricular MI

Roughly one-third of inferior MIs involve meaningful RV infarction — and the standard ACS reflexes (nitrates for chest pain, diuretics for any hint of congestion) can actively harm these patients.

Preload-Dependent Physiology

Top: why RV infarction creates a preload-dependent circulation. Middle: the diagnostic approach (right-sided leads, classic triad). Bottom: the management pitfalls that make this a genuine "don't miss."
The single highest-yield teaching point in this section

A patient with an inferior STEMI who becomes hypotensive after receiving sublingual nitroglycerin has RV MI until proven otherwise. This is one of the most common preventable iatrogenic complications in ACS management — check right-sided leads before reaching for nitrates in any inferior MI, not after the patient crashes.

Putting It Together at the Bedside

Recognize early

Inferior STEMI + hypotension + clear lungs + elevated JVP is close to pathognomonic even before right-sided leads confirm it. Get V4R on every inferior MI as a reflexive habit, the same way you'd reflexively get a rhythm strip.

Treat the physiology, not the reflex

Fluid boluses first, inotropes (dobutamine) if hypotension persists despite adequate preload, and emergent reperfusion as the definitive treatment — the same reperfusion pathway as any other STEMI, just with a very different supportive-care approach in the meantime.

AV block co-travels with RV MI

Because the RCA also supplies the AV node in most people, proximal RCA occlusion causing RV MI frequently coexists with transient AV block — don't be surprised to see both, and remember that the AV block usually improves with reperfusion rather than requiring permanent pacing (see Vol. 3's Bradyarrhythmias section for the broader AV block framework).

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/interventional cardiology immediately for any confirmed RV MI — this changes both hemodynamic management and reperfusion urgency framing.
ICU admission
Any RV MI with hypotension, need for fluid resuscitation titration, or inotropic support — these patients need closer hemodynamic monitoring than a routine STEMI.
Transfer if no cath lab on-site
RV MI at a non-PCI-capable facility follows the same STEMI transfer urgency as any other territory — communicate the RV involvement specifically so the receiving team anticipates the preload-dependent physiology.
Transport considerations
IV access for fluid administration secured before transport; avoid nitrates/opioids for pain control en route if RV MI is suspected (consider alternative analgesia); hemodynamic monitoring throughout given the risk of preload-sensitive decompensation with positioning changes or vibration/movement during transport.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for emergent reperfusion — the definitive treatment is the same as any STEMI, but pre-notify the receiving team about the RV involvement so they're prepared for potential hemodynamic instability during and after the procedure.

Special Populations

Four patterns that change either the recognition or the management of ACS meaningfully enough to warrant a reflexive mental checklist.

Each population shifts either the presentation, the risk profile, or the treatment approach in a specific, actionable way — not just generically "higher risk."

Women — Presentation and SCAD

Atypical presentation in women is well documented, but framing it purely as "women present atypically" undersells the actionable point: dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, and back or jaw pain are anginal equivalents, not atypical noise to be discounted. The other critical pattern to know: spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) disproportionately affects women, particularly in the peripartum/postpartum period, and represents a fundamentally different mechanism (an intimal tear within the coronary artery wall itself, not atherosclerotic plaque rupture) that changes the approach to revascularization — conservative management is often preferred over PCI in stable SCAD, since instrumentation can propagate the dissection.

Diabetics — Silent Ischemia

Autonomic neuropathy blunts the anginal warning system in longstanding diabetes, so ischemia can present as nothing more than fatigue, malaise, or unexplained glycemic instability. Combined with diabetes automatically qualifying as very-high-risk for lipid goals (Vol. 1a), the practical message is a lower threshold for cardiac workup in a diabetic patient with vague symptoms than you'd apply to a non-diabetic patient with the same complaint.

Cocaine-Associated Chest Pain

The pharmacology here directly changes first-line therapy. Cocaine causes both direct coronary vasospasm and accelerated atherosclerosis with chronic use — but the acute management pitfall is reaching for a beta-blocker the way you would for any other chest pain presentation.

Why beta-blockers are dangerous here

Cocaine causes both alpha- and beta-adrenergic stimulation. Beta-blockade leaves the alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed, which can worsen coronary vasospasm and paradoxically increase blood pressure and ischemia. Benzodiazepines (treating the underlying sympathetic surge), nitrates, and aspirin are first-line; calcium channel blockers or phentolamine (alpha-blockade) are reasonable for persistent hypertension or suspected ongoing vasospasm.

CKD / Dialysis Patients

Two practical issues dominate: anticoagulant dosing and troponin interpretation. Enoxaparin requires renal dose adjustment below a CrCl of 30, and many clinicians default to UFH in this population specifically to avoid drug accumulation and unpredictable anticoagulation effect. Separately, chronically elevated baseline troponin (reduced renal clearance of the protein, not necessarily active cardiac injury) means the diagnostic emphasis shifts to a clear rise-and-fall pattern on serial measurement rather than any single absolute value — a stably elevated troponin without a dynamic change is a chronic renal finding, not evidence of acute MI.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for any suspected SCAD (imaging characteristics differ from atherosclerotic disease and may need a specific interventional approach or conservative management decision), and toxicology/cardiology jointly for cocaine-associated ACS with refractory hypertension or suspected ongoing vasospasm.
ICU admission
Any CKD/dialysis patient with ACS and hemodynamic instability, given the narrower margin for fluid and medication dosing errors in this population; cocaine-associated ACS with persistent hypertensive emergency features.
Transfer if no interventional cardiology on-site
Suspected SCAD needing specialized imaging interpretation and a nuanced revascularization-vs-conservative decision; any diabetic or CKD patient with ACS requiring emergent PCI at a facility without that capability, following standard STEMI/high-risk NSTE-ACS transfer criteria.
Transport considerations
For CKD/dialysis patients, communicate dialysis schedule and last session timing to the receiving facility — this affects both fluid management and timing of any contrast-requiring procedure. For cocaine-associated presentations, avoid beta-blockers throughout transport and ensure benzodiazepines are available if agitation or recurrent hypertension develops.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for SCAD (specialized decision-making around intervention vs. conservative management) and for standard high-risk ACS in any of these populations; nephrology co-management for CKD/dialysis patients around contrast timing and anticoagulant dosing.

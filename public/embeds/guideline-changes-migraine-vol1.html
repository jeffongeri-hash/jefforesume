What Changed · Published Jan 2026

The 2026 AHS ED migraine guideline rewrote the parenteral playbook

Ten years after the 2016 American Headache Society (AHS) guideline, a new evidence assessment — the first to formally grade nerve blocks alongside drugs — upgraded prochlorperazine to a hard "must offer," hardened the case against IV hydromorphone, and folded greater occipital nerve blocks into standard ED migraine care for the first time.

01 · Overview

At a glance

Six categories of change separate the 2026 guideline from 2016. Every recommendation below is graded using the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) classification process — number and class of supporting studies, effect size, consistency, and adverse events — not expert consensus alone.

1
Recommendation upgraded (B → A): prochlorperazine
1
Restriction hardened to "must not offer": hydromorphone
3
New agents/modalities graded for the first time: GONB, SONB, eptinezumab
1
Split recommendation: dexamethasone (recurrence vs. acute)
⚡ Why this update exists

The 2016 guideline evaluated drugs only. Since then, RCTs of peripheral nerve blocks in ED populations accumulated and parenteral CGRP antagonists emerged as a drug class — neither existed as a gradable evidence base in 2016. The 2026 update is the first ED migraine guideline anywhere to formally harmonize nerve blocks and pharmacotherapy in a single recommendation framework.

03 · Deep Dive

Why prochlorperazine — not metoclopramide — is now the must-offer agent

Prochlorperazine IV A · Must offer

Upgraded from Level B (2016) to Level A (2026)

Both dopamine antagonists remain effective. The upgrade reflects consistency and quality of evidence, not clinical superiority in every head-to-head trial — a distinction worth carrying into how you explain the change to colleagues who reach for metoclopramide out of habit.

Metoclopramide IVProchlorperazine IV
Evidence volumeMany studies, heterogeneous designsFewer studies, more consistent quality
Placebo-controlled clarityMixed; ≥1 Class I trial missed primary endpointClearer, reproducible benefit
Head-to-head performanceOften similar to comparators, sometimes underperformsStrong comparative performance

✓ Verify before use
  • Prochlorperazine 10 mg IV is co-administered with diphenhydramine 25 mg IV in the pivotal trials to prevent akathisia — confirm your institutional order set pairs them.
  • If prochlorperazine is unavailable on formulary, metoclopramide IV remains an appropriate Level B substitute — this is an explicit "practical takeaway" from guideline authors, not an off-label workaround.
04 · Deep Dive

Nerve blocks enter the ED algorithm for the first time

Greater occipital nerve block (GONB) A · Must offer new in 2026

Not evaluated in 2016 — first-ever ED migraine guideline to formally grade a procedural intervention

The Level A grade rests on three positive Class I RCTs (two sham-controlled) plus supportive Class II evidence:

StudyComparatorResult
Friedman 2018ShamGONB superior
Friedman 2020Metoclopramide IVInferior overall; comparable when performed by experienced injectors
Hokenek 2021ShamGONB superior
Korucu 2018Dexketoprofen + metoclopramide vs. shamGONB superior

Supraorbital nerve block (SONB) — Level B, should offer — was evaluated in one Class I study; superior to sham, more effective when combined with GONB. Sphenopalatine ganglion (SPG) block remains Level U (no recommendation): a Class II outpatient study using the TX360 device beat sham at 30 minutes, but a Class III ED study of intranasal lidocaine + metoclopramide showed no benefit over metoclopramide alone, and it's often unclear whether the block itself, or simple maxillary-nerve anesthesia from intranasal lidocaine, drives any effect.

✓ Verify before use
  • The Friedman 2020 trial found GONB's real-world performance is operator-dependent — the "inferior overall, comparable among experienced injectors" finding argues for deliberate technique practice, not routine use by unpracticed hands under a blanket "must offer."
  • Standard risks apply: pain, bleeding, infection, transient numbness, and rare vasovagal response. Review anticoagulant status and local infection before injecting.
05 · Deep Dive · Safety-Critical

Hydromorphone: from "avoid" to a hard must-not-offer

Hydromorphone IV A · Must NOT offer

2016: discouraged by expert opinion · 2026: formally graded Level A on Class I RCT evidence

The single trial driving this grade is the Friedman 2017 Neurology RCT (127 patients, 2 NYC EDs) comparing hydromorphone 1 mg IV against prochlorperazine 10 mg IV + diphenhydramine 25 mg IV. The trial was halted early by the data monitoring committee after a planned interim analysis.

The authors' own conclusion: IV hydromorphone is substantially less effective than IV prochlorperazine for acute migraine and should not be used as first-line therapy. They were explicit that this is an efficacy finding, not a claim about addiction risk from single ED doses.

⚠ Safety context: opioid utilization gap

Despite the 2016 guideline already discouraging opioids, retrospective ED practice-pattern data (NHAMCS-based, pre-dating this update) found narcotics were used in over half of ED migraine visits — evidence of a persistent gap between guideline and practice that the 2026 "must not offer" language is explicitly designed to close. Migraine drives roughly 1.2 million ED visits annually in older US survey data, within a broader pool the AHS now cites as 3.5 million annual headache-related ED visits, of which migraine accounts for at least a quarter — figures from different data eras, cited here for order-of-magnitude context rather than a precise current count.

06 · Deep Dive

Dexamethasone: one drug, two now-distinct indications

Dexamethasone IV B · Should offer recurrence prevention  +  C · May offer acute treatment

2016 graded recurrence prevention only (Level B) · 2026 adds a separate, weaker grade for acute pain treatment

Dexamethasone's role in reducing headache relapse after ED discharge is unchanged and well established (Level B). What's new is a distinct evaluation of whether it treats the acute attack itself — evidence for that indication is mixed:

Study (class)ComparatorFinding
Karimi 2017 (Class I)Sodium valproate 400 mg IVComparable efficacy
Mazaheri 2015 (Class II)Sodium valproate 400 mg IVComparable efficacy
Khazaei 2019 (Class III)Chlorpromazine, ketorolac, metoclopramideComparable efficacy

Because valproate itself is judged likely inferior to the anti-dopaminergics, "comparable to valproate" is a modest signal — hence Level C, not B, for the acute-treatment indication.

07 · Deep Dive

The first parenteral CGRP antagonist ever graded for ED use

Eptinezumab IV U · No ED-specific recommendation new in 2026

Four CGRP monoclonal antibodies are FDA-approved for migraine prevention (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab, eptinezumab); eptinezumab is the only IV formulation, making it the only one testable as an ED acute treatment.
23.5%
2-hour headache freedom, eptinezumab (n=480 total, RCT)
12.0%
2-hour headache freedom, placebo

Eptinezumab beat placebo for acute headache freedom — but the trial was conducted in the outpatient setting, not the ED. The panel rated it Level B only for patients matching that outpatient trial population, and Level U (no recommendation) for a general ED population pending ED-specific trials. This is the guideline's clearest "watch this space" signal — expect it to be reassessed at the next update cycle.

08 · Interpretation Note

Don't misread the diphenhydramine grade

⚡ Two different questions, one drug

Diphenhydramine IV as a stand-alone migraine treatment is graded Level C — may not offer. That is not the same question as whether to co-administer it with prochlorperazine.

Diphenhydramine 25 mg IV given alongside prochlorperazine to prevent akathisia is the regimen used in the very trials that earned prochlorperazine its Level A grade (including the pivotal Friedman 2017 hydromorphone comparison), and is separately supported by a dedicated RCT (Vinson & Drotts, 2001) showing it reduces prochlorperazine-induced akathisia. Stopping the akathisia premedication because "diphenhydramine is a may-not-offer drug" would misapply the guideline to the wrong clinical question.

09 · Safety Flag

Droperidol made the "may offer" list — the QT warning didn't go away

Droperidol IV C · May offer

Unchanged tier from 2016; flagged here because a Level C inclusion can read as tacit reassurance
⚠ Black-box warning still applies

Droperidol carries an FDA black-box warning for dose-dependent QT prolongation and torsades de pointes. A "may offer" evidence grade for migraine efficacy says nothing about waiving standard droperidol precautions — baseline and post-dose ECG consideration, electrolyte review, and caution with other QT-prolonging agents remain appropriate regardless of what the migraine literature shows.

10 · Methodology

How the panel got here

Six authors — four neurologists, two headache-focused emergency physicians, split evenly between the US and Canada — conducted a systematic review registered with PROSPERO and reported per PRISMA, reviewed and formally affirmed by the American Academy of Neurology (AAN) Quality Committee's Affirmation of Value process.

26 RCTs
Met inclusion criteria
13 / 8 / 5
Class I / Class II / Class III studies
Through 2/10/25
MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP searched
No pooled MA
No intervention had ≥2 studies meeting pre-specified pooling criteria

Scope was restricted to adults (any ICHD version), parenteral therapies only (drugs + nerve blocks), RCTs reporting outcomes within 6 hours, and comparators of placebo, sham, or another parenteral therapy. Studies already included in the 2016 guideline were excluded — every citation here reflects genuinely new evidence accrued between 2016 and February 2025.

11 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Reach for prochlorperazine 10 mg IV + diphenhydramine 25 mg IV first-line; use metoclopramide when prochlorperazine isn't available — both remain guideline-concordant.
  • Don't reach for hydromorphone as a migraine analgesic. If a patient specifically has a documented history of anti-dopaminergic non-response, that's a different clinical question than routine first-line care.
  • Build institutional comfort with GONB technique — it's now a must-offer option, and its real-world performance is operator-dependent.
  • Use dexamethasone for recurrence prevention as before; treat its acute-pain benefit as modest, not primary.
  • Don't discontinue diphenhydramine premedication for akathisia prevention based on its unrelated Level C grade as a stand-alone agent.
  • If you use droperidol, keep your QT precautions exactly as they were — nothing here changes that risk.

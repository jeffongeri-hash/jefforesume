🌬️ RSI Sequence Overview

The companion piece to the Vasopressor and Fluid Resuscitation Atlases: full RSI pharmacology, a difficult airway framework, post-intubation management, and induction-agent selection by special population — built around the evidence that has shifted meaningfully in just the last 1–2 years (the 2024 PREOXI trial, the 2023 DEVICE trial, and the 2025 RSI ketamine-vs-etomidate trial all directly inform current practice here).

🫁 Preoxygenation & Apneic Oxygenation

Preoxygenation replaces the nitrogen in the functional residual capacity with oxygen, creating a reservoir that extends the time available between the onset of paralysis-induced apnea and clinically significant desaturation. The goal is denitrogenation, not just a higher pulse-ox reading at the start.

Methods

Non-rebreather mask / bag-mask device

Traditional default — simple, low aspiration risk, delivers up to ~100% FiO2 with a good seal, but ceiling-limited compared to positive-pressure options in patients with significant shunt physiology or poor respiratory effort.

Noninvasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP)

Adds positive pressure to overcome shunt and recruit atelectatic alveoli — now preferred by trial data over a simple oxygen mask for critically ill adults specifically (see PREOXI below).

Apneic oxygenation (nasal cannula during the apneic period)

High-flow nasal cannula (or standard NC at 15 L/min) left running through the paralysis-to-tube window — doesn't replace preoxygenation but extends the safe apnea window once it begins, via passive oxygen diffusion (aventilatory mass flow) even without active breathing.

⚡ PREOXI Trial (2024)
The PREOXI trial (Gibbs 2024, NEJM, n=1,301 critically ill ED/ICU adults) found preoxygenation with noninvasive ventilation resulted in significantly lower hypoxemia during intubation than an oxygen mask: 9.1% (57/624) vs. 18.5% (118/637) — a striking absolute difference of 9.4 percentage points (95% CI ⋅−13.2 to −5.6, p<0.001). Cardiac arrest during the peri-intubation period was also lower with NIV (0.2% vs. 1.1%). This is a genuinely practice-changing, current (2024) result: noninvasive ventilation should be the default preoxygenation method for critically ill adults when available and not contraindicated, not simply an alternative to an oxygen mask.
Clinical Bottom Line
For a critically ill patient being intubated in the ED or ICU, reach for NIV/BiPAP preoxygenation rather than a plain non-rebreather mask as the new evidence-based default — and keep apneic oxygenation running via nasal cannula through the entire paralysis-to-tube window regardless of which primary preoxygenation method you used.

💉 Induction Agents

Etomidate

Amidate
Induction agent — imidazole derivative
Dose
0.3 mg/kg IV
Onset
15–45 seconds
Duration
3–12 minutes
Physiology

Enhances GABA-A receptor activity, producing rapid hypnosis with minimal effect on sympathetic tone — the basis of its long-standing reputation as the most hemodynamically neutral induction agent, valuable in patients who cannot tolerate a drop in blood pressure.

Benefits
  • Hemodynamically neutral — minimal effect on heart rate or blood pressure at induction doses, historically the default choice for hemodynamically unstable or borderline patients
  • Rapid onset and predictable, short duration
  • No histamine release, no significant bronchospasm risk
Downfalls / Risks
  • Reliably produces transient adrenal suppression via 11β-hydroxylase inhibition after even a single induction dose, lasting up to 72 hours
  • Myoclonus during induction is common and can be mistaken for seizure activity
⚡ The Etomidate-vs-Ketamine Mortality Question
The 2025 RSI trial (Casey/Seitz/Driver, NEJM, n=2,365 critically ill adults) is the largest and highest-quality answer yet: no significant difference in 28-day in-hospital mortality between ketamine and etomidate (28.1% vs. 29.1%, difference −0.8%, 95% CI −4.5 to 2.9, p=0.65). But ketamine produced significantly MORE cardiovascular collapse within 2 minutes of intubation (SBP <65, new/increased vasopressors, or arrest): 22.1% vs. 17.0% (difference 5.1%, 95% CI 1.9–8.3) — a gap that widened further in the septic shock subgroup (30.6% vs. 20.9%). This directly inverts the commonly-taught assumption that ketamine is simply the 'safer, more hemodynamically stable' choice; the trial found the opposite for early cardiovascular collapse, even though neither agent proved superior for the outcome that actually matters most (mortality). A pediatric propensity-matched study similarly found no mortality difference between etomidate and ketamine, though etomidate showed a lower (not higher) rate of adrenal suppression than ketamine in that cohort (1.8% vs 4.2%) — counter to the adult adrenal-suppression narrative and a reminder that the etomidate-adrenal-suppression-equals-harm chain of reasoning has never been as clean as the oral tradition suggests.
Special Considerations
  • Reasonable default in hemodynamically unstable/shock patients where avoiding induction-related hypotension is the priority — the adrenal suppression concern has not been shown to translate into a clear mortality signal across the highest-quality current trial data
  • Myoclonus can complicate neurologic exams immediately post-induction — worth anticipating and documenting so it isn't mistaken for a seizure

Ketamine

Ketalar
Induction agent — NMDA receptor antagonist, dissociative
Dose
1–2 mg/kg IV (RSI trial median dose 140 mg — notably higher than many real-world practice doses)
Onset
45–60 seconds
Duration
10–20 minutes
Physiology

Non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist producing a dissociative anesthetic state, distinct from the GABA-ergic mechanism of every other agent on this list. Typically preserves airway reflexes and respiratory drive better than propofol or benzodiazepines, and its sympathomimetic effect (catecholamine release) usually supports rather than drops blood pressure — though this sympathomimetic reserve can be exhausted in catecholamine-depleted critical illness (see evidence below).

Benefits
  • Bronchodilation — the preferred induction agent in reactive airway disease/status asthmaticus for this reason specifically
  • Analgesic in addition to dissociative/amnestic effect, unlike every other induction agent here
  • Preserves spontaneous respiratory drive and airway reflexes at sub-dissociative doses, enabling delayed sequence intubation and procedural sedation use cases beyond RSI
  • No adrenal suppression
Downfalls / Risks
  • Emergence reactions (hallucinations, agitation) on recovery, more common in adults than children and mitigated by co-administered benzodiazepines when used for procedural sedation
  • Sialorrhea (increased secretions)
  • Contraindicated in known psychosis given risk of exacerbation
⚡ The ICP Myth — Debunked
Ketamine was dogmatically taught as contraindicated in traumatic brain injury / elevated ICP for roughly 50 years, based entirely on a small cluster of 1970s studies using lumbar CSF pressure measurements in patients with obstructive hydrocephalus or aqueductal stenosis — a fundamentally different population and measurement technique than modern TBI care. None of those foundational studies were performed in actual TBI patients. Multiple modern systematic reviews and meta-analyses have since found no evidence of harm and no significant ICP increase with ketamine in TBI populations (one 2025 meta-analysis: RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.45–1.01, p=0.054, trending toward reduced rather than increased ICP), and ketamine is now considered by many neurocritical care groups to be a reasonable, even preferred, induction agent in TBI given its hemodynamic stability profile relative to propofol/benzodiazepines — a complete reversal of 50 years of teaching. Retrospective ICU data have even found ketamine boluses associated with reduced ICP and increased cerebral perfusion pressure in severe TBI with refractory intracranial hypertension.
Special Considerations
  • No longer contraindicated in TBI/elevated ICP — update this teaching if you trained when it was still taught as an absolute contraindication
  • First-line induction agent in status asthmaticus/severe bronchospasm given its unique bronchodilatory property
  • The 2025 RSI trial's cardiovascular collapse finding is most relevant in septic/catecholamine-depleted shock — don't assume 'ketamine is always the hemodynamically safe choice' in this specific population

Propofol

Diprivan
Induction agent — alkylphenol, GABA-ergic
Dose
1.5–2.5 mg/kg IV (reduce substantially in hemodynamically unstable patients)
Onset
15–45 seconds
Duration
5–10 minutes
Physiology

Potentiates GABA-A receptor activity producing rapid, smooth hypnosis. Causes dose-dependent vasodilation and myocardial depression — the mechanism behind its most clinically significant drawback.

Benefits
  • Rapid, smooth onset with a favorable side-effect profile for airway reflexes/laryngospasm risk compared to some alternatives
  • Antiemetic properties
  • Excellent choice for elective/controlled intubations in hemodynamically normal patients
  • Same agent used for post-intubation sedation, allowing pharmacologic continuity from induction through maintenance
Downfalls / Risks
  • Significant, dose-dependent hypotension via vasodilation and direct myocardial depression — the primary reason it's used cautiously or avoided as a sole induction agent in hemodynamically unstable/shock patients
  • Injection pain (mitigated by lidocaine pretreatment through the same line)
  • Propofol infusion syndrome with prolonged high-dose infusions (rare, but relevant for post-intubation sedation planning, not the single induction dose)
Special Considerations
  • Reduce dose substantially (or avoid as sole agent) in hypotensive/shock patients — this is the agent most likely to precipitate peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse if dosed at standard elective-anesthesia levels in a sick patient
  • A reasonable default for elective/controlled RSI in a hemodynamically normal patient given its smooth, predictable profile

Midazolam

Versed
Induction agent (less common for RSI) — benzodiazepine
Dose
0.1–0.3 mg/kg IV for induction (notably higher than typical procedural sedation dosing)
Onset
60–90 seconds (slower than the other agents here)
Duration
15–30 minutes
Physiology

GABA-A receptor agonist producing sedation, amnesia, and anxiolysis, with no analgesic properties.

Benefits
  • Reliable amnestic effect
  • Familiar, widely available
Downfalls / Risks
  • Slower, less predictable onset than etomidate, ketamine, or propofol makes it a poor choice as a primary RSI induction agent — commonly under-dosed for induction because typical procedural sedation doses are far lower than what true induction requires
  • Hypotension risk, particularly in combination with opioids
  • No analgesic effect — intubation itself is a painful stimulus that midazolam alone does not address
Special Considerations
  • Far more commonly used for post-intubation sedation or procedural sedation than as a primary RSI induction agent — included here for completeness and because under-dosing it for induction (using procedural-sedation-level doses) is a recognized pitfall

⛓️ Paralytics

Succinylcholine

Sux · Anectine
Depolarizing neuromuscular blocker
Dose
1–1.5 mg/kg IV
Onset
45–60 seconds (fastest of any paralytic)
Duration
6–10 minutes
Physiology

Depolarizes the neuromuscular junction by binding nicotinic acetylcholine receptors as an acetylcholine agonist, producing sustained depolarization (visible as fasciculations) followed by flaccid paralysis as the receptor becomes refractory. Metabolized by plasma pseudocholinesterase — the basis of its short, self-terminating duration.

Benefits
  • Fastest onset of any paralytic — the traditional default when the fastest possible time-to-paralysis matters
  • Shortest duration — if intubation fails, spontaneous respiration returns fastest with this agent among standard paralytics
Downfalls / Risks
  • Hyperkalemia risk in specific populations (see evidence below) — can be life-threatening
  • Malignant hyperthermia trigger in susceptible individuals
  • Fasciculations can transiently raise intragastric, intraocular, and intracranial pressure
  • Bradycardia, particularly with repeat dosing or in children
⚡ The Hyperkalemia Timing Window — More Precise Than “Avoid in Burns”
The mechanism is upregulation of extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors spreading across the entire muscle membrane (not just the neuromuscular junction) in denervation, burns, crush injury, prolonged immobilization, or upper motor neuron lesions — succinylcholine depolarizes all of these upregulated receptors simultaneously, producing a massive, potentially lethal potassium efflux rather than the small, clinically insignificant efflux seen in normal muscle. Critically, this upregulation takes time to develop: primate denervation models show changes in serum potassium beginning as early as day 4, with half-peak rise at 8.4 days and peak rise at 14 days post-injury — succinylcholine is NOT dangerous in the acute window (roughly the first 3–4 days) after burns, denervation, or crush injury, contrary to the commonly taught blanket rule of 'avoid in burns.' The risk then persists for 2–3 months in burns/trauma, and potentially 3–6 months (or per some sources, up to 2 years) in upper motor neuron lesions/prolonged denervation, requiring an individualized rather than purely time-based judgment as the window extends.
Special Considerations
  • Safe in the acute setting (roughly first 3–4 days) after burns, crush injury, or denervation — the danger window is delayed onset (day 4 onward, peaking around day 14), not immediate
  • Avoid from several days out through the 2–3+ month risk window in burns, denervation, spinal cord injury, prolonged immobilization, or neuromuscular disease — use rocuronium instead
  • Avoid in known/suspected hyperkalemia, malignant hyperthermia history, or pseudocholinesterase deficiency

Rocuronium

Zemuron
Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocker — aminosteroid
Dose
1–1.2 mg/kg IV for RSI (higher than the standard OR dose of 0.6 mg/kg, specifically to shorten onset time to compete with succinylcholine)
Onset
45–90 seconds at RSI dosing
Duration
45–70 minutes at RSI dosing
Physiology

Competitively antagonizes acetylcholine at the nicotinic receptor without depolarizing it — no fasciculations, no direct potassium efflux mechanism, making it fundamentally immune to the hyperkalemia risk that limits succinylcholine.

Benefits
  • No hyperkalemia risk — the default paralytic whenever succinylcholine is contraindicated
  • At RSI dosing (1–1.2 mg/kg), onset is now comparable to succinylcholine, largely closing the historical speed gap
  • Reversible with sugammadex (see below) — a genuine safety advantage over succinylcholine when reversal might be needed urgently (failed airway, can't-intubate-can't-oxygenate scenario where restoring spontaneous ventilation matters)
Downfalls / Risks
  • Much longer duration than succinylcholine (45–70 min vs. 6–10 min) — if intubation fails and the patient cannot be ventilated, this duration becomes a genuine liability unless sugammadex is immediately available
  • Anaphylaxis risk (aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers are a leading cause of perioperative anaphylaxis)
Special Considerations
  • Default choice whenever succinylcholine is contraindicated (hyperkalemia risk populations, malignant hyperthermia history, pseudocholinesterase deficiency)
  • Have sugammadex immediately available/considered whenever rocuronium is used for RSI, specifically as a rescue plan for the failed airway scenario

Vecuronium

Norcuron
Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocker — aminosteroid
Dose
0.08–0.1 mg/kg IV (RSI/high dose); not typically used for RSI given slower onset than rocuronium
Onset
2–3 minutes at standard dosing — too slow for primary RSI use
Duration
25–40 minutes
Physiology

Same mechanism class as rocuronium (competitive nicotinic antagonist), structurally related aminosteroid.

Benefits
  • More hemodynamically neutral than some alternatives
  • Useful for post-intubation ongoing paralysis (e.g., severe ARDS with ventilator dyssynchrony) rather than the induction moment itself
Downfalls / Risks
  • Too slow an onset for primary RSI use — rocuronium has essentially replaced it for this indication
  • Active metabolite can prolong effect in renal/hepatic impairment
Special Considerations
  • Reserve for post-intubation maintenance paralysis rather than the RSI paralytic step itself

Cisatracurium

Nimbex
Non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocker — benzylisoquinolinium
Dose
0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV (RSI dosing); not first-line for primary RSI given onset time
Onset
2–3 minutes — too slow for primary RSI
Duration
45–60 minutes
Physiology

Non-depolarizing blocker undergoing Hofmann elimination (spontaneous, organ-independent degradation) rather than hepatic/renal metabolism — the key distinguishing feature from the aminosteroids.

Benefits
  • Organ-independent elimination (Hofmann degradation) makes it the preferred agent for ongoing paralysis in significant renal or hepatic failure, where rocuronium/vecuronium clearance becomes unpredictable
  • Minimal histamine release compared to atracurium (its parent compound)
Downfalls / Risks
  • Too slow an onset for primary RSI use
  • Not reversible by sugammadex (which is specific to aminosteroid neuromuscular blockers)
Special Considerations
  • The preferred paralytic for continuous post-intubation neuromuscular blockade (e.g., severe ARDS requiring paralysis) specifically in renal or hepatic failure, not for the RSI paralysis step itself

Sugammadex Reversal

A modified gamma-cyclodextrin that encapsulates rocuronium (and, less effectively, vecuronium) molecules directly, terminating neuromuscular blockade independent of acetylcholinesterase — a fundamentally different reversal mechanism from neostigmine, and one that works even at deep levels of blockade where neostigmine is ineffective.

Rescue Dose
16 mg/kg IV for immediate reversal of rocuronium-induced blockade (the 'can't intubate, can't oxygenate' rescue dose) — lower doses (2–4 mg/kg) suffice for reversal at lighter, more typical post-procedure blockade levels.
⚡ Clinical Relevance
The existence of sugammadex is the strongest modern argument for choosing rocuronium over succinylcholine even when speed is the only historical advantage succinylcholine offered — a truly failed rocuronium-paralyzed airway can, in principle, be pharmacologically reversed within roughly 2–3 minutes at the 16 mg/kg rescue dose, restoring spontaneous ventilation faster than simply waiting out succinylcholine's own natural 6–10 minute duration in some comparisons. Not FDA-approved for pediatric use under 2 years of age at the time of most current labeling, and not effective for reversing succinylcholine, vecuronium (only partially effective), or the benzylisoquinolinium agents like cisatracurium.

Video vs. Direct Laryngoscopy

Video laryngoscopy adds a camera near the blade tip, displaying the glottic view on a screen rather than requiring direct line-of-sight — the operator can visualize around anatomic obstructions that would block a direct line of sight.

Figure — DEVICE trial first-attempt success by device and operator experience
⚡ DEVICE Trial Evidence
The DEVICE trial (Prekker 2023, NEJM, n=1,417 critically ill adults across 17 ED/ICU sites) found video laryngoscopy produced significantly higher first-attempt success than direct laryngoscopy: 85.1% (600/705) vs. 70.8% (504/712) — an absolute difference of 14.3 percentage points. The benefit was present across all operator experience levels but was largest for the least experienced operators (27.1 percentage point gap: 80.0% vs. 59.3%), suggesting video laryngoscopy meaningfully flattens the experience-dependent learning curve rather than only helping already-skilled operators. This is now one of the most decisive head-to-head device trials in emergency airway management — earlier smaller trials had given conflicting signals (e.g., the 2017 MACMAN trial did not show a first-pass benefit), but DEVICE's size and pragmatic multi-specialty design make it the most influential current evidence.
Clinical Bottom Line
Video laryngoscopy should be the default first-attempt device for emergency intubation when available, particularly for less experienced operators — not reserved only for predicted-difficult airways. Maintain direct laryngoscopy skill for device failure/unavailability scenarios, but the default-device decision has shifted meaningfully based on DEVICE trial data.

📈 Post-Intubation Management

Sedation & Analgesia

The immediate post-intubation period is a well-documented high-risk window for both under-sedation (awareness, agitation, self-extubation) and hemodynamic deterioration from over-sedation — a frequently under-taught phase relative to the drama of the intubation itself.

AgentRole & notes
Propofol infusionCommon default for hemodynamically stable patients — titratable, short context-sensitive half-time, but the same vasodilatory/negative inotropic risk from induction persists at infusion doses; watch for delayed post-intubation hypotension as the bolus effects of induction agents wear off and infusion effects take over.
Fentanyl (± propofol/midazolam)Analgesia is frequently under-dosed relative to sedation in the immediate post-intubation period — an intubated patient still needs pain control, not just sedation, and endotracheal tubes themselves are a painful stimulus.
Dexmedetomidine (precedex)Useful when a patient needs to remain more arousable/cooperative (e.g., approaching extubation readiness assessment) or when avoiding respiratory depression matters, given its distinct alpha-2 agonist mechanism — less acutely useful as the very first post-intubation agent in an unstable patient given its own hypotension/bradycardia profile.
Ketamine infusionAn emerging option particularly when hemodynamic stability is the priority or when the patient was induced with ketamine and continuity of agent is desired — less standardized than propofol/fentanyl-based sedation but increasingly used in shock physiology.
Pitfall
Post-intubation hypotension is common and multifactorial: residual induction agent effect, transition to positive-pressure ventilation (reducing venous return/preload, especially in volume-depleted patients), and sedation infusion effects can all stack — have push-dose pressors ready proactively rather than reactively, particularly in any patient who was borderline before induction.

Ventilator Initiation

Lung-protective ventilation principles (originally validated in ARDS, now broadly applied as a reasonable default even before ARDS is confirmed) aim to minimize ventilator-induced lung injury from the first breath after intubation, not just after a delayed ARDS diagnosis.

ParameterStarting point
Tidal volume6 mL/kg predicted body weight (calculated from height and sex, not actual body weight — a frequently missed detail that leads to over-sized tidal volumes in obese patients specifically)
Respiratory rate14–20/min initially, titrated to pH/PaCO2 and patient synchrony
PEEP5 cmH2O as a reasonable universal starting point, titrated upward per oxygenation and the specific clinical scenario (higher in ARDS/obesity, lower in COPD/severe bronchospasm given air-trapping risk)
FiO2100% initially post-intubation, then titrated down to the lowest FiO2 achieving an acceptable SpO2 (generally targeting the low-to-mid 90s rather than reflexively keeping FiO2 high)
⚡ Pitfall
Using actual body weight instead of predicted body weight for tidal volume calculation is one of the most common ventilator-initiation errors, particularly in obese patients, where it can lead to volutrauma from an inappropriately large tidal volume.

🧑‍⚕️ Special Population Induction Selection

Figure — Standard vs. ramped positioning (obesity/OSA)

🧠 Elevated ICP / Neurotrauma

The single biggest teaching reversal in this atlas: ketamine's 50-year 'contraindicated in head injury' status has been thoroughly debunked, while the actual priority — avoiding hypotension, which independently worsens outcomes in TBI far more than any induction-agent-specific ICP effect — is often under-emphasized relative to drug selection.
  • Ketamine or etomidate are both reasonable induction choices — ketamine is no longer contraindicated (see Induction Agents tab for the full evidence), and etomidate's hemodynamic neutrality remains valuable here
  • Avoiding peri-intubation hypotension matters more than which specific induction agent is chosen — a single hypotensive episode is independently associated with worse TBI outcomes, making agent-induced blood pressure drops (propofol especially) the bigger practical concern than any ICP-specific drug property
  • Rocuronium or succinylcholine are both acceptable paralytics — succinylcholine's transient ICP elevation from fasciculations is generally considered clinically insignificant relative to the benefit of rapid, reliable paralysis
  • Maintain adequate MAP/cerebral perfusion pressure as the actual physiologic target throughout, not just at the moment of induction
Common Pitfalls
Reflexively avoiding ketamine out of outdated training, and under-appreciating that hypotension — not drug selection — is the dominant modifiable risk in this population

⚡ Hemodynamically Unstable / Shock

The population where induction agent choice has the most direct, immediate life-or-death consequence — and where the 2025 RSI trial data meaningfully updated the conventional wisdom.
  • Etomidate remains a reasonable default given its hemodynamic neutrality, particularly outside septic shock specifically
  • Ketamine is also reasonable but the 2025 RSI trial found significantly more early cardiovascular collapse with ketamine than etomidate, especially pronounced in septic shock (30.6% vs. 20.9%) — don't assume ketamine is automatically the 'safe' choice in this population without accounting for this data
  • Reduce induction doses substantially regardless of which agent is chosen — standard elective-anesthesia dosing in a shock patient is a common and dangerous error
  • Avoid propofol as a sole induction agent given its more pronounced vasodilatory/negative inotropic profile, unless dosed very conservatively
  • Have push-dose pressors drawn up and ready before induction, not after hypotension develops
Common Pitfalls
Assuming ketamine is the reflexively 'safe' hemodynamic choice without considering current trial data, and using standard (non-reduced) induction doses in a hypotensive patient

🌬️ Reactive Airway Disease / Status Asthmaticus

One of the few scenarios in this atlas with a genuinely clean, mechanism-based first-choice answer.
  • Ketamine is the preferred induction agent specifically for its bronchodilatory property — no other agent on this list shares this effect
  • Avoid histamine-releasing agents/adjuncts where possible given bronchospasm risk
  • Anticipate breath-stacking/dynamic hyperinflation risk once ventilation begins — permissive hypercapnia with a lower respiratory rate and prolonged expiratory time is generally preferred over aggressive normalization of PaCO2 in the immediate post-intubation period
  • Have a plan for post-intubation bronchodilator delivery through the circuit
Common Pitfalls
Aggressive bag-mask or ventilator rates immediately post-intubation in a severely obstructed patient, risking breath-stacking, auto-PEEP, and secondary hypotension/barotrauma

❤️ Right Heart Failure / Pulmonary Hypertension

A frequently under-recognized population where the induction and positive-pressure-ventilation transition itself — not just drug choice — carries major risk, since acute RV failure can be precipitated by increases in pulmonary vascular resistance or drops in RV preload/coronary perfusion.
  • Etomidate is generally preferred given hemodynamic neutrality — avoiding a systemic BP drop is critical since RV coronary perfusion depends on maintaining a favorable systemic-to-pulmonary pressure gradient
  • Avoid factors that acutely raise pulmonary vascular resistance during induction/intubation: hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, and excessive positive intrathoracic pressure from aggressive positive-pressure ventilation
  • Have inotropic/vasopressor support (and ideally pulmonary-vasodilator-capable agents) ready before induction, not as a reactive measure
  • Ventilator strategy post-intubation should specifically avoid excessive PEEP/plateau pressure, which can further increase RV afterload
Common Pitfalls
Treating this like routine hemodynamically unstable shock without recognizing the RV-specific physiology — standard aggressive fluid resuscitation, for instance, can worsen rather than help an acutely failing right ventricle

👥 Obesity / OSA

Primarily a mechanical/positioning and preoxygenation challenge more than a drug-selection one — the airway anatomy and rapid desaturation physiology matter more here than which induction agent is chosen.
  • Ramped position (external auditory meatus aligned horizontally with the sternal notch, often requiring blankets/pillows under the shoulders and head) rather than standard sniffing position — significantly improves laryngoscopic view and reduces time to desaturation in this population
  • Preoxygenation is disproportionately important given reduced functional residual capacity and faster desaturation kinetics — NIV/BiPAP preoxygenation (per PREOXI data) plus apneic oxygenation via nasal cannula is especially valuable here
  • Drug dosing: use ideal/adjusted body weight for most induction agents and paralytics rather than total body weight, to avoid overdosing (an important and frequently missed calculation)
  • Video laryngoscopy is particularly advantageous in this population given the anatomic challenges
  • Tidal volume calculation must use predicted body weight (height/sex-based), not actual weight — see Post-Intubation Ventilator section
Common Pitfalls
Standard sniffing position instead of ramping, underestimating how quickly this population desaturates once apneic, and dosing induction agents/paralytics by total body weight instead of ideal/adjusted body weight

👶 Pregnancy

Physiologic changes of pregnancy (reduced FRC, increased oxygen consumption, airway edema/friability, delayed gastric emptying and reduced lower esophageal sphincter tone) combine to produce both faster desaturation and a higher difficult-airway/aspiration-risk profile than the general population.
  • Left uterine displacement (left lateral tilt or manual displacement) beyond ~20 weeks to relieve aortocaval compression — improves both maternal venous return and, indirectly, preoxygenation tolerance and hemodynamic stability during induction
  • Preoxygenation is even more critical than usual given reduced FRC and increased oxygen consumption — desaturation happens faster in pregnancy than in an otherwise-similar non-pregnant patient
  • Anticipate a more difficult airway than the general population given airway edema/friability — have a lower threshold for video laryngoscopy and a smaller-than-usual endotracheal tube available
  • Standard RSI induction/paralytic agents (etomidate or ketamine, succinylcholine or rocuronium) are all generally considered acceptable in pregnancy — this is not a scenario requiring fundamentally different pharmacology, but rather heightened attention to positioning, preoxygenation, and aspiration precautions
  • Aspiration risk is elevated (delayed gastric emptying, reduced LES tone, gravid uterus increasing intra-abdominal pressure) — standard RSI technique with cricoid pressure remains commonly taught here even as its routine use elsewhere has become more selective
Common Pitfalls
Underestimating how quickly a pregnant patient desaturates, and failing to position with left uterine displacement in a patient beyond ~20 weeks gestation

👶 Pediatric RSI Dosing

Same pharmacologic principles as adult RSI, with weight-based dosing and a few population-specific considerations — cross-reference the companion Fluid Resuscitation Atlas's pediatric fluid content and the Lab Values Atlas's pediatric calculators for adjacent dosing tools.
  • Etomidate: 0.3 mg/kg IV — same weight-based dose as adults; the pediatric propensity-matched comparison to ketamine found no mortality difference and, notably, lower (not higher) adrenal suppression with etomidate than ketamine in that cohort
  • Ketamine: 1–2 mg/kg IV — widely used in pediatric RSI, particularly valued for hemodynamic stability and bronchodilation in pediatric reactive airway presentations
  • Succinylcholine: 1–2 mg/kg IV in infants/young children (higher mg/kg than the adult dose given a larger relative extracellular fluid volume/volume of distribution); atropine pretreatment is more commonly considered in young children given a higher risk of succinylcholine-associated bradycardia in this age group than in adults
  • Rocuronium: 1–1.2 mg/kg IV, same RSI-dose principle as adults
  • Weight-based/length-based dosing references (Broselow tape or equivalent) are the standard safeguard against dosing errors in a genuine pediatric emergency — have one available rather than relying on mental math under pressure
Common Pitfalls
Using adult fixed doses instead of weight-based pediatric dosing, and forgetting the higher bradycardia risk with succinylcholine in young children specifically

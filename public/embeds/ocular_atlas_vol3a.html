Bacterial Keratitis

The size/location threshold for culturing, not a fixed rule about every ulcer, is what should drive whether you reach for fortified antibiotics or start fluoroquinolone monotherapy.

Bacterial invasion of the corneal stroma, typically following a break in the epithelial barrier (contact lens wear, trauma, or ocular surface disease), produces a focal white-gray stromal infiltrate with an overlying epithelial defect of roughly matching size — a helpful discriminator from fungal or sterile inflammatory infiltrates, where the epithelial defect and infiltrate size are frequently mismatched. Pseudomonas is the dominant pathogen in contact lens wearers and progresses notably fast, capable of causing stromal melt and perforation within 24–48 hours if inadequately treated; Staphylococcus species are common in non-contact-lens-related cases.

Risk Factors
  • Contact lens wear, especially extended/overnight wear and poor lens hygiene (the dominant risk factor in the developed world, strongly associated with Pseudomonas)
  • Ocular surface disease: dry eye, exposure keratopathy, trichiasis, chronic blepharitis
  • Corneal trauma or foreign body, recent ocular surgery
  • Topical corticosteroid use on an unrecognized infection (worsens bacterial, fungal, and HSV keratitis alike)
  • Immunosuppression, diabetes
Fundoscopic Findings

Not directly relevant; the disease is confined to the cornea. In severe cases with a large hypopyon or scleral extension, ophthalmology may still perform a fundus/B-scan assessment to exclude secondary endophthalmitis, but this is not a routine fundus finding of bacterial keratitis itself.

History
  • Contact lens wear (type, wear schedule, hygiene practices, any overnight wear), recent trauma, prior ocular surgery, and any recent topical steroid use.
  • Timing and rate of symptom progression (rapid progression over hours suggests a more virulent organism, classically Pseudomonas).
Exam
  • Visual acuity, slit lamp exam to characterize the infiltrate: size, depth, location (central vs. peripheral), and whether the epithelial defect size matches the infiltrate size (a bacterial hallmark).
  • Look for hypopyon, degree of anterior chamber reaction, and any sign of impending or frank perforation (positive Seidel, descemetocele).
Imaging/Labs
  • Corneal scraping for Gram stain and culture is recommended for any large, central, or atypical (e.g., ring infiltrate) ulcer, per current AAO Preferred Practice Pattern guidance — obtained before starting antibiotics whenever feasible. Smaller peripheral ulcers in low-risk patients may reasonably be treated empirically without culture, at the treating clinician's discretion.
1
Obtain corneal culture before starting antibiotics when indicated

Culture any large, central, vision-threatening, or atypical ulcer, and any ulcer failing initial therapy. Culturing small peripheral ulcers in low-risk patients is optional and left to clinical judgment.

⏰ Before first antibiotic dose, when feasible
2
Choose initial empiric therapy based on size/severity

Small, peripheral, low-risk ulcers: fourth-generation fluoroquinolone monotherapy (moxifloxacin or gatifloxacin) is well-supported by randomized trial data as equivalent to fortified combination therapy. Larger, central, or severe ulcers: fortified antibiotics — cefazolin or vancomycin (gram-positive) alternating with tobramycin or gentamicin (gram-negative) — remain the traditional and still-recommended approach, particularly given rising fluoroquinolone resistance in some regions/populations (notably MRSA).

⏰ Immediately, empirically, pending culture results
3
Dose aggressively during the induction phase

A typical fortified antibiotic regimen: hourly round-the-clock dosing for the first 24 hours, then hourly while awake for the following 48 hours, then a gradual taper every 2–3 days down to QID, continuing for a total of roughly 5–7 days after clinical resolution.

⏰ First 24–72 hours are the most intensive dosing period
4
Reassess response and adjust

If worsening on fluoroquinolone monotherapy, switch to fortified broad-spectrum antibiotics. If worsening on fortified antibiotics, consider whether drug toxicity (rather than undertreated infection) is now the limiting factor, since fortified agents are epithelial-toxic with prolonged use; reducing frequency may be appropriate once infection is controlled.

⏰ Reassessed at 24–48h and each follow-up
5
Add a cycloplegic for comfort

Cyclopentolate or homatropine reduces ciliary spasm pain; avoid phenylephrine-containing combination cycloplegics given theoretical vasoconstrictive concerns in an already-compromised cornea.

⏰ Same visit
6
Avoid topical steroids until infection is controlled

Topical corticosteroids are not started until the infection is responding to antibiotics and any infectious mimic (especially HSV/fungal) has been excluded; premature steroid use can accelerate bacterial (and especially fungal) progression.

⏰ Deferred, ophthalmology-directed if used at all
7
Escalate to surgical options if progressing despite maximal medical therapy

Corneal collagen cross-linking has emerging evidence as an adjunct in resistant cases; therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty is reserved for impending/frank perforation or medically unresponsive disease.

⏰ If medical therapy fails or perforation is impending

Complications to Anticipate
  • Corneal scarring and reduced vision, worse with central or deep infiltrates
  • Corneal melt/perforation, particularly with Pseudomonas or delayed/inadequate treatment
  • Endophthalmitis if the infection extends intraocularly (uncommon but serious)

Clinical Pearls
  • When in doubt about the underlying organism, assume bacterial until proven otherwise — this is the default diagnostic assumption for an unexplained corneal infiltrate, given how common bacterial causes are relative to fungal/parasitic alternatives in most populations.
  • An epithelial defect that closely matches the infiltrate size in dimension is a bacterial hallmark; a mismatch (infiltrate much larger or smaller than the overlying defect) should raise suspicion for a fungal or non-infectious process instead.
  • Don't reach for topical steroids to 'help' a painful eye before the infection is controlled and mimics (especially HSV) have been excluded — this is one of the most common iatrogenic ways a treatable keratitis becomes a much harder problem.
Evidence Notes
  • Multiple RCTs (Constantinou et al. 2007; Sharma et al. 2013 comparing moxifloxacin monotherapy to fortified combination therapy) found fourth-generation fluoroquinolone monotherapy statistically equivalent to fortified cefazolin/tobramycin for non-severe bacterial keratitis, supporting current AAO Preferred Practice Pattern guidance favoring monotherapy for smaller, less severe ulcers while reserving fortified antibiotics for large/central/severe disease.
  • The Steroids for Corneal Ulcers Trial (SCUT) demonstrated that in vitro antibiotic susceptibility correlates with clinical outcomes, supporting routine culture and sensitivity testing for significant corneal ulcers to guide any necessary therapy adjustment.

HSV Keratitis

The HEDS trials fundamentally split this disease into two management philosophies: epithelial disease is active viral replication that steroids will make worse, while stromal disease is an immune response that steroids (with antiviral cover) will actually help control.

Reactivation of latent HSV-1 in the trigeminal ganglion, triggered by stress, UV exposure, fever, or immunosuppression, produces a spectrum of corneal disease. Epithelial keratitis reflects active viral replication within corneal epithelial cells, producing the pathognomonic dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs. Stromal keratitis, by contrast, is primarily an immune-mediated response to viral antigen rather than active viral replication in the stroma itself — a distinction that is the entire basis for why stromal disease, unlike epithelial disease, can be safely treated with topical steroids (under antiviral cover).

Reduced corneal sensation from repeated nerve involvement is a frequently underappreciated finding: it means patients with recurrent HSV keratitis often report less pain than the exam findings would suggest, which can falsely reassure both patient and clinician.

Risk Factors
  • Prior episodes of ocular HSV (strongest predictor of recurrence; risk of recurrent stromal keratitis specifically is reduced by roughly half with one year of oral acyclovir prophylaxis)
  • UV light exposure, fever, emotional stress, and other classic reactivation triggers
  • Immunosuppression
  • Topical corticosteroid use on unrecognized epithelial disease (accelerates viral replication and can progress to geographic ulceration)
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable to isolated epithelial or stromal HSV keratitis, which is confined to the anterior segment; HSV can rarely cause acute retinal necrosis in immunocompromised or disseminated cases, which is a distinct, far more severe posterior segment diagnosis requiring different (often IV) antiviral therapy.

History
  • Prior episodes of ocular HSV (cold sores are a less reliable predictor of ocular involvement than a documented prior ocular episode).
  • Any recent topical steroid use, which can mask or worsen epithelial disease.
Exam
  • Fluorescein staining under cobalt blue light: dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs (epithelial disease) versus stromal haze/edema with an overlying intact or minimally affected epithelium (stromal disease) versus a geographic (amoeboid) ulcer, a larger and more severe variant of epithelial disease often associated with prior steroid exposure.
  • Corneal sensation testing (light wisp of cotton) — reduced sensation supports the diagnosis and explains disproportionately mild reported pain.
  • Assess whether stromal disease is accompanied by an epithelial defect (treated as active epithelial disease first) versus intact epithelium over stromal haze (can proceed to steroid-inclusive therapy under antiviral cover).
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis; PCR of a corneal swab is available for atypical or diagnostically uncertain cases but not routinely needed.
1
Classify as epithelial vs. stromal disease first

This single distinction drives the entire treatment approach: epithelial disease (active viral replication) is treated with antivirals ALONE, no steroids; stromal disease (immune-mediated) can be treated with a topical steroid PLUS antiviral cover once appropriately classified.

⏰ At initial exam
2
Epithelial keratitis: antiviral monotherapy

Topical ganciclovir 0.15% gel (5x/day, lower epithelial toxicity, now generally preferred) or topical trifluridine 1% (9x/day, higher epithelial toxicity with prolonged use) or oral antiviral (acyclovir 400mg 5x/day, or valacyclovir/famciclovir at appropriate epithelial-keratitis dosing) if topical agents aren't tolerated or oral therapy is preferred. Response typically occurs within 2–5 days, with complete resolution generally within 2 weeks; taper rapidly after response and discontinue once healed (generally 10–14 days total) to limit epithelial toxicity.

⏰ Until fully healed, generally 10–14 days
3
Stromal keratitis: add topical steroid under antiviral cover

Topical corticosteroid (e.g., prednisolone) is added specifically for stromal disease, always alongside an antiviral (oral is generally preferred for stromal disease over topical, given adequate systemic/aqueous penetration without added epithelial toxicity) to prevent reactivation of epithelial disease while immunosuppressing locally. Steroids are titrated to the lowest effective dose and tapered slowly, often over weeks to months for recurrent/severe stromal disease.

⏰ Started once stromal disease (not active epithelial disease) is confirmed, continued and tapered per ophthalmology guidance
4
Cycloplegic for comfort

Cyclopentolate or homatropine for ciliary spasm-related discomfort, used in either epithelial or stromal disease as needed.

⏰ As needed
5
Consider long-term oral antiviral prophylaxis after resolution

One year of oral acyclovir 400mg twice daily reduces recurrence of any ocular HSV disease by roughly 41% and specifically reduces recurrent stromal keratitis by over 50%; this is a reasonable discussion for patients with recurrent disease, particularly stromal keratitis given its scarring potential.

⏰ After acute episode resolves, continued for approximately 1 year

Complications to Anticipate
  • Corneal scarring, especially with recurrent or stromal disease — a leading infectious cause of corneal blindness in the developed world
  • Geographic ulcer (larger, more destructive epithelial variant), often related to prior inappropriate steroid use
  • Neurotrophic keratopathy from cumulative corneal nerve damage, predisposing to poor healing and recurrent breakdown
  • Progression to corneal perforation in severe, inadequately treated stromal disease
Clinical Pearls
  • The epithelial-vs-stromal distinction is the single most important branch point in HSV keratitis management: get this wrong (steroids for active epithelial disease) and you can convert a self-limited problem into a progressive, destructive ulcer.
  • Reduced corneal sensation means these patients often under-report pain relative to how severe the exam looks — don't let a reassuring pain history talk you out of a concerning exam finding.
  • Adding oral acyclovir to topical trifluridine did not show benefit in preventing progression from epithelial to stromal disease in HEDS — stromal disease is believed to arise from an immune response rather than ongoing active viral replication, which is why treating epithelial disease harder doesn't prevent it.
Evidence Notes
  • HEDS I established that topical steroids plus topical trifluridine resolved stromal keratitis faster than trifluridine alone, with no added benefit from oral acyclovir on top of that combination for the acute episode itself.
  • HEDS II (long-term acyclovir trial) showed one year of oral acyclovir 400mg BID reduced recurrence of any ocular HSV disease by 41% and recurrent stromal keratitis specifically by over 50%, with the greatest absolute benefit in patients with the most prior episodes; benefit did not persist once acyclovir was stopped (no rebound effect, but also no lasting protection).

Fungal Keratitis

The MUTT trials rewrote first-line therapy here twice over: natamycin beat voriconazole for filamentous disease in MUTT I, and then adding oral voriconazole on top of topical therapy showed no benefit at all in MUTT II.

Fungal invasion of the corneal stroma, most often filamentous fungi (Fusarium, Aspergillus) following plant/vegetative matter trauma, or yeast (Candida) in the setting of chronic ocular surface disease or immunosuppression. A history of prior topical corticosteroid use is a common and important antecedent, since steroids suppress the local immune response that would otherwise help contain fungal invasion. Unlike bacterial ulcers, the epithelial defect frequently does NOT match the size of the underlying infiltrate, and feathery/filamentous infiltrate margins with satellite lesions are more typical.

Risk Factors
  • Organic/vegetative matter trauma (tree branch, plant material, agricultural work) — the classic antecedent for filamentous fungal keratitis specifically
  • Prior topical corticosteroid use on the ocular surface
  • Contact lens wear (more often associated with yeast or occasionally Fusarium in certain contact lens solution-related outbreaks)
  • Chronic ocular surface disease, immunosuppression, and diabetes (particularly relevant for Candida keratitis)
  • Geographic factors: substantially higher incidence in agricultural/tropical and subtropical regions (most large trials conducted in South Asia given the high regional incidence)
Fundoscopic Findings

Not directly relevant to the corneal process itself; severe or deep fungal keratitis with scleral or intraocular extension can rarely progress to endogenous-appearing endophthalmitis, in which case posterior segment assessment becomes relevant, but this is a complication rather than a primary fungal keratitis finding.

History
  • Specific inquiry about any plant/vegetative matter trauma, however minor it seemed at the time.
  • Any recent topical steroid use on the eye.
  • Contact lens wear and hygiene practices.
Exam
  • Slit lamp exam for a gray-white infiltrate with feathery, filamentous margins, possible satellite lesions distant from the main infiltrate, and — classically — an epithelial defect notably smaller than the underlying stromal infiltrate (the mirror image of the bacterial pattern).
  • Assess depth and look for a hypopyon, which can occur with fungal as well as bacterial disease.
Imaging/Labs
  • Corneal scraping for fungal smear (KOH prep, Gram/Giemsa stain) and fungal culture on appropriate media — essential given how much treatment differs by organism and how much slower fungal culture can be to result compared to bacterial culture.
  • Confocal microscopy, where available, can rapidly identify fungal filaments non-invasively and support earlier diagnosis while awaiting culture.
1
Obtain cultures before starting therapy whenever possible

Fungal culture and smear are central to diagnosis and to distinguishing filamentous from yeast forms, which affects drug choice.

⏰ Before or at initiation of antifungal therapy
2
Start topical natamycin 5% as first-line for filamentous fungal keratitis

Natamycin has trial-level evidence (MUTT I) showing superiority over topical voriconazole monotherapy for filamentous organisms and remains the only FDA-approved topical ophthalmic antifungal in the US.

⏰ Immediately on diagnosis/strong suspicion, hourly initially
3
Reassess at 7–10 days and add topical voriconazole if responding poorly

For inadequate response to natamycin alone, adding topical voriconazole 1% (same dosing frequency) is a reasonable next step per stepwise protocols (e.g., the TST protocol), though evidence for added benefit over natamycin alone in filamentous disease is mixed.

⏰ Reassessed at 7–10 days
4
Do NOT routinely add oral voriconazole

MUTT II specifically tested adding oral voriconazole to topical antifungal therapy for severe filamentous fungal ulcers and found no benefit in reducing corneal perforation or need for therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty — oral voriconazole should not be used as a default addition based on this trial-level evidence.

⏰ N/A — a default-avoid based on trial evidence
5
Consider targeted intrastromal/intracameral injection for non-responders

For patients failing topical (± a second topical agent) therapy after an additional 7–10 days, targeted intrastromal or intracameral voriconazole/amphotericin B injections (repeated up to 4 times, 72 hours apart) are used in some stepwise protocols, though trial-level evidence for benefit over continued medical therapy alone is limited and mixed.

⏰ After failure of topical ± second-agent therapy
6
Therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty for perforation or refractory disease

Reserved for frank or impending perforation, significant corneal thinning that precludes further injections, or continued progression despite maximal medical therapy.

⏰ When medical therapy has failed or perforation is imminent/present
7
Yeast (Candida) keratitis: consider topical amphotericin B or voriconazole preferentially

Candida and other yeast forms may respond better to topical amphotericin B 0.15–0.3% or voriconazole rather than natamycin, which has relatively less activity against yeasts compared to filamentous organisms; treatment should be tailored to culture/smear results once available.

⏰ Once organism type (yeast vs. filamentous) is known

Clinical Pearls
  • A history of vegetative/plant trauma or prior topical steroid use on the eye should raise fungal keratitis specifically on the differential, since these details are non-specific and shared with other keratitis subtypes but are disproportionately associated with fungal disease.

  • Don't reflexively add oral voriconazole to topical therapy for severe filamentous ulcers expecting better outcomes — the trial that specifically tested this (MUTT II) found no benefit.

  • MUTT II found no benefit to adding oral voriconazole to topical antifungal therapy (voriconazole plus natamycin) for severe filamentous fungal ulcers in reducing corneal perforation or need for therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty.

Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO)

The 72-hour window for starting oral antivirals is the number to anchor on — it reduces the severity and duration of ocular complications, even though (somewhat counterintuitively) it doesn't reduce the incidence of postherpetic neuralgia itself.

Reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus within the ophthalmic (V1) division of the trigeminal ganglion produces a dermatomal vesicular rash and, in roughly half of cases, ocular involvement ranging from mild conjunctivitis to sight-threatening keratitis, uveitis, scleritis, or (rarely) acute retinal necrosis or optic neuritis. Hutchinson's sign — vesicular lesions on the tip, side, or root of the nose — indicates nasociliary branch involvement and is a strong clinical predictor of subsequent ocular involvement, since the nasociliary nerve also innervates the globe.

Risk Factors
  • Age over 60 (both incidence and complication severity increase with age) and any condition/medication causing immunosuppression (HZ is 4–5 times more common in immunocompromised patients, though the majority of HZO cases still occur in immunocompetent individuals)
  • Lack of prior varicella (shingles) vaccination
  • Presence of Hutchinson's sign specifically predicts higher likelihood of ocular involvement and should heighten urgency of ophthalmologic evaluation
Fundoscopic Findings

Usually normal in uncomplicated anterior segment HZO. In the rare but serious complication of HZV-associated acute retinal necrosis or optic neuritis, fundus exam may show retinal whitening/necrosis, vasculitis, or disc edema — these posterior segment complications require urgent same-day ophthalmology evaluation and often IV antiviral therapy, distinct from the standard oral outpatient regimen for anterior segment disease.

History
  • Timing of rash onset (critical, since the 72-hour antiviral window is calculated from this) and any prodromal pain/paresthesia preceding the rash.
  • Immunocompromise status.
  • Vaccination history (Shingrix).
Exam
  • Dermatomal vesicular rash respecting the midline, in the V1 distribution; specifically check the tip/side/root of the nose for Hutchinson's sign.
  • Full ophthalmic exam: visual acuity, pupil exam, slit lamp for pseudodendrites (a less well-defined, more heaped-up variant distinct from true HSV dendrites), anterior chamber reaction (uveitis), scleral/episcleral involvement, and IOP.
  • Extraocular movements and cranial nerve exam given occasional cranial nerve palsy association.
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis; viral PCR of skin or conjunctival swabs can confirm in atypical presentations.
  • Neuroimaging or lumbar puncture only if cerebral/CNS involvement is suspected (altered mental status, focal neurologic deficits).
1
Start oral antivirals as early as possible, ideally within 72 hours of rash onset

Antivirals reduce pain, speed rash healing, and decrease the incidence and severity of ocular complications, though they do NOT reduce the incidence of postherpetic neuralgia itself (early treatment does reduce its duration when it occurs). Treatment is still reasonable to start beyond 72 hours if new vesicles are still forming or ocular involvement is present.

⏰ As soon as possible, ideally ≤72h from rash onset
2
Choose valacyclovir or famciclovir over acyclovir when feasible

Both valacyclovir and famciclovir have shown more effective pain resolution than acyclovir in comparative studies, with the added advantage of less frequent dosing improving compliance.

⏰ At initiation
3
Refer promptly to ophthalmology for any ocular involvement or Hutchinson's sign

All cases with intraocular involvement (uveitis, keratitis, scleritis) or a positive Hutchinson's sign should be promptly evaluated by ophthalmology given the strong association with ocular disease and potential for vision-threatening complications.

⏰ Same day to within 24-48 hours depending on severity
4
Treat ocular complications with topical agents, not topical antivirals

Topical antivirals are generally not used for HZO (unlike HSV) since the mechanism is largely immune-mediated rather than ongoing viral replication at the ocular surface in most cases. Keratitis, uveitis, or scleritis from HZO is treated with topical corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone acetate) rather than topical antivirals.

⏰ Once ocular involvement is identified
5
Consider oral corticosteroids as an adjunct for acute pain

Combining oral corticosteroids with antivirals has been shown to reduce the duration of pain during the acute phase, though this doesn't change long-term postherpetic neuralgia outcomes; this decision is individualized based on patient risk factors for steroid use.

⏰ Individualized, typically initiated alongside antivirals
6
Manage the skin/adnexal component supportively

Cool compresses, analgesics for pain, lubricating and antibiotic ophthalmic ointment (e.g., erythromycin) if blinking is impaired or to prevent bacterial superinfection of skin lesions; avoid non-ophthalmic topical antibiotics/adhesive dressings on facial rash, which can exacerbate the rash and delay healing.

⏰ Throughout the acute course
7
Monitor IOP and pupil dilation status

IOP should be monitored and treated if elevated; cycloplegia (atropine 1% or scopolamine 0.25%, three times daily) is used for comfort and to reduce synechiae risk if uveitis is present.

⏰ Ongoing through active ocular disease

  • Uveitis, scleritis/episcleritis, and secondary glaucoma

  • Unlike HSV keratitis, topical antivirals are generally not useful for HZO's ocular complications; treatment there leans on topical steroids instead, reflecting the more immune-mediated nature of the disease in the eye.

  • Current AAO EyeNet guidance confirms early (within 72h) antiviral therapy reduces pain, speeds rash healing, and decreases ocular involvement, without changing PHN incidence.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Most cases are self-limited whether or not antibiotics are given — the real management task is recognizing which minority (neonates, contact lens wearers, severe/hyperacute disease) actually needs urgent, aggressive treatment.

Direct bacterial infection of the conjunctiva, most commonly Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species in adults, Haemophilus influenzae in children, and Neisseria gonorrhoeae in a distinct hyperacute, rapidly sight-threatening form (classically in neonates or sexually active adults) capable of penetrating an intact corneal epithelium and causing corneal perforation within days if untreated.

Risk Factors
  • Close contact settings (daycare, schools, households) for typical bacterial conjunctivitis
  • Contact lens wear specifically raises concern for Pseudomonas and should change empiric antibiotic choice
  • Neonates (ophthalmia neonatorum) from maternal genital tract organisms (Chlamydia, Neisseria gonorrhoeae) acquired during birth
  • Sexual activity/exposure for gonococcal or chlamydial conjunctivitis in adults
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; bacterial conjunctivitis is confined to the conjunctival surface and does not produce fundus findings. A dilated/posterior exam is not part of routine workup unless another diagnosis is suspected.

History
  • Onset and laterality (often starts unilateral then becomes bilateral), discharge character, contact lens wear, and any sexual history/neonatal context relevant to gonococcal/chlamydial disease.
Exam
  • Purulent discharge that reaccumulates within minutes of wiping, papillary conjunctival reaction, and typically no significant preauricular adenopathy (a discriminator from viral conjunctivitis).

Distinguish typical from hyperacute/high-risk presentations

Typical bacterial conjunctivitis (mild-moderate purulence, no systemic symptoms) can be managed with routine topical antibiotics. ⏰ At initial assessment

3

Use a fluoroquinolone with reliable Pseudomonas coverage and advise discontinuing lens wear until resolved and cleared by follow-up, given the elevated risk of a more severe or vision-threatening presentation in this group.

4
Treat suspected gonococcal conjunctivitis aggressively and urgently

This requires both systemic (typically a single dose of IM ceftriaxone per current gonorrhea treatment guidelines) AND topical antibiotic therapy, urgent same-day ophthalmology involvement, and testing/treatment for concurrent chlamydial infection given frequent co-infection; this is not managed with topical antibiotics alone.

Viral Conjunctivitis

There's no antiviral treatment that changes the course — the actual job here is patient education about contagion precautions and recognizing when subepithelial infiltrates from EKC are going to cause symptoms for weeks after the red eye itself has resolved.

Adenovirus is the dominant cause, spread highly efficiently via direct contact and fomites (towels, doorknobs, tonometer tips), producing a follicular conjunctival reaction with watery discharge and a tender preauricular lymph node — a triad rarely seen together in bacterial disease. Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC), a more severe adenoviral subtype, can produce subepithelial corneal infiltrates that persist for weeks to months after the acute infection resolves, causing glare and photophobia long after the eye is no longer contagious.

Risk Factors
  • Close contact with an infected individual or contaminated surfaces (schools, healthcare settings, shared towels)
  • Healthcare exposure specifically for EKC, which has caused documented outbreaks traced to improperly disinfected tonometer tips and other shared equipment
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; viral conjunctivitis is confined to the conjunctiva (and cornea, in EKC) and does not produce fundus findings.

History
  • Recent sick contacts, timing of onset (often unilateral initially, becoming bilateral within days), and any healthcare/tonometry exposure.
Exam
  • Watery discharge, follicular conjunctival reaction, tender preauricular lymph node.
  • Fluorescein exam to assess for corneal involvement (subepithelial infiltrates in EKC, typically appearing days after the initial red eye, not at presentation).
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis; point-of-care adenovirus immunoassay testing is available in some settings but not routinely necessary for management decisions.
1
Confirm the diagnosis and exclude bacterial/other mimics

Watery discharge with a follicular reaction and tender preauricular node supports viral etiology over bacterial; fluorescein exam to exclude a dendrite (HSV) or other corneal process.

2
Supportive care only for typical cases

No antiviral treatment changes the course of typical adenoviral conjunctivitis; management is supportive: cool compresses, artificial tears, and antihistamine/decongestant drops for symptomatic relief if needed.

This is highly contagious via fomites; counsel on frequent hand hygiene, avoiding shared towels/pillowcases, not touching the unaffected eye after touching the affected one, and staying home from school/work/daycare per local/institutional policy while actively weeping discharge is present (typically the first 10-14 days).

4
Set expectations for EKC-associated subepithelial infiltrates

If subepithelial infiltrates develop (more likely with more severe presentations/EKC), warn patients these can cause glare and photophobia for weeks to months after the acute redness resolves, and that the patient is generally no longer contagious by the time these develop.

⏰ Counseling at diagnosis, managed by ophthalmology if they develop

Reserved for ophthalmology-directed use when infiltrates are visually significant; not started reflexively or in the ED given steroid use requires excluding infectious mimics first and careful tapering to avoid rebound.

Anterior Uveitis / Iritis (Non-Traumatic)

HLA-B27 and its associated spondyloarthropathies account for a large share of recurrent cases, but the acute ED task is symptom control and safe steroid initiation, not chasing down the systemic diagnosis in a single visit.

Inflammation of the iris and ciliary body from an autoimmune, infectious, or (less commonly in this non-traumatic category) idiopathic process. HLA-B27-associated disease is the single most common identifiable systemic association, accounting for a substantial proportion (commonly cited around one-third to as high as half in some populations) of acute anterior uveitis cases, and is linked to the seronegative spondyloarthropathies: ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease-associated arthritis. HLA-B27-associated disease has a classically recurrent, alternating (side-switching between episodes), unilateral pattern.

Risk Factors
  • HLA-B27 positivity and any associated seronegative spondyloarthropathy (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease)
  • Sarcoidosis, Behçet disease, and other systemic autoimmune/inflammatory conditions
  • Infectious causes: herpetic (HSV/VZV) anterior uveitis, syphilis, tuberculosis
  • Prior episodes (strongest predictor of recurrence, particularly in HLA-B27-associated disease)
Fundoscopic Findings

Typically normal in isolated anterior uveitis; posterior segment involvement (vitritis, papillitis, vasculitis) occurs in a minority of HLA-B27-associated cases (reported in a meaningful minority, historically cited around 17% in some series) and should prompt consideration of a panuveitis/posterior uveitis process rather than isolated anterior disease, with correspondingly more aggressive systemic workup and treatment.

History
  • Prior episodes (unilateral, alternating pattern is classic for HLA-B27-associated disease), and directed review of systems for spondyloarthropathy symptoms: inflammatory back pain/stiffness, joint pain/swelling, psoriasis, and GI symptoms (diarrhea, blood in stool) suggesting IBD.
  • Any history suggesting an infectious cause: cold sores/genital herpes history, tuberculosis exposure/risk factors, sexual history relevant to syphilis risk.
Exam
  • Slit lamp exam for cell and flare, perilimbal flush, keratic precipitates (their pattern/distribution can suggest granulomatous vs. non-granulomatous disease, relevant to the differential), posterior synechiae, and pupil irregularity.
  • IOP measurement (can be elevated from trabecular inflammation or, less commonly, low from ciliary shutdown).
  • Assess for signs of associated systemic disease on exam where relevant (e.g., skin/joint findings) if history suggests it.
Imaging/Labs
  • For a first episode, non-severe, and no directing history/exam findings: extensive systemic workup is often not necessary, and empiric topical treatment with close follow-up is reasonable.
  • For recurrent, severe, bilateral, or granulomatous-appearing disease, or a directing history: consider HLA-B27 testing, and depending on directing symptoms, further workup such as inflammatory markers, ANA, RPR/treponemal testing, chest imaging, and referral to rheumatology if a systemic association is identified or strongly suspected.
1

Rule out HSV/VZV keratouveitis (check for dendrites, reduced corneal sensation) and confirm no relevant trauma history, since these change management (antivirals, avoiding steroid monotherapy) compared to typical inflammatory anterior uveitis.

A common starting regimen is prednisolone acetate 1%, one drop every 1–2 hours while awake for significant inflammation, tapered gradually over several weeks as the anterior chamber reaction improves.

Cyclopentolate 1% twice daily in the affected eye for comfort and to reduce posterior synechiae formation risk during the inflammatory period.

First episode, unilateral, non-granulomatous, responding well to topical therapy: often reasonable to defer extensive systemic workup pending recurrence. Episcleritis

This is the reassuring half of the episcleritis/scleritis pair — the phenylephrine blanching test that distinguishes them is the single most useful two-minute test in general ophthalmic triage.

Inflammation of the episclera, the thin vascular layer between the conjunctiva and sclera, is usually idiopathic and self-limited, though a minority of cases (particularly recurrent nodular forms) are associated with systemic conditions like rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease. Unlike scleritis, episcleritis does not threaten vision and does not cause the severe pain or globe tenderness characteristic of its more serious counterpart.

Risk Factors
  • Idiopathic in the large majority of cases
  • Systemic association in a minority, particularly with recurrent or nodular presentations: rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and other connective tissue disease
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; episcleritis is confined to the superficial episcleral tissue and does not produce fundus findings or require dilated exam as part of routine workup.

History
  • Onset, laterality, and whether this is a first episode or recurrent (recurrent/nodular disease raises the likelihood of an associated systemic condition worth asking about directly: joint pain, GI symptoms).
Exam
  • Sectoral or diffuse redness with mild to no discomfort (often described as irritation rather than true pain), normal vision, normal pupil, and a quiet anterior chamber.
  • Phenylephrine 2.5–10% blanching test: instill a drop and reassess in several minutes — blanching of the injected vessels confirms episcleritis (superficial plexus) rather than scleritis (deep plexus, does not blanch).
Imaging/Labs
  • Not required for a typical, first-episode presentation; consider directed workup (rheumatoid factor, inflammatory bowel disease screening per symptoms) only for recurrent or nodular disease with a suggestive history.
1
Confirm the diagnosis with the phenylephrine blanching test

Distinguishes episcleritis from scleritis, which changes the entire management approach and urgency — scleritis needs systemic anti-inflammatory therapy and workup, episcleritis does not.

⏰ At initial exam
2

Most cases resolve spontaneously within 1–3 weeks without specific treatment; artificial tears provide symptomatic relief for mild irritation.

⏰ Throughout the self-limited course
3

For patients with more bothersome discomfort, a short course of topical NSAID drops or a mild topical corticosteroid can speed symptomatic improvement, though this is a comfort measure rather than a necessity given the self-limited natural history.

⏰ As needed for symptom control
4
Investigate systemic association only for recurrent/nodular disease

A first, simple episode does not warrant systemic workup; recurrent or nodular episcleritis, particularly with a suggestive review of systems, warrants consideration of an associated systemic inflammatory condition.

Scleritis

The stepwise systemic therapy ladder here (NSAIDs, then steroids, then immunosuppressants) has real trial-level failure-rate data behind each rung, and necrotizing disease specifically demands aggressive immunosuppression from the start rather than working up the ladder slowly.

Inflammation of the sclera itself, unlike the superficial episclera, that can threaten vision through scleral thinning, secondary keratitis or uveitis, and (in the necrotizing form) globe perforation. Roughly half of scleritis cases are associated with an underlying systemic autoimmune disease, most commonly rheumatoid arthritis, but also granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), relapsing polychondritis, and other vasculitides — making new scleritis, especially in a patient without a known autoimmune diagnosis, a reasonable trigger for systemic workup.

Risk Factors
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (the most common systemic association overall)
  • Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and other ANCA-associated vasculitides, particularly relevant for necrotizing scleritis
  • Relapsing polychondritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, and other connective tissue diseases
  • Infectious scleritis (less common) following ocular surgery or trauma
Fundoscopic Findings

Usually normal in anterior scleritis; posterior scleritis (a less common variant) can show choroidal folds, disc edema, or exudative retinal detachment, and should be considered when a patient has pain and vision changes without obvious anterior scleral findings — B-scan ultrasound showing scleral/choroidal thickening can help confirm posterior scleritis when the diagnosis isn't obvious on anterior exam alone.

History
  • Known autoimmune disease history; new scleritis without a known diagnosis should prompt a directed review of systems for joint pain/swelling, sinus/renal/pulmonary symptoms (GPA), and cartilage inflammation symptoms (relapsing polychondritis: ear/nose cartilage pain or deformity).
  • Nocturnal pain pattern (classically wakes patients from sleep) supports scleritis over episcleritis.
Exam
  • Violaceous/bluish-red scleral hue best seen in natural daylight, scleral edema, and a NEGATIVE phenylephrine blanching test (distinguishing from episcleritis).
  • Globe tenderness to palpation, assessment for associated keratitis (peripheral ulcerative keratitis can accompany scleritis, particularly in GPA) or anterior uveitis.
  • Assess visual acuity and, if posterior scleritis is suspected, consider B-scan ultrasound.
Imaging/Labs
  • For new-onset scleritis without a known systemic diagnosis: ANCA (specifically for GPA given its ocular-presenting potential), rheumatoid factor/anti-CCP, ESR/CRP, and consideration of further targeted workup (renal function, urinalysis, chest imaging) based on directing symptoms.
  • B-scan ultrasound if posterior scleritis is suspected clinically.
1
Confirm the diagnosis with the phenylephrine blanching test

A persistently red/violaceous eye after phenylephrine, with globe tenderness and often nocturnal pain, supports scleritis over episcleritis.

⏰ At initial exam
2

Oral NSAIDs (indomethacin, ibuprofen, naproxen, and others have all been used) are the recommended initial therapy for diffuse and nodular anterior scleritis based on comparative failure-rate data, with steroids reserved as second-line if NSAIDs fail.

⏰ First-line at diagnosis for non-necrotizing disease
3

If NSAIDs fail to control diffuse or nodular scleritis, oral corticosteroids are added or substituted, then tapered and discontinued as soon as possible while maintaining remission with continued NSAIDs.

⏰ Second-line, if NSAID monotherapy fails
4
Escalate to immunosuppressive therapy for further failures or necrotizing disease

Immunosuppressive agents (methotrexate and others, per the SITE study and subsequent literature) are the third-line option for NSAID/steroid failures in diffuse or nodular scleritis, but should be considered FIRST-LINE (alongside or instead of NSAIDs/steroids) for necrotizing scleritis specifically, given the near-universal treatment failure rates for NSAIDs (100% failure in one large series) and very high failure rates for steroids alone (91%) in this more severe subtype.

⏰ Third-line for non-necrotizing disease; consider first-line for necrotizing disease
5

Given the roughly 50% rate of associated systemic autoimmune disease, involve rheumatology for new-onset scleritis, particularly necrotizing disease or any case with a positive ANCA/directing systemic finding, since the underlying systemic vasculitis (e.g., GPA) itself requires treatment beyond the ocular disease.

⏰ Early in the workup, particularly for necrotizing or systemically-associated disease
6

For associated ciliary spasm/discomfort; manage any coexisting keratitis or uveitis per those entries' protocols.

Blepharitis

This is fundamentally a chronic disease-management problem, not an acute cure — setting expectations about ongoing lid hygiene rather than promising resolution is the actual clinical skill here.

Chronic inflammation of the eyelid margins, classified as anterior (affecting the lash follicles and associated glands, often staphylococcal or seborrheic) or posterior (meibomian gland dysfunction, MGD, where altered meibomian gland lipid secretion destabilizes the tear film). The two frequently coexist, and MGD in particular is a major contributor to evaporative dry eye disease given the tear film's dependence on a healthy lipid layer.

Risk Factors
  • Age (MGD prevalence increases with age)
  • Rosacea (strongly associated with meibomian gland dysfunction specifically)
  • Seborrheic dermatitis
  • Contact lens wear, prior ocular surgery, and chronic ocular surface disease as contributing/aggravating factors
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; blepharitis is confined to the eyelid margin and does not involve or require assessment of the fundus.

History
  • Chronicity, morning versus end-of-day symptom pattern, associated skin conditions (rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis), and contact lens wear.
Exam
  • Anterior blepharitis: lash collarettes, lid margin erythema/telangiectasia, possible lash misdirection or loss with chronic disease.
  • Posterior blepharitis/MGD: meibomian gland orifice inspissation, gland dropout (assessed by meibography where available), and expressed meibum quality (normal clear oil vs. thickened, toothpaste-like, or absent secretion on gentle expression).
  • Assess tear film quality/stability and look for associated dry eye signs given the frequent overlap.
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis based on lid margin exam and meibomian gland expression; specialized imaging (meibography) is available in some ophthalmology/optometry settings but not required for diagnosis or initial management.
1
Start with lid hygiene as the foundation of therapy

Warm compresses (sufficient duration/temperature to soften meibum, commonly 5-10 minutes) followed by lid margin cleansing (diluted baby shampoo, commercial lid scrub products, or in-office/at-home mechanical debridement devices) forms the base of therapy for both anterior and posterior blepharitis.

⏰ Ongoing, daily, indefinitely for chronic disease control
2

Gentle manual expression of the meibomian glands (in-office or via patient self-massage after warm compresses) helps clear inspissated glands and improve secretion quality.

⏰ Regularly, as part of ongoing management
3
Consider topical antibiotic/anti-inflammatory therapy for inadequate response

Topical azithromycin has anti-inflammatory properties beyond its antibacterial effect and has shown benefit for MGD-associated posterior blepharitis; topical erythromycin ointment is a reasonable option for anterior blepharitis with significant lash collarette formation.

⏰ Added if lid hygiene alone is insufficient
4
Consider oral doxycycline for refractory MGD

Low-dose oral doxycycline (anti-inflammatory rather than purely antimicrobial mechanism at these doses) is used for moderate-to-severe or refractory MGD; both continuous low-dose and pulsed higher-dose regimens have been studied, though a formal treatment consensus on optimal regimen is still evolving.

⏰ For refractory cases, typically a multi-week to multi-month course

Given the frequent overlap between MGD and evaporative dry eye, artificial tears and other dry-eye-directed therapy are often used alongside blepharitis-specific treatment.

⏰ Concurrent, ongoing
6

Blepharitis, particularly MGD, is typically a chronic condition requiring ongoing maintenance therapy (lid hygiene) rather than a one-time cure; relapse is expected if hygiene measures are discontinued.

Dacryocystitis

Don't probe the acutely infected sac — it's essentially always secondary to an underlying obstruction, and instrumenting it during the acute infection risks precisely the kind of cellulitis you're trying to prevent.

Infection of the lacrimal sac, almost always secondary to an underlying nasolacrimal duct obstruction that prevents normal tear drainage and allows stagnant tears to become a culture medium for bacteria (typically Staphylococcus or Streptococcus species). Untreated, infection can spread to the surrounding periorbital tissue (preseptal cellulitis) or, less commonly but seriously, to the orbit (orbital cellulitis) or intracranially (via posterior spread to the cavernous sinus), making the diagnosis of dacryocystitis itself an implicit diagnosis of underlying nasolacrimal duct obstruction that will eventually need addressing.

Risk Factors
  • Nasolacrimal duct obstruction from any cause: age-related involutional stenosis (most common in adults), prior facial trauma, chronic sinus disease, or (in infants) congenital nasolacrimal duct obstruction
  • Female sex and older age, reflecting the epidemiology of involutional nasolacrimal duct stenosis
  • Prior dacryocystitis episodes (recurrence is common without definitive surgical correction of the underlying obstruction)
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; dacryocystitis is confined to the lacrimal sac and surrounding periorbital soft tissue. If there's any concern the infection has spread posteriorly to involve the orbit (proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, decreased vision), this represents progression to orbital cellulitis, a distinct and more serious diagnosis requiring its own workup and IV therapy, at which point posterior segment/fundus assessment becomes relevant as part of that broader workup.

History
  • Chronic tearing (epiphora) preceding the acute infection is common and reflects the underlying, often longstanding, nasolacrimal duct obstruction.
  • Prior similar episodes, and any red-flag symptoms for malignancy (bloody discharge/regurgitation, a firm or fixed mass, recurrence after prior dacryocystorhinostomy) that would warrant a different diagnostic pathway.
Exam
  • Pain, erythema, and edema centered over the medial canthal region, over the lacrimal sac specifically (distinguishing it from the more diffuse periorbital involvement of preseptal cellulitis, though the two can coexist/overlap).
  • Gentle pressure over the lacrimal sac may express purulent material from the punctum — helpful diagnostically but should be done gently, not aggressively.
  • Assess for signs of orbital involvement (proptosis, restricted extraocular movements, decreased vision, significant pain with eye movement) that would indicate progression to orbital cellulitis and a substantially more urgent situation.
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis; CT orbit/sinus with contrast is warranted if there's any concern for orbital extension, abscess formation, or diagnostic uncertainty (e.g., to exclude a mass lesion in atypical or red-flag presentations).
  • Culture of any expressed purulent material can guide antibiotic selection, particularly for recurrent or treatment-refractory cases.
1
Start systemic antibiotics promptly

Mild-to-moderate cases: oral antibiotics with reliable gram-positive coverage (amoxicillin-clavulanate, cephalexin, or in penicillin-allergic patients ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole). Severe cases, systemic toxicity, or any concern for orbital extension: IV antibiotics and consideration of admission.

⏰ As soon as the diagnosis is made
2
Apply warm compresses and gentle massage

Warm compresses to the medial canthal region, combined with gentle Crigler massage (directed pressure over the lacrimal sac toward the nose) can help promote drainage of purulent material through the nasolacrimal system.

⏰ Throughout the acute treatment course
3
Do NOT probe or irrigate the lacrimal system during acute infection

Lacrimal probing is discouraged during the acute phase, since the underlying process may involve a mucocele that is not sterile, and instrumentation risks inciting a worse cellulitis rather than helping.

⏰ Avoided throughout the acute infectious phase
4
Consider incision and drainage for a pointing abscess or antibiotic-refractory cases

If a fluctuant abscess develops or the infection doesn't respond adequately to antibiotics alone, incision and drainage with culture of the material provides symptomatic relief and diagnostic information, though this is generally an ophthalmology/oculoplastics-performed procedure given the delicate anatomy involved.

⏰ If abscess forms or antibiotics alone are inadequate
5
Monitor closely for signs of orbital extension

Any development of proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, decreased vision, or worsening pain with eye movement should prompt immediate reevaluation for orbital cellulitis, which requires a substantially more aggressive workup (CT, IV antibiotics, likely admission) than uncomplicated dacryocystitis.

⏰ Throughout treatment, at every reassessment
6
Plan for definitive surgical correction after the acute infection resolves

Since dacryocystitis is fundamentally a disease of nasolacrimal duct obstruction, definitive treatment is dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR), typically performed electively after the acute infection has been treated and resolved, though it is occasionally performed during the acute phase in select cases per the operating surgeon's judgment. DCR has a high reported success rate (roughly 87-95% in various series).

Complications to Anticipate
  • Progression to preseptal or (less commonly) orbital cellulitis if inadequately treated
  • Rare but serious posterior spread to cavernous sinus thrombosis or meningitis in severely neglected cases
  • Recurrent dacryocystitis if the underlying nasolacrimal duct obstruction is never definitively addressed with surgery
  • Fistula formation with chronic or recurrent untreated disease
Clinical Pearls
  • Don't probe or irrigate an acutely infected lacrimal sac — this is one of the more counterintuitive management points here, since probing feels like it should help clear an obstruction but instead risks provoking a worse cellulitis in the acute infectious setting.
  • A diagnosis of dacryocystitis is implicitly a diagnosis of underlying nasolacrimal duct obstruction — treating only the acute infection without arranging eventual DCR referral sets the patient up for recurrence.
  • Red flag features (bloody regurgitation, a firm/fixed mass, recurrence after prior DCR) should prompt consideration of lacrimal sac malignancy rather than assuming straightforward recurrent infection.
Evidence Notes
  • Case series of external dacryocystorhinostomy for dacryocystitis report surgical success rates around 87-95%, supporting DCR as reliable definitive therapy once the acute infection has been treated.

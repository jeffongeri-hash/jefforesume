01 · Overview

Where Everything In This Volume Acts

Every drug class below is trying to interrupt the same immune activation cascade covered across this series — just at a different point in it.

“This patient is on adalimumab for Crohn's — what does that actually block, and what does it leave working?”

Figure 1. Five chokepoints, five drug categories — corticosteroids are the only class that hits all of them at once.
Why this matters practically: a patient on two drugs that act at different chokepoints (e.g., a TNF inhibitor plus a corticosteroid) has compounding immunosuppression even though neither dose looks extreme in isolation — the infection-risk framework from Volume II applies cumulatively, not just to whichever single drug is most recently dosed.
Verify before use: dosing throughout this volume reflects commonly used adult regimens and is a teaching reference, not a prescribing source — confirm current dosing, renal/hepatic adjustment, and indication-specific regimens against current product labeling before prescribing.
02 · Cytokine-Targeted Biologics

TNF Inhibitors

The first biologic class to transform autoimmune disease management — and still the most commonly encountered in the ED.

TNF-α drives fever, endothelial activation, and downstream inflammatory cascades (see Volume I). These agents bind and neutralize TNF-α before it can signal, reducing inflammation across rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, and psoriatic/ ankylosing spondyloarthritis. Class-wide risks: reactivation of latent TB and hepatitis B (screen before starting), increased serious infection risk, rare demyelinating disease and heart failure exacerbation.

Infliximab
MechanismChimeric monoclonal antibody, IV infusion
IndicationsRA, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis/PsA; also used as a second-line agent for steroid-refractory colitis irAEs (Volume III)
DosingLoading: 5 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6. Maintenance: 5 mg/kg IV q8 weeks (up to 10 mg/kg in IBD)
Key adverse effectsInfusion reactions, increased infection risk, TB/HBV reactivation, rare hepatotoxicity
Adalimumab
MechanismFully human monoclonal antibody, SC injection
IndicationsRA, Crohn's, UC, psoriasis/PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, uveitis
DosingLoading: 160 mg day 1 (or split over 2 days), 80 mg at week 2. Maintenance: 40 mg SC every 2 weeks
Key adverse effectsInjection site reactions, infection risk, TB/HBV reactivation
Etanercept
MechanismTNF receptor fusion protein (not a monoclonal antibody), SC injection
IndicationsRA, psoriasis/PsA, ankylosing spondylitis
Dosing50 mg SC weekly (or 25 mg twice weekly) — no loading dose required given the mechanism
Key adverse effectsInjection site reactions, infection risk; generally not effective for Crohn's disease despite being a TNF inhibitor
Certolizumab pegol
MechanismPEGylated Fab fragment (no Fc region — doesn't cross the placenta as readily), SC injection
IndicationsRA, Crohn's, psoriasis/PsA, ankylosing spondylitis
DosingLoading: 400 mg SC at weeks 0, 2, 4. Maintenance: 200 mg SC every 2 weeks
Key adverse effectsInfection risk, TB/HBV reactivation; favored in pregnancy given minimal placental transfer
Golimumab
MechanismFully human monoclonal antibody, SC or IV
IndicationsRA, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, ulcerative colitis
DosingSC: 50 mg monthly (no loading). IV: 2 mg/kg at weeks 0, 4, then every 8 weeks
Key adverse effectsInfection risk, TB/HBV reactivation
02 · Cytokine-Targeted Biologics

IL-6 Inhibitors

The class most ED clinicians now recognize from cytokine release syndrome management (Volume III).

Tocilizumab
MechanismHumanized monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor
IndicationsRA, giant cell arteritis, systemic/polyarticular JIA; first-line for Grade ≥2 cytokine release syndrome (Volume III); severe COVID-19
DosingRA: 4–8 mg/kg IV q4 weeks, or SC weekly/biweekly. CRS: 8 mg/kg IV (max 800 mg), may repeat
Key adverse effectsIncreased infection risk, GI perforation (rare but notable in patients with diverticulitis history), transaminitis, cytopenias
Sarilumab
MechanismHuman monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor
IndicationsRA (typically after inadequate response to a TNF inhibitor)
Dosing200 mg SC every 2 weeks
Key adverse effectsNeutropenia, transaminitis, increased infection risk
02 · Cytokine-Targeted Biologics

IL-17 Inhibitors

Targets the Th17 axis directly — see Volume I's T-cell differentiation section for the underlying biology.

Secukinumab
MechanismHuman monoclonal antibody against IL-17A
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis
DosingLoading: 300 mg SC weekly at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. Maintenance: 300 mg SC monthly
Key adverse effectsIncreased risk of mucocutaneous candidiasis (IL-17 is central to antifungal mucosal defense — Volume I), may worsen IBD
Ixekizumab
MechanismHumanized monoclonal antibody against IL-17A
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis
DosingLoading: 160 mg SC week 0, then 80 mg at weeks 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Maintenance: 80 mg SC every 4 weeks
Key adverse effectsCandidiasis risk, injection site reactions, may worsen IBD
Brodalumab
MechanismHuman monoclonal antibody against the IL-17 receptor (blocks multiple IL-17 isoforms, not just IL-17A)
IndicationsModerate-to-severe plaque psoriasis
DosingLoading: 210 mg SC at weeks 0, 1, 2. Maintenance: 210 mg SC every 2 weeks
Key adverse effectsCarries a boxed warning for suicidal ideation/behavior — available only through a restricted REMS program; candidiasis risk
Bimekizumab
MechanismHumanized monoclonal antibody against both IL-17A and IL-17F
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis
Dosing320 mg SC every 4 weeks (dosing intervals vary by indication and may extend to every 8 weeks after initial period)
Key adverse effectsHighest candidiasis rate of the class (dual IL-17A/F blockade); injection site reactions
02 · Cytokine-Targeted Biologics

IL-23 / IL-12-23 Inhibitors

Upstream of IL-17 — blocking the signal that drives Th17 differentiation in the first place, rather than the downstream effector cytokine itself.

Ustekinumab
MechanismHuman monoclonal antibody against the shared p40 subunit of IL-12 and IL-23 (blocks both cytokines)
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA, Crohn's, ulcerative colitis
DosingLoading: weight-based IV infusion. Maintenance: 90 mg SC (or 45 mg depending on weight) every 8–12 weeks
Key adverse effectsInfection risk (less candidiasis-specific than pure IL-17 blockers); rare posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome
Guselkumab
MechanismHuman monoclonal antibody against IL-23 (p19 subunit — spares IL-12)
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA
DosingLoading: 100 mg SC at weeks 0, 4. Maintenance: 100 mg SC every 8 weeks
Key adverse effectsInfection risk; generally well tolerated
Risankizumab
MechanismHumanized monoclonal antibody against IL-23 (p19 subunit)
IndicationsPsoriasis, PsA, Crohn's disease
DosingInduction dosing (IV or SC depending on indication), then 150 mg SC every 12 weeks maintenance
Key adverse effectsInfection risk; generally well tolerated
Tildrakizumab
MechanismHumanized monoclonal antibody against IL-23 (p19 subunit)
IndicationsModerate-to-severe plaque psoriasis
DosingLoading: 100 mg SC at weeks 0, 4. Maintenance: 100 mg SC every 12 weeks
Key adverse effectsInfection risk; generally well tolerated
03 · Small-Molecule Immunosuppressants

JAK Inhibitors

Oral small molecules that hit the shared intracellular pathway downstream of many cytokine receptors at once — see Volume I's JAK-STAT diagram.

Tofacitinib
MechanismJAK1/JAK3 inhibitor (oral)
IndicationsRA, PsA, ulcerative colitis, ankylosing spondylitis
Dosing5 mg PO twice daily (immediate-release), or 11 mg PO once daily (extended-release/XR formulation)
Key adverse effectsBoxed warning: increased risk of major cardiovascular events, malignancy, and thrombosis (VTE) versus TNF inhibitors in higher-risk patients; herpes zoster risk; infection risk
Baricitinib
MechanismJAK1/JAK2 inhibitor (oral)
IndicationsRA, severe alopecia areata, severe COVID-19 (hospitalized, hypoxemic patients)
Dosing2–4 mg PO once daily depending on indication
Key adverse effectsSimilar class boxed warning (CV events, malignancy, thrombosis, infection); herpes zoster risk
Upadacitinib
MechanismJAK1-selective inhibitor (oral)
IndicationsRA, PsA, ankylosing spondylitis, atopic dermatitis, ulcerative colitis, Crohn's
Dosing15 mg PO once daily (extended-release), higher doses used for some indications (e.g., atopic dermatitis, UC induction)
Key adverse effectsSame class boxed warning; herpes zoster risk; infection risk
Ruxolitinib
MechanismJAK1/JAK2 inhibitor (oral)
IndicationsMyelofibrosis, polycythemia vera, steroid-refractory acute and chronic GVHD (Volume V)
DosingMyelofibrosis: dosed by baseline platelet count. GVHD: commonly started at 5 mg PO twice daily and titrated
Key adverse effectsCytopenias (dose-limiting in myeloproliferative indications), infection risk, PRES has been reported (Volume V)
Class-wide myth worth flagging: “JAK inhibitors are just a more convenient oral alternative to biologics with a similar safety profile.” The boxed warning for major cardiovascular events, malignancy, and thrombosis (driven largely by the ORAL Surveillance trial with tofacitinib) means JAK inhibitors are generally reserved for patients who've had an inadequate response to a TNF inhibitor, not used as an equivalent first-line alternative.
Verify before use: JAK inhibitor boxed warning applicability and specific dosing by indication continue to be refined — confirm current FDA labeling and indication-specific dosing before prescribing.
04 · Oncologic Immunotherapy

Checkpoint Inhibitors

The mechanism and toxicity (irAEs) are covered in full in Volume III — this entry is the pharmacology quick-reference.

Pembrolizumab
MechanismAnti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody
IndicationsBroad oncology use across many solid tumor types
Dosing200 mg IV every 3 weeks, or 400 mg IV every 6 weeks
Key adverse effectsirAEs across any organ — see Volume III in full
Nivolumab
MechanismAnti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody
IndicationsBroad oncology use, often combined with ipilimumab
Dosing240 mg IV every 2 weeks, or 480 mg IV every 4 weeks
Key adverse effectsirAEs across any organ; combination with ipilimumab substantially increases irAE incidence/severity (Volume III)
Ipilimumab
MechanismAnti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody
IndicationsMelanoma (monotherapy or combined with nivolumab), renal cell carcinoma
Dosing3 mg/kg IV every 3 weeks (monotherapy dosing; lower in combination regimens)
Key adverse effectsHighest irAE rate of the checkpoint class, especially colitis and hypophysitis (Volume III)
Atezolizumab / Durvalumab / Avelumab
MechanismAnti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibodies
IndicationsVarious solid tumors, often in combination with chemotherapy
DosingFixed or weight-based IV dosing every 2–4 weeks depending on agent and indication
Key adverse effectsSimilar irAE spectrum to PD-1 inhibitors
05 · Transplant Immunosuppression

Calcineurin Inhibitors

Full toxicity profile (including PRES) is covered in Volume V — dosing/monograph detail here.

Tacrolimus
MechanismCalcineurin inhibitor — blocks NFAT-driven IL-2 transcription
IndicationsSolid organ transplant rejection prophylaxis; GVHD prophylaxis/treatment
DosingPO ~0.1–0.2 mg/kg/day divided twice daily, trough-adjusted (target varies by transplant type/time post-transplant); extended-release once-daily formulation available; IV available for patients who can't tolerate oral
Key adverse effectsNephrotoxicity, neurotoxicity/PRES (Volume V), hyperglycemia, hyperkalemia, hypertension
Cyclosporine
MechanismCalcineurin inhibitor — same mechanism, different binding protein than tacrolimus
IndicationsSolid organ transplant rejection prophylaxis; severe psoriasis/RA (non-transplant use)
DosingPO 3–5 mg/kg/day divided twice daily, trough-adjusted
Key adverse effectsNephrotoxicity, hypertension (more pronounced than tacrolimus), gingival hyperplasia, hirsutism
05 · Transplant Immunosuppression

mTOR Inhibitors

Sirolimus
MechanismmTOR inhibitor — blocks IL-2 receptor signal transduction and cell cycle progression
IndicationsSolid organ transplant (calcineurin-sparing regimens), lymphangioleiomyomatosis
DosingLoading dose followed by daily maintenance, trough-adjusted
Key adverse effectsImpaired wound healing (avoid in the immediate post-surgical period), hyperlipidemia, thrombocytopenia, interstitial pneumonitis (Volume V)
Everolimus
MechanismmTOR inhibitor, shorter half-life than sirolimus
IndicationsSolid organ transplant, certain oncology indications (breast cancer, neuroendocrine tumors)
DosingPO twice daily, trough-adjusted
Key adverse effectsSame class profile as sirolimus — impaired wound healing, hyperlipidemia, pneumonitis
05 · Transplant Immunosuppression

Antimetabolites

Block the DNA/purine synthesis needed for the clonal expansion of activated lymphocytes.

Mycophenolate mofetil
MechanismInhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, blocking purine synthesis preferentially in lymphocytes
IndicationsSolid organ transplant (calcineurin-sparing/nephron-sparing regimens), lupus nephritis, other autoimmune disease
Dosing1000 mg PO twice daily is a typical transplant maintenance dose (varies by indication and regimen)
Key adverse effectsGI toxicity (diarrhea — can mimic GVHD/infection, Volume V), leukopenia; teratogenic — requires contraception counseling
Azathioprine
MechanismPurine analog, converted to 6-mercaptopurine, incorporated into DNA to halt lymphocyte proliferation
IndicationsSolid organ transplant, autoimmune disease (IBD, autoimmune hepatitis)
Dosing1–3 mg/kg/day PO
Key adverse effectsMyelosuppression (markedly increased in TPMT-deficient patients — consider testing before starting), hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis
Methotrexate
MechanismDihydrofolate reductase inhibitor — blocks folate-dependent DNA synthesis
IndicationsRA, psoriasis/PsA, many autoimmune conditions (also high-dose oncology use, a distinct dosing paradigm not covered here)
DosingWeekly dosing for autoimmune indications, typically 7.5–25 mg PO or SC once per week, with folic acid supplementation to reduce toxicity
Key adverse effectsFatal outcomes have resulted from accidental daily dosing instead of weekly — this is a recognized, preventable medication error worth actively screening for in any patient on methotrexate presenting with unexplained pancytopenia or mucositis
The single highest-stakes prescribing error in this entire volume: low-dose methotrexate for autoimmune disease is dosed weekly, not daily. Confusing the two is a well-documented, repeatedly fatal error — pancytopenia, mucositis, and multi-organ toxicity from inadvertent daily dosing is a real ED presentation, and the history (“I've been taking my arthritis pill every day like it says”) is the clue that the error happened, not evidence the regimen was correct.
06 · Broad-Spectrum Immunosuppression

Corticosteroids

The oldest class in this volume, and still the fastest-acting — relative potency and duration determine which agent fits which clinical situation.

Figure 2. Relative glucocorticoid potency, duration of action, and mineralocorticoid activity across commonly used agents.
AgentTypical ED/acute useWhy this agent for this use
HydrocortisoneStress-dose replacement in adrenal insufficiency/crisisMeaningful mineralocorticoid activity — supports blood pressure the way the other agents don't
PrednisoneOutpatient taper, moderate flaresOral, intermediate duration, well-established dosing familiarity
MethylprednisoloneIV pulse dosing for severe/organ-threatening flares (lupus, myocarditis, severe irAEs)Higher potency, available IV, minimal mineralocorticoid activity avoids unwanted sodium/fluid retention at high doses

Verify before use: relative potency figures are standard teaching approximations — confirm specific conversion ratios against a current equivalency reference before calculating an actual dose conversion.
07 · Adjunctive Immunotherapy

IVIG

Not a single mechanism but several running in parallel — which is part of why it works across such a wide range of conditions.

Figure 3. IVIG's several proposed mechanisms, converging on a broad range of autoimmune/inflammatory indications.
Use in this seriesTypical dosing
Myasthenic crisis (Volume IV)2 g/kg total dose, divided over 5 days
Guillain-Barré syndrome, CIDP2 g/kg total dose, divided over 2–5 days
Refractory ITP1 g/kg/day for 1–2 days
Kawasaki disease2 g/kg as a single infusion (with aspirin)
CAPS triple therapy (Volume IV)Dosing varies — often alongside or as an alternative to plasma exchange
Adverse effects to know: infusion reactions (slow the rate), aseptic meningitis, and — the one worth specifically remembering — volume overload and renal impairment, since IVIG is a large infused protein load; use caution in patients with heart failure or renal insufficiency, and check IgA levels first when feasible, since severe IgA deficiency (Volume I) carries anaphylaxis risk with IgA-containing products.
07 · Adjunctive Immunotherapy

Therapeutic Plasma Exchange

A mechanical filter, not a drug — but it belongs in this atlas because the decision to use it is a pharmacologic-equivalent choice in every condition covered across this series.

Figure 4. Plasma exchange physically removes pathogenic plasma components rather than modulating them biologically.
Condition (this series)Current role
Myasthenic crisis (Volume IV)First-line rapid therapy, comparable to IVIG (some data favors PLEX for functional outcomes, at higher complication cost)
Goodpasture/anti-GBM disease (Volume IV)Established mainstay — clearly beneficial, especially started early
ANCA-associated vasculitis (Volume IV)No longer routine after PEXIVAS (2020) — reserved for select severe cases
CAPS (Volume IV)One of three triple-therapy components; use with anticoagulation and steroids improves survival
Practical mechanics worth knowing: replacement fluid is typically albumin (preferred for its safety profile) unless the patient is also coagulopathic or the indication specifically calls for replacing consumed plasma factors, in which case fresh frozen plasma is used instead. A course is typically 5–7 exchanges over 1–2 weeks, and it removes not just the pathogenic antibody but also therapeutic drugs — timing other medications (including recently dosed biologics) around a PLEX session is a genuine coordination point with the treating specialist.
Verify before use: PLEX protocols (volume exchanged, replacement fluid choice, frequency) vary by indication and institution — this is apheresis-service-directed, not an ED-initiated decision in most cases.
08 · Practical Synthesis

The Common Thread Across This Volume

Every agent in this atlas trades autoimmune/rejection/tumor control for a predictable category of vulnerability — knowing the category tells you what to worry about before the chart does.

Practical synthesis

Before assuming a new ED presentation is unrelated to a patient's immunosuppressive regimen, ask which chokepoint their specific drug(s) hit (Figure 1) — that alone narrows the infection and toxicity differential substantially, and cross-references directly to the pathogen-mapping framework in Volume II and the organ-specific toxicity patterns in Volumes III and V. When multiple agents from different chokepoints are on board simultaneously, treat the immunosuppressive burden as cumulative, not just equal to whichever single drug seems most relevant to today's complaint.

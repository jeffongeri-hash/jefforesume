Lab Values & Corrections

Electrolyte & Blood Product Correction Atlas

Reference ranges, clinical considerations, correction routes (oral, liquid, IV) with typical doses, and expected-correction math for the electrolytes and blood products you replace most often — plus two quick calculators for the bedside math. Doses and rates are typical starting points; verify against your institution's protocol and current guidelines, and recheck labs after any significant correction.

Potassium

Reference Range

3.5 – 5.0 mEq/L (mmol/L)

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hypokalemia
Mild3.0 – 3.4 mEq/LUsually asymptomatic. Outpatient oral repletion reasonable if reliable follow-up.
Moderate2.5 – 2.9 mEq/LWeakness/ECG changes possible. IV repletion; consider admission, especially if symptomatic or on digoxin.
Severe<2.5 mEq/L — OR any arrhythmia/ECG change at any levelAdmit with continuous cardiac monitoring; aggressive IV repletion via central line if rate >10 mEq/hr needed.
Hyperkalemia
Mild5.1 – 5.5 mEq/LRecheck (rule out hemolysis/pseudohyperkalemia); review diet/meds (ACEi/ARB, K-sparing diuretics).
Moderate5.6 – 6.4 mEq/LObtain ECG; treat cause, consider binder therapy (patiromer/SPS); recheck level.
Severe≥6.5 mEq/L — OR any ECG changes (peaked T, widened QRS) at any levelImmediate treatment: IV calcium (membrane stabilization), insulin/dextrose, albuterol; continuous monitoring; consider dialysis.

Clinical Considerations

  • Almost entirely intracellular (~98%) — a serum level is a poor proxy for total body stores; large deficits can exist with a near-normal serum K⁺, and vice versa in acidosis (transcellular shift masks a true deficit).
  • Hypomagnesemia makes hypokalemia refractory to correction — magnesium is required for the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and renal K⁺-ATPase to retain potassium; always check/replace Mg²⁺ first or alongside in resistant cases.
  • ECG changes correlate loosely with severity: hypoK → flattened T, U waves, ST depression, prolonged QU (risk of torsades); hyperK → peaked T waves → widened QRS → sine wave → arrest.
  • Renal function and concurrent K⁺-altering drugs (ACEi/ARB, K-sparing diuretics, loop/thiazide diuretics, insulin, β-agonists) change both the safe replacement dose and monitoring interval.

Correction Methods & Routes

Oral (PO)

Preferred first-line if the patient can tolerate PO and the deficit is not severe/symptomatic. Typical dose: 20–40 mEq PO, may repeat. Onset slower than IV but a lower risk of local vein irritation and no rate-limited infusion ceiling.

Liquid / effervescent PO

Faster GI absorption than tablets and useful when swallowing tablets is difficult, but more GI upset (nausea) is common; often dosed as 20 mEq per 15–20 mL solution, diluted and taken with food.

IV — peripheral line

Max concentration ~10 mEq/100 mL, max rate 10 mEq/hr peripherally — faster peripheral rates cause significant vein irritation/phlebitis and burning.

IV — central line + continuous cardiac monitoring

Allows faster replacement (commonly up to 20 mEq/hr, occasionally 40 mEq/hr in critical hypokalemia with arrhythmia) — requires continuous ECG monitoring given arrhythmia risk from rapid extracellular rise.

Expected Correction

Classic teaching: 10 mEq of KCl raises serum K⁺ by ~0.1 mEq/L. Modern data are more modest — a retrospective cohort found the daily rise was closer to 0.05–0.07 mEq/L per 10 mEq given, and a meta-analysis of 20 RCTs found no dose-dependent relationship at all above a ceiling (i.e., higher doses don't reliably produce a bigger rise once absorption/renal excretion catch up).

Calculator

Estimated Potassium Replacement

Uses the 10 mEq ≈ 0.1 mEq/L rule of thumb — treat this as a rough starting estimate, not a guarantee (see pearl below).

Clinical / Safety Pearl

The 10:0.1 rule is a useful rough estimate for planning, not a guarantee — it is specifically LESS reliable in severe hypokalemia (K⁺ <2.5) where potassium preferentially refills the enormous intracellular pool first, and it assumes normal renal function and no concurrent K⁺-wasting losses (diuretics, GI losses). Recheck levels after any significant replacement rather than trusting the formula alone.

Calcium (+ Albumin Correction)

Reference Range

Total Ca: 8.5 – 10.5 mg/dL · Ionized Ca: 1.1 – 1.3 mmol/L

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hypocalcemia (corrected)
Mild8.0 – 8.4 mg/dLOften asymptomatic. Oral calcium ± vitamin D, outpatient follow-up.
Moderate7.0 – 7.9 mg/dLMay have paresthesias, Chvostek's/Trousseau's sign. Consider IV calcium; check Mg/phos/PTH.
Severe<7.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (tetany, seizure, QT prolongation/arrhythmia)Immediate IV calcium (gluconate peripherally / chloride via central line), admission, cardiac monitoring.
Hypercalcemia
Mild10.5 – 11.9 mg/dLOften asymptomatic; outpatient workup (PTH, malignancy screen) reasonable if stable.
Moderate12.0 – 13.9 mg/dL"Stones, bones, groans, psychiatric overtones" symptoms; IV fluids, treat cause, consider admission.
Severe≥14.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (AMS, severe dehydration) = hypercalcemic crisisICU admission; aggressive IV isotonic fluids, calcitonin/bisphosphonate, treat underlying cause emergently.

Clinical Considerations

  • ~40% of serum calcium is protein-bound (mostly to albumin); only the ionized (unbound) fraction is physiologically active — this is why a low total calcium in a hypoalbuminemic patient can be entirely artifactual ("pseudohypocalcemia").
  • Chvostek's sign (facial twitch with tapping over CN VII) and Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation) are the classic bedside signs of true (ionized) hypocalcemia.
  • When precision matters (critical illness, abnormal pH, large albumin swings, paraproteinemia), a directly measured ionized calcium is more reliable than any correction formula — the correction equations were derived decades ago and perform progressively worse as albumin falls further from normal.
  • Correct magnesium and check phosphate/PTH/vitamin D as part of a hypocalcemia workup — hypomagnesemia impairs PTH secretion and action, producing a treatment-resistant hypocalcemia until Mg²⁺ is repleted.

Correction Methods & Routes

Oral — calcium carbonate

~40% elemental calcium by weight (e.g., 1250 mg CaCO₃ ≈ 500 mg elemental Ca); best absorbed with food/stomach acid present — poorly absorbed in patients on PPIs or with achlorhydria.

Oral — calcium citrate

~21% elemental calcium; absorption is acid-independent, making it the preferred oral form for patients on acid suppression or with a history of gastric bypass/achlorhydria.

IV — calcium gluconate

Preferred for peripheral IV administration — lower risk of tissue necrosis if extravasation occurs; standard acute symptomatic dose ~1–2 g IV over 10–20 min, repeat/infusion as needed.

IV — calcium chloride

Contains ~3x the elemental calcium of an equal volume of gluconate (better for cardiac arrest/severe symptomatic hypocalcemia or hyperkalemia-related arrhythmia), but is highly vesicant/necrotic on extravasation — central line strongly preferred.

Expected Correction

Payne formula (most widely used, though its accuracy is debated): Corrected Ca (mg/dL) = Measured total Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − Albumin [g/dL]). A rough bedside rule: every 1 g/dL the albumin is below 4.0 g/dL, add 0.8 mg/dL to the measured total calcium.

Calculator

Albumin-Corrected Calcium (Payne formula)

Formula: Corrected Ca = Measured Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − Albumin). Order a direct ionized calcium if this result will change management.

Clinical / Safety Pearl

Studies re-examining the Payne formula found it correlates only modestly with directly measured ionized calcium (correlation coefficients in the 0.45–0.8 range) and performs worst in the sickest patients — critical illness, severe hypoalbuminemia, and abnormal pH are exactly the situations where you most want an accurate number, and exactly where the formula is least trustworthy. If a treatment decision hinges on the result, order a directly measured ionized calcium instead.

Magnesium

Reference Range

1.7 – 2.2 mg/dL (1.4 – 1.8 mEq/L)

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hypomagnesemia
Mild1.4 – 1.7 mg/dLOral repletion, outpatient follow-up reasonable if asymptomatic.
Moderate1.0 – 1.3 mg/dLIV repletion; consider admission if symptomatic or concurrent K⁺/Ca²⁺ derangement.
Severe<1.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (seizure, torsades, tetany)Admit, IV MgSO₄ repletion, continuous cardiac monitoring (torsades risk).
Hypermagnesemia
Mild2.2 – 3.9 mg/dLMonitor; hold exogenous Mg sources (antacids, laxatives).
Moderate4.0 – 5.9 mg/dLLoss of deep tendon reflexes possible; hold Mg, IV fluids/diuresis if renal function allows.
Severe≥6.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (respiratory depression, heart block, arrest)Immediate IV calcium to antagonize cardiac/neuromuscular effects; dialysis if renal failure.

Clinical Considerations

  • Mg²⁺ is required for normal PTH secretion/action and for the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase — hypomagnesemia commonly drives refractory hypokalemia AND refractory hypocalcemia simultaneously; treat the Mg²⁺ first or you'll chase the other two indefinitely.
  • Common causes: diuretics (loop > thiazide), alcohol use disorder, PPI use (rare but described), diarrhea/malabsorption, and refeeding syndrome.
  • ECG changes overlap with hypokalemia (prolonged QT/PR, U waves) — hypomagnesemia is a major precipitant of torsades de pointes and should be checked/empirically treated in any patient with polymorphic VT.
  • Hypermagnesemia is almost always iatrogenic — eclampsia treatment or renal failure with ongoing Mg-containing antacid/laxative use; loss of deep tendon reflexes is the classic early warning sign before respiratory depression and cardiac arrest.

Correction Methods & Routes

Oral — magnesium oxide/gluconate

Reasonable for mild, asymptomatic deficiency; poorly absorbed (only ~4% for oxide) and reliably causes osmotic diarrhea at replacement doses, which itself can worsen losses — a real limitation of the oral route for this electrolyte specifically.

IV — magnesium sulfate (non-urgent)

Typical inpatient replacement: 1–2 g IV MgSO₄ infused over 1–2 hours for asymptomatic/mild-moderate deficiency; infuse slowly to avoid flushing, hypotension.

IV — magnesium sulfate (urgent: torsades, eclampsia)

2 g IV MgSO₄ push over 1–2 minutes for torsades de pointes/pulseless arrest; 4–6 g IV load over 15–20 min followed by an infusion for eclampsia seizure prophylaxis/treatment.

Renal impairment caution

Magnesium is renally cleared — reduce dose and infusion rate substantially and monitor levels/reflexes more frequently in CKD/dialysis patients, who are at high risk of iatrogenic hypermagnesemia.

Expected Correction

No reliable linear "X grams raises level by Y" rule exists for magnesium the way it does for potassium — total body Mg²⁺ stores are overwhelmingly intracellular/skeletal (similar to potassium and calcium), so a single IV dose produces only a modest, often transient rise in the serum level even though it may still correct the clinical problem (arrhythmia, refractory hypokalemia).

Clinical / Safety Pearl

Because serum Mg²⁺ reflects such a small fraction of total body stores, a single normal level after one dose does NOT rule out ongoing deficiency — patients with continued losses (diuretics, alcohol, diarrhea) often need repeated dosing over days, guided by trend and clinical response rather than chasing one number.

Sodium

Reference Range

135 – 145 mEq/L

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hyponatremia
Mild130 – 134 mEq/LOften asymptomatic; outpatient workup possible if clinically stable.
Moderate121 – 129 mEq/LNausea, confusion possible; admission for workup and monitored correction.
Severe≤120 mEq/L — OR symptomatic (seizure, coma, severe AMS)Immediate treatment with hypertonic (3%) saline bolus; ICU admission; cap correction ≤8 mEq/L/24h even acutely.
Hypernatremia
Mild146 – 149 mEq/LUsually free water deficit; oral free water or mild IV correction, outpatient if stable.
Moderate150 – 159 mEq/LAdmission likely; calculated IV free water replacement over 48h.
Severe≥160 mEq/L — OR symptomatic (AMS, seizure)ICU admission; correction capped ≤0.5 mEq/L/hr decline to avoid cerebral edema.

Clinical Considerations

  • The overarching safety principle for CHRONIC dyshyponatremia of either direction: correct SLOWLY. Overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia risks osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS); overly rapid correction of chronic hypernatremia risks cerebral edema.
  • Standard safe correction limit: ≤8 mEq/L per 24 hours for chronic hyponatremia (some guidelines use an even more conservative ≤6 mEq/L/24h in high-risk patients — alcoholics, malnourished, hypokalemic, liver disease).
  • Acute, symptomatic, severe hyponatremia (seizure, coma, herniation risk) is the exception — an initial rapid bolus is appropriate and life-saving, but total correction is still capped over the full 24-hour window, and 3% hypertonic saline is dosed as a bolus (100–150 mL) rather than a continuous rapid infusion for this reason.
  • The Adrogué-Madias formula estimates the effect of 1 L of a given infusate on serum Na⁺ but is only a starting estimate — ongoing free water losses/gains (osmotic diuresis, DI, GI losses) can make actual correction diverge substantially; recheck levels frequently (often q2–4h during active correction).

Correction Methods & Routes

Hyponatremia — oral salt tablets / fluid restriction

For mild, chronic, asymptomatic hyponatremia (e.g., SIADH), fluid restriction (<800–1000 mL/day) ± oral sodium chloride tablets is first-line; effect is slow (days).

Hyponatremia — IV isotonic (0.9%) saline

First-line for hypovolemic hyponatremia to correct the volume-driven ADH stimulus; NOT appropriate for SIADH (may worsen hyponatremia via desalination if urine osmolality exceeds serum).

Hyponatremia — IV hypertonic (3%) saline

Reserved for severe/symptomatic hyponatremia; typically dosed as 100–150 mL bolus, may repeat once or twice, targeting a rise of only 4–6 mEq/L acutely to resolve symptoms — NOT full correction.

Hypernatremia — free water replacement (PO/D5W)

Oral free water (or IV D5W if NPO) to replace the free water deficit, calculated and given over 48+ hours for chronic hypernatremia to avoid cerebral edema; rate typically capped at ≤0.5 mEq/L/hr decline.

Expected Correction

Adrogué-Madias equation (estimated change in serum Na⁺ from 1 L of a given infusate): ΔNa⁺ ≈ (Infusate Na⁺ − Serum Na⁺) ÷ (Total Body Water + 1). Total body water is roughly weight (kg) × 0.6 (men) or × 0.5 (women), lower in the elderly.

Clinical / Safety Pearl

The single highest-yield safety number in this entire section: cap correction of CHRONIC hyponatremia at 8 mEq/L per 24 hours. Overshooting is common when a patient auto-corrects faster than expected (e.g., water diuresis once the underlying cause, like adrenal insufficiency or a thiazide, is addressed) — if correction is running ahead of target, DDAVP + free water ("re-lowering") can be used to relower sodium and avoid ODS.

Phosphate

Reference Range

2.5 – 4.5 mg/dL

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hypophosphatemia
Mild2.0 – 2.5 mg/dLOral repletion, outpatient follow-up if asymptomatic.
Moderate1.0 – 1.9 mg/dLIV repletion; monitor, especially in DKA recovery or refeeding.
Severe<1.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (respiratory weakness, rhabdomyolysis)Immediate IV repletion, admission/ICU, cardiorespiratory monitoring.
Hyperphosphatemia
Mild4.5 – 5.9 mg/dLUsually renal-related; dietary/binder adjustment, outpatient management.
Moderate6.0 – 8.9 mg/dLTreat underlying cause; monitor calcium for calcium-phosphate precipitation risk.
Severe≥9.0 mg/dL — OR symptomatic (tetany from resultant hypocalcemia)Admission; consider dialysis, especially with concurrent renal failure or tumor lysis syndrome.

Clinical Considerations

  • Classic setting for severe, dangerous hypophosphatemia: refeeding syndrome (malnourished patient reintroduced to carbohydrates → insulin surge → phosphate shifts intracellularly) and recovery phase of DKA (insulin drives phosphate into cells).
  • Severe hypophosphatemia (<1.0 mg/dL) can cause respiratory muscle weakness/failure, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis, and cardiac dysfunction — check and preemptively replace phosphate before extubating an at-risk patient.
  • Hyperphosphatemia is most often seen in renal failure (impaired excretion) or tumor lysis syndrome — watch for the accompanying hypocalcemia, since calcium-phosphate complexes can precipitate in tissue when the calcium-phosphate product is high.
  • Replacement products contain potassium OR sodium as the counter-ion — check the patient's potassium level before choosing (avoid K-phosphate in a hyperkalemic or renal failure patient).

Correction Methods & Routes

Oral — phosphate tablets

Reasonable for mild-moderate, asymptomatic hypophosphatemia (1.5–2.5 mg/dL); commonly causes diarrhea at replacement doses, limiting practical daily dose.

IV — potassium phosphate or sodium phosphate

For moderate-severe or symptomatic hypophosphatemia (<1.5–2.0 mg/dL or unable to tolerate PO): typical dose 15–30 mmol IV infused over 4–6 hours; choose K- vs Na-phosphate based on concurrent potassium status.

Renal impairment caution

Slow the infusion and reduce total dose substantially in renal failure — impaired excretion raises the risk of overshoot hyperphosphatemia and secondary hypocalcemia/precipitation.

Expected Correction

No single validated linear replacement formula is in routine clinical use (unlike potassium) — dosing is empiric and guided by severity and symptoms, with a recheck 4–6 hours after an IV dose to guide further replacement.

Clinical / Safety Pearl

Always recheck calcium and magnesium alongside phosphate during correction — rapid IV phosphate administration can cause acute hypocalcemia via calcium-phosphate complex formation, especially if given too fast or in a patient with pre-existing renal impairment.

Iron & Anemia

Reference Range

Hgb: 13.5–17.5 g/dL (M) / 12.0–15.5 g/dL (F) · Ferritin: 20–250 ng/mL (wide reference-range variability by lab/sex)

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Anemia (approximate adult bands — adjust for baseline/sex)
MildHgb ~10.0 – 11.9 g/dLOutpatient oral iron reasonable if hemodynamically stable and source identified/controlled.
ModerateHgb ~8.0 – 9.9 g/dLAdmission vs. outpatient depends on symptoms/comorbidities (CAD, elderly); consider IV iron for faster correction.
SevereHgb <8.0 g/dL — OR symptomatic (chest pain, hemodynamic instability, active bleeding)Admission; consider transfusion, especially if symptomatic or actively bleeding, per restrictive-threshold guidelines.

Clinical Considerations

  • Iron deficiency anemia workup should identify the SOURCE (GI blood loss is the default assumption in an adult male or postmenopausal female until proven otherwise) — treating the anemia without finding the cause risks missing a GI malignancy.
  • Ferritin is an acute phase reactant — a "normal" ferritin can still reflect true iron deficiency in the setting of concurrent inflammation/infection; a low ferritin (<30 ng/mL) is highly specific for iron deficiency regardless of inflammatory state.
  • Oral iron absorption is actively limited by hepcidin, which rises transiently after a dose and blunts absorption of a second dose taken later the same day — this is the physiologic basis for the modern alternate-day dosing strategy.
  • IV iron largely bypasses the GI absorption/hepcidin bottleneck and is preferred when oral iron is not tolerated, ineffective, or correction is urgent (active bleeding, severe symptomatic anemia, malabsorption, CKD on erythropoiesis-stimulating agents).

Correction Methods & Routes

Oral — ferrous sulfate

325 mg tablet ≈ 65 mg elemental iron. Traditional dosing was 2–3x/day, but modern evidence favors ONE tablet daily or even every other day — alternate-day dosing improves total absorption and GI tolerability by working around the hepcidin response.

Oral — ferrous gluconate / fumarate

Alternative salts with different elemental iron content per tablet and marketed as better-tolerated, though evidence for superior tolerability over sulfate is inconsistent; useful second-line options if sulfate isn't tolerated.

IV — iron sucrose / ferric gluconate

Lower single-dose max, requiring multiple infusion visits (e.g., several 200–300 mg doses) to reach total replacement dose; favorable low-anaphylaxis-risk profile.

IV — ferric carboxymaltose / low-molecular-weight iron dextran

Newer high-single-dose formulations allow full or near-full replacement (e.g., 750–1000 mg) in one or two visits — favored when rapid, convenient correction is needed (e.g., pregnancy, pre-op anemia).

Expected Correction

Oral iron benchmark: ~2 g/dL hemoglobin rise over 3 weeks is considered a successful response (roughly 0.1 g/dL/day); a smaller or absent rise at 2–3 weeks should prompt reassessment of adherence, ongoing losses, or an incorrect diagnosis. IV iron: hemoglobin typically begins rising within 1–2 weeks, with +1–2 g/dL by 2–4 weeks and maximal effect by 4–8 weeks.

Clinical / Safety Pearl

If hemoglobin has NOT risen by the expected benchmark at 2–3 weeks on adequate oral therapy, the leading explanations are (in rough order): ongoing blood loss exceeding replacement, poor adherence/tolerability, malabsorption (celiac disease, prior bariatric surgery, H. pylori), or an incorrect diagnosis (e.g., anemia of chronic disease/inflammation instead of true iron deficiency) — don't just increase the dose.

Massive Transfusion (MTP)

Reference Range

MTP activation criteria vary by institution but commonly include: ≥10 units PRBC anticipated/given in 24h, or the ABC score / shock index, or clinician gestalt for ongoing life-threatening hemorrhage

Severity — Mild / Moderate / Severe

Hemorrhage severity / activation tier (approximates ATLS shock classes — institution-dependent)
MildClass I–II (<30% blood volume loss; HR <120, near-normal BP)Type &amp; screen/crossmatch, close monitoring, notify blood bank; MTP not yet activated.
ModerateClass III (30–40% loss; HR >120, dropping BP, shock index >1)Activate MTP — begin 1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets; give TXA if within 3h of injury.
SevereClass IV (>40% loss; HR >140, hypotensive, altered mental status) OR ongoing uncontrolled hemorrhageFull MTP rounds; empiric calcium replacement every ~4 units; emergent surgical/IR source control; consider whole blood.

Clinical Considerations

  • The modern balanced-resuscitation principle: give red cells, plasma, and platelets together and EARLY rather than crystalloid-first with blood products added late — crystalloid-heavy resuscitation dilutes clotting factors and worsens trauma-induced coagulopathy.
  • The PROPPR trial compared a 1:1:1 to a 1:1:2 (plasma:platelet:RBC) ratio and found no significant difference in 24-hour or 30-day mortality overall, though the 1:1:1 group had less death specifically from hemorrhage in the first 24 hours and achieved hemostasis faster — this is the primary evidence basis for the 1:1:1 standard.
  • Give 1 g calcium chloride (or ~3 g calcium gluconate) for roughly every 4 units of blood product transfused — stored blood products are citrated as an anticoagulant, and citrate chelates ionized calcium, producing clinically significant hypocalcemia during rapid massive transfusion if not proactively replaced.
  • Whole blood (naturally balanced 1:1:1 in a single unit) is increasingly used as the initial MTP product where available — aggregated data lean toward improved survival, especially with early use, though refrigerated storage does modestly reduce platelet function compared to fresh.

Correction Methods & Routes

Standard MTP "round" / cooler

6 units PRBC + 6 units FFP + 1 apheresis platelet unit (≈ 6 pooled units) — delivered as a package and repeated in subsequent rounds until bleeding is controlled and the protocol is deactivated.

Cryoprecipitate

Add ~10 units when fibrinogen falls below 150–200 mg/dL (or per institutional trigger) — cryoprecipitate is concentrated fibrinogen, Factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, and Factor XIII.

Calcium replacement (mandatory, not optional)

1 g CaCl₂ IV (central line preferred — vesicant) or 3 g calcium gluconate IV, empirically with every ~4 units of blood product, or guided by ionized calcium if point-of-care testing is available.

Tranexamic acid (TXA)

1 g IV over 10 minutes as early as possible (ideally within 3 hours of injury) followed by a 1 g infusion over 8 hours in trauma-related hemorrhage — benefit is time-sensitive and diminishes/reverses if given late.

Expected Correction

1 unit PRBC raises hemoglobin by roughly ~1 g/dL (hematocrit by ~3%) in a non-bleeding, average-sized adult — this estimate is unreliable during ACTIVE hemorrhage, where ongoing losses can outpace any single unit's expected rise, which is why MTP is dosed by protocol/ratio and clinical response rather than by targeting a specific hemoglobin per unit given.

Clinical / Safety Pearl

Do not chase a hemoglobin number in real time during active massive hemorrhage — a fingerstick or even a send-out hemoglobin lags the true picture by the time it results, and early in acute hemorrhage the hemoglobin can look falsely reassuring before hemodilution/equilibration occurs. Treat physiology (vital signs, response to product, ongoing visible/suspected blood loss) and protocol ratios, not a single lab value.

What to look for

    2Read any 12-lead in 60 seconds

    The same order every time. Most missed findings are the ones nobody was looking for — the order forces you to look.

    1Rate & rhythm

    A P wave before every QRS, a QRS after every P, regular? Use the lead II rhythm strip. Rate ≈ 300 ÷ large boxes between R waves (300 · 150 · 100 · 75 · 60 · 50).

    Irregularly irregular without P waves = AF; wide + regular + fast = think VT until proven otherwise.

    2Axis

    Look at I and aVF. Both up = normal. I up / aVF down → check II: down = left axis. I down / aVF up = right axis. Both down = extreme (“northwest”).

    Axis deviation + wrong P-wave polarity in I = limb-lead reversal. Try the Axis Lab below.

    3Intervals

    PR 120–200 ms · QRS <120 ms (≥120 wide) · QT/QTc ≈ <450 (♂) / <460 (♀) ms. Measure one lead where the waves are clear (II or V5).

    QRS wide? Decide RBBB (rSR′ V1), LBBB (broad R, no septal q) or non-specific. PR short + delta = WPW.

    4P waves

    Best in II and V1. Tall peaked (>2.5 mm) = right atrial; wide notched (>120 ms) / deep terminal-negative in V1 = left atrial. Inverted in II = non-sinus.

    P waves in aVR must be negative.

    5R-wave progression & voltage

    R grows V1 → V5, equal R/S near V3–V4. Early transition (V1–V2) → RVH, posterior MI, WPW. Late (V5) → LVH, old anterior MI, COPD. Check voltage: S(V1)+R(V5) >35 mm; limb leads <5 mm = low voltage.

    Misplaced V1–V2 electrodes mimic poor progression.

    6Q waves

    Pathologic = ≥40 ms (one small box) or ≥25% of the R, or any Q/QS in V1–V3 beyond V1. Look for them in contiguous leads (II/III/aVF, I/aVL, V4–V6).

    Narrow + deep Q + LVH = HCM; Q with delta = WPW; lone Q in III = often normal.

    7ST segments

    Elevation / depression at the J point in territories (inferior, lateral, septal, anterior). Territorial + convex + reciprocal = ischemia. Diffuse + concave + PR depression = pericarditis.

    Early repol: concave + notching + no reciprocal. LBBB/LVH/paced: check proportion.

    8T waves & U waves

    Upright in I, II, V2–V6; inverted in aVR. Peaked (narrow-based = hyperK; broad + asymmetric = hyperacute); inverted symmetric V2–V4 = Wellens / PE / apical HCM; flat + U = hypoK.

    Last pass: compare with an old ECG. Change is the diagnosis.
    Verify · scope

    These are teaching rules of thumb. Thresholds (Q-wave criteria, QT cut-offs, voltage criteria) differ by guideline, sex, age and race. Schematic tracings — not patient data.

    4Why every lead looks the way it does — the QRS in three steps

    Think of each lead as a camera pointed at the heart from a different spot on the body. The QRS is the electrical wave of ventricular depolarization moving through the heart in three steps. Each lead draws what that wave does relative to its own position.

    Toward the lead → upA wave of depolarization moving toward the lead’s positive electrode draws a positive (upward) deflection.
    Away from the lead → downThe same wave moving away from the lead draws a negative (downward) deflection. That is why aVR, which looks from the right shoulder, is negative: the heart’s main force goes down and left, away from it.
    Sideways → little or bothA wave moving at right angles to the lead draws almost nothing or an equal up-and-down (equiphasic) complex — that is V3–V4, the transition zone.

    Limb vs chest. I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF are limb leads — they look at the heart in the frontal plane (as if seen from the front). V1–V6 are the chest (precordial) leads — they look in the horizontal plane (as if seen from the feet). aVR is a limb lead, not a chest lead.

    Frontal plane — limb leads, body seen from the front (RA/LA/LL = electrodes)
    Horizontal plane — a slice through the chest, seen from the feet (R = patient’s right)
    Same rule for P and T waves

    P wave: the atria depolarize from the SA node (upper right) down and to the left, so P is upright in I, II, aVF and inverted in aVR — the same logic as the QRS. T wave: repolarization runs outside-in (epicardium → endocardium), the opposite direction to depolarization — but because the electrical charge is also reversed, the T wave still points the same way as the QRS in most leads: upright where the R is tall (I, II, V3–V6), inverted in aVR.

    Verify · limits of this model

    A simplified teaching model: one force (vector) per step, limb leads in the frontal plane and chest leads in the horizontal plane, equal distance from the heart. Real hearts are 3-D, steps overlap in time, and chest-lead size also depends on distance from the heart. Use it to reason about why a lead is positive or negative, not to predict exact voltages.

    5Why ST segments move — the injury current

    Same camera idea as the QRS, but now the “wave” is a steady injury current between damaged and healthy muscle. Pick a scenario: the arrow is the ST vector. A lead that looks toward it draws ST elevation; a lead looking away draws ST depression — which is exactly what a reciprocal change is.

    Transmural injury → toward the wallWhole thickness injured (blocked artery): the ST vector points at the injured outer surface. Overlying leads: ST elevation. Opposite leads: reciprocal depression.
    Subendocardial injury → into the cavityOnly the inner layer injured: the vector points inward, so leads over the outside see ST depression and aVR (looking down the cavity) sees elevation.
    Diffuse epicardial → along the long axisPericarditis injures the whole surface, so the vector follows the heart’s axis: elevation wherever the R is tall, depression in aVR (and V1).

    Frontal plane — ST vector, body seen from the front
    Horizontal plane — ST vector, chest slice seen from the feet
    ↑ STE toward the vector · ↓ STD away (reciprocal) · ≈ iso sideways

    T waves follow the same logic

    Normal T waves point the same way as the QRS (repolarization runs outside-in, which reverses both direction and charge). Ischemia changes repolarization first: hyperacute T waves (tall, broad, symmetric) appear minutes into an occlusion, then ST elevation, then T inversion as the wall evolves or reperfuses — Wellens (deep, symmetric anterior T inversion after pain) means a critical proximal LAD stenosis. Because the vector reverses, T inversion in a lead that should have an upright T is the same reasoning applied to repolarization.

    Anterior-lead T-wave evolution (V3) — hyperacute → STE → Wellens, and the de Winter variant

    Same V3 lead at each stage of a proximal LAD occlusion. Pain-free with these T waves = think Wellens; pain with up-sloping ST depression and tall T = think de Winter.

    Verify · Wellens / de Winter numbers

    Wellens: type B (~75%) deep symmetric T inversion, type A (~25%) biphasic T; ST near baseline, no pathologic Q waves, normal R-wave progression, usually V2–V3 (Verify against the original descriptions). de Winter: seen in a small minority of acute LAD occlusions (~2%) — treat as a STEMI equivalent per local protocol.

    Verify · limits of this model

    One vector per scenario, every lead drawn at equal distance, so V4–V6 over-estimate the ST depression of a posterior MI (in life V1–V3 dominate because they sit closest to the injured wall). Real patterns overlap territories and vary with the artery’s dominance and collaterals. Use it to understand why the mirror image appears, not to size ST shifts; measure at the J point against the TP/PR baseline and check current criteria (e.g. 4th Universal Definition) in your protocol.

    Important simulation limitations

    These are event-driven teaching examples, not patient-derived electrophysiology or diagnostic ECGs. Activation paths are flattened from 3D anatomy. The onset, PR, QRS, RR, and blocked-versus-conducted relationships are defined per beat; P-wave amplitudes, T-wave morphology, intracardiac propagation speeds, and wall motion are approximate. A few seconds of a representative pattern cannot independently diagnose a rhythm. For example, Mobitz type needs adequate sequences of conducted beats; a 2:1 block cannot reliably be classified from ratio alone.

    Clinical cross-checks: AV block definitions · PACs · MAT · Atrial tachycardia · AIVR. Teaching animations do not replace a diagnostic ECG or electrophysiology evaluation.

What Changed · July 2025

Men can have uncomplicated UTIs now — and courses got shorter

The first IDSA complicated UTI (cUTI) guideline ever published replaces fifteen years of guidance that covered only uncomplicated cystitis and pyelonephritis in women. Male sex, diabetes, and immunocompromise no longer automatically make a UTI "complicated" — what matters is whether the infection has progressed beyond the bladder.

02 · Deep Dive

Classification rebuilt around anatomy, not demographics

Uncomplicated vs. complicated UTI Redefined

Uncomplicated UTI: presumed confined to the bladder — no fever, chills, rigors, unstable vital signs, or other signs of systemic illness not explained by a non-UTI cause. Can now apply to men or women. Complicated UTI: infection that has progressed beyond the bladder. Diabetes, immunocompromise, and male sex no longer automatically define a UTI as complicated — they may still influence treatment choice, but they don't drive the classification anymore.

⚡ What still makes it complicated regardless

Urinary catheters (transurethral, suprapubic, or intermittent), stents, and percutaneous nephrostomy tubes generally remove a patient from the "uncomplicated" category regardless of symptoms. The guideline explicitly excludes bacterial prostatitis, epididymitis, and orchitis — those need separate diagnostic pathways.

Context for the change: gram-negative urinary organisms collected from US outpatients now show resistance rates above the thresholds the 2010 guideline assumed for empiric therapy, across all US regions — part of why classification alone (rather than resistance-blind demographic rules) now drives antibiotic selection.

03 · Deep Dive

A 4-step framework replaces reflexive prescribing

Empiric antibiotic selection for cUTI New framework

StepAssessment
1Severity of illness — sepsis vs. no sepsis
2Patient-specific risk factors for resistant uropathogens
3Allergies and clinically meaningful drug interactions
4Local antibiogram, specifically when the patient is septic

For septic cUTI, preferred empiric options are third- or fourth-generation cephalosporins, carbapenems, piperacillin-tazobactam, or fluoroquinolones — not newer agents (novel beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors, cefiderocol, plazomicin) or older aminoglycosides, which are reserved rather than first-line (Grade C, Very Low certainty evidence).

04 · Deep Dive

Shorter courses, where the evidence supports them

Treatment duration Shortened

For complicated UTI with associated Gram-negative bacteremia in patients improving clinically on effective therapy, the guideline suggests 7 days rather than the traditional 14-day course. More broadly, 5–7 day courses may be considered for some cUTI patients where previously 10–14 days was the default — a continuation of the broader antimicrobial-stewardship trend toward shorter effective courses once source control and clinical improvement are established.

✓ Verify before use

Duration recommendations are class- and scenario-specific — bacteremic cUTI, non-bacteremic cUTI, and pyelonephritis don't all default to the same number. Check the specific clinical scenario against the primary guideline before shortening a course.

05 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Stop auto-labeling every male UTI as "complicated" — an afebrile man with infection confined to the bladder can be treated as uncomplicated.
  • Diabetes and immunocompromise inform your antibiotic choice, not your classification.
  • Any catheter, stent, or nephrostomy tube means complicated, regardless of symptoms.
  • For septic cUTI, run the 4-step framework rather than reaching for the newest broad-spectrum agent by habit — cefiderocol and novel beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitors are reserved, not first-line.
  • Bacteremic cUTI that's responding well doesn't need a reflexive 14 days — 7 days may be appropriate.

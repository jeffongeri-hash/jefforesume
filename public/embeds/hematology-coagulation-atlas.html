Coagulation, Clotting Disorders & Anticoagulation Atlas

The intrinsic/extrinsic/common pathway mapped to every major anticoagulant class, antiplatelet vs. anticoagulant mechanism, hereditary/acquired bleeding disorders and thrombophilias, venous vs. arterial thrombosis, VTE prophylaxis scoring, and current dosing/reversal reference.

Coagulation Cascade & Where Drugs Act

Intrinsic (contact/PTT) and extrinsic (tissue factor/PT) pathways converge at Factor X. Every major anticoagulant class has a specific point of attack on this diagram.

PT/INR vs. PTT — what each actually tests

Mixing study logic: if a prolonged PT or PTT corrects when mixed 1:1 with normal plasma, the prolongation is due to a factor deficiency (hemophilia, vitamin K deficiency, liver disease). If it does not correct, an inhibitor is present (factor-specific alloantibody, or lupus anticoagulant — the latter prolongs PTT in vitro but is prothrombotic in vivo).

Antiplatelets vs. Anticoagulants

Different targets, different clot types, different clinical uses — and different monitoring requirements.

Antiplatelets

Block platelet activation/aggregation (primary hemostasis) rather than the fibrin-forming coagulation cascade.

AspirinIrreversible COX-1 inhibition → ↓thromboxane A2 (lasts the 7–10 day platelet lifespan since platelets can't resynthesize COX-1)
Clopidogrel / Prasugrel / TicagrelorP2Y12 receptor antagonists → block ADP-mediated activation. Clopidogrel/prasugrel irreversible (prodrugs, need hepatic activation — clopidogrel affected by CYP2C19 poor-metabolizer status); ticagrelor reversible, direct-acting, no hepatic activation needed.
GPIIb/IIIa inhibitors (eptifibatide, tirofiban)Block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation (fibrinogen cross-linking) — IV only, used in ACS/PCI, not chronic outpatient use.

Indications Arterial thrombosis prevention: ACS, post-PCI (dual antiplatelet therapy), secondary stroke prevention, peripheral artery disease.

Monitoring No routine lab monitoring for chronic use (unlike warfarin). Platelet function assays exist but aren't standard practice outside research/specific bleeding-risk assessment.

Anticoagulants

Block the coagulation cascade (fibrin formation) rather than platelet aggregation — the dominant mechanism in venous clot formation and cardioembolic stroke prevention.

WarfarinVitamin K epoxide reductase inhibitor → ↓ synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C/S. Requires INR monitoring; numerous drug/food interactions.
DOACs — apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxabanDirect factor Xa inhibitors.
DOAC — dabigatranDirect thrombin (factor IIa) inhibitor.
Heparin (UFH)Potentiates antithrombin III → inhibits both IIa and Xa; IV, requires PTT monitoring, reversible with protamine.
LMWH (enoxaparin, dalteparin)Potentiates antithrombin III with relative Xa selectivity; predictable pharmacokinetics, generally no monitoring needed except in renal impairment/pregnancy/extremes of weight (anti-Xa level).
FondaparinuxSynthetic pentasaccharide, pure indirect Xa inhibitor via antithrombin; no direct thrombin effect — safe in HIT.

Indications VTE treatment/prevention, atrial fibrillation stroke prevention, mechanical heart valves (warfarin only — DOACs contraindicated), LV thrombus.

Monitoring Warfarin: INR (goal usually 2–3, or 2.5–3.5 for mechanical mitral valves). DOACs: no routine monitoring; anti-Xa or dilute thrombin time assays exist for specific situations (bleeding, renal failure, extremes of weight, urgent surgery).

Dual antiplatelet + anticoagulant (“triple therapy”) is sometimes required (e.g., AFib + recent PCI) but substantially raises bleeding risk — current cardiology guidance favors minimizing triple-therapy duration and preferring a DOAC + single antiplatelet (usually clopidogrel) over warfarin-based triple therapy when feasible.

Bleeding Disorders: Hereditary & Acquired

Factor deficiencies, von Willebrand disease, and the acquired coagulopathies of critical illness and liver disease.

Hemophilia A (Factor VIII deficiency)

Inheritance / cause

X-linked recessive (~1:5,000 male births). Severity: severe <1% activity, moderate 1–5%, mild 5–40%.

Presentation

Hemarthroses (knees, elbows, ankles — hallmark), deep muscle hematomas, prolonged bleeding after surgery/dental work, intracranial hemorrhage in severe neonatal cases.

Labs

↑PTT (corrects on mixing study, confirming factor deficiency rather than an inhibitor), normal PT/INR, normal platelet count, ↓ Factor VIII activity.

Management

Recombinant factor VIII replacement (on-demand or prophylactic), desmopressin (DDAVP) for mild disease (releases stored endothelial VIII/vWF — ineffective in severe disease with no reserve), emicizumab (bispecific antibody mimicking VIII, subcutaneous, revolutionary for prophylaxis, does NOT prolong PTT so can't be monitored the same way), avoid NSAIDs/IM injections/antiplatelets.

Pearl

Inhibitor development (alloantibodies against factor VIII) occurs in up to ~30% of severe hemophilia A patients treated with factor concentrate — suspect when a patient stops responding to replacement; requires bypassing agents (activated PCC/FEIBA, recombinant factor VIIa).

Hemophilia B (Factor IX deficiency, “Christmas disease”)

Inheritance / cause

X-linked recessive, less common than Hemophilia A (~1:25,000–30,000 male births).

Presentation

Clinically indistinguishable from Hemophilia A — hemarthroses, deep tissue bleeds.

Labs

↑PTT (corrects on mixing study), normal PT, ↓ Factor IX activity.

Management

Recombinant/plasma-derived Factor IX concentrate; longer half-life products allow less frequent dosing than Factor VIII products; gene therapy (etranacogene dezaparvovec) approved and evolving — verify current access/labeling.

Pearl

Factor IX has a longer half-life (~24h) than Factor VIII (~8–12h), so Hemophilia B prophylaxis dosing intervals are typically longer.

Hemophilia C (Factor XI deficiency)

Inheritance / cause

Autosomal (not X-linked) — both sexes affected; especially enriched in Ashkenazi Jewish populations.

Presentation

Milder, unpredictable bleeding that correlates poorly with factor level — often bleeds only after trauma/surgery (especially in high-fibrinolysis tissues: mouth, nose, urinary tract), rarely spontaneous hemarthrosis.

Labs

↑PTT, normal PT, ↓ Factor XI activity.

Management

Fresh frozen plasma or factor XI concentrate (where available) for procedures; antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid) often sufficient for mucosal bleeding given the fibrinolysis-sensitive bleeding pattern.

Pearl

Unlike Hemophilia A/B, bleeding severity does NOT reliably correlate with factor XI level — clinical bleeding history matters more than the number when deciding on prophylaxis for a procedure.

Von Willebrand Disease (VWD)

Inheritance / cause

Most common inherited bleeding disorder (~1% of population, though clinically significant disease is less common); autosomal, most types dominant.

Presentation

Mucocutaneous bleeding pattern — epistaxis, easy bruising, menorrhagia (often the presenting complaint in women), prolonged bleeding after dental extraction; hemarthrosis is uncommon except in severe Type 3.

Labs

↑ or normal PTT (depends on secondary VIII deficiency), normal PT, ↓ vWF antigen, ↓ vWF activity (ristocetin cofactor or equivalent), ±↓ Factor VIII (vWF stabilizes VIII). Type 1 (partial quantitative deficiency, ~75% of cases, mildest), Type 2 (qualitative defects, subtypes 2A/2B/2M/2N), Type 3 (near-total deficiency, most severe, autosomal recessive).

Management

Desmopressin (DDAVP) for Type 1 and many Type 2 (contraindicated in Type 2B — worsens thrombocytopenia by releasing dysfunctional vWF that binds platelets), vWF-containing factor concentrates for Type 3/severe disease or DDAVP-unresponsive cases, antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid) for mucosal bleeding/menorrhagia, avoid antiplatelets/NSAIDs.

Pearl

Type 2N vWD mimics mild Hemophilia A (low Factor VIII, normal vWF antigen/activity) because the vWF defect specifically impairs Factor VIII binding — genetic testing distinguishes them since management differs (2N responds to vWF-replete products, not isolated factor VIII).

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Inheritance / cause

Acquired, not hereditary — triggered by sepsis, trauma, malignancy (especially APL/acute promyelocytic leukemia), obstetric catastrophes (abruption, amniotic fluid embolism), severe pancreatitis.

Presentation

Paradoxical simultaneous bleeding (mucosal, IV sites, surgical sites) AND microvascular thrombosis (organ dysfunction) — consumptive coagulopathy.

Labs

↑PT, ↑PTT, ↓fibrinogen, ↑↑D-dimer, ↓platelets, schistocytes on smear (microangiopathic hemolysis). ISTH DIC score incorporates platelet count, D-dimer/fibrin markers, prolonged PT, and fibrinogen level — score ≥5 supports overt DIC.

Management

Treat the underlying trigger (this is the actual treatment — DIC is a consequence, not a primary disease). Supportive: FFP for factor replacement if bleeding, cryoprecipitate if fibrinogen <100–150 mg/dL and bleeding, platelet transfusion if <10–20k (or higher threshold if active bleeding/procedure), avoid anticoagulation unless thrombosis clearly dominates the picture (rare, e.g. purpura fulminans).

Pearl

APL (acute promyelocytic leukemia) causes a particularly severe DIC at diagnosis and with induction therapy — early empiric ATRA (all-trans retinoic acid) is started on clinical suspicion before genetic confirmation because early DIC-related mortality is the main cause of induction death.

Liver Disease Coagulopathy

Inheritance / cause

Acquired — the liver synthesizes nearly all coagulation factors (except vWF, made by endothelium) as well as their natural inhibitors (protein C, protein S, antithrombin).

Presentation

Can present with bleeding tendency, but critically the hemostatic system is often “re-balanced” rather than simply hypocoagulable — both pro- and anti-coagulant factor synthesis fall together, and many cirrhotic patients are simultaneously at increased VTE/portal vein thrombosis risk.

Labs

↑PT/INR (often used as part of MELD score, not a true bleeding-risk marker in this context), variable PTT, low fibrinogen in advanced disease, thrombocytopenia from hypersplenism (portal hypertension) compounds the picture.

Management

INR should NOT be used alone to guide prophylactic FFP/factor correction before low-risk procedures in cirrhosis — it does not reliably predict bleeding risk in this population because both pro- and anti-coagulant factors are reduced in parallel (rebalanced hemostasis). Correct only for active bleeding or high-risk procedures, guided by clinical judgment ± viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) where available.

Pearl

This is a key teaching point distinguishing liver disease from vitamin K deficiency/warfarin: an elevated INR from liver disease does not carry the same bleeding-risk implication as the same INR number from warfarin, because protein C/S (also liver-made) are proportionally reduced too.

Vitamin K Deficiency

Inheritance / cause

Acquired — malabsorption (celiac, cystic fibrosis, biliary obstruction), poor oral intake, prolonged antibiotic use (disrupts gut flora vitamin K production), warfarin (pharmacologic vitamin K antagonism), neonates (physiologic deficiency at birth — universal IM vitamin K prophylaxis).

Presentation

Bleeding proportional to severity — in neonates, classically presents as hemorrhagic disease of the newborn (VKDB) if prophylaxis is refused/omitted.

Labs

↑PT/INR first (factor VII shortest half-life ~6h), ↑PTT with more severe/prolonged deficiency, normal platelets, normal fibrinogen (distinguishes from DIC).

Management

Vitamin K repletion (oral/IV depending on urgency and malabsorption status); FFP or 4F-PCC for active severe bleeding needing immediate correction (vitamin K alone takes 6–24h to normalize INR).

Pearl

IV vitamin K works faster than oral but carries a rare anaphylactoid reaction risk with rapid infusion — give slowly, diluted, per institutional protocol.

Thrombophilias: Hereditary & Acquired

The inherited and autoimmune conditions that tip Virchow's triad toward clot — including testing-timing pitfalls that trip up even experienced clinicians.

Factor V Leiden

Type

Hereditary (most common inherited thrombophilia, ~3–8% of the White population; less common in other ancestries)

Mechanism

Point mutation (R506Q) in Factor V renders it resistant to cleavage/inactivation by activated protein C (“APC resistance”) → prolonged factor Va activity → excess thrombin generation.

Risk magnitude

Heterozygous: ~5–7x increased VTE risk. Homozygous: ~25–80x increased risk. Risk multiplies further with additional factors (OCPs, pregnancy, surgery, immobility).

Testing pearls

APC resistance functional assay (screening) confirmed by genetic testing (PCR) — genetic test is NOT affected by acute thrombosis or anticoagulation, unlike protein C/S functional assays.

Management

Standard anticoagulation duration/intensity for an incident VTE is generally similar to non-thrombophilic patients per current ASH guidance for heterozygotes; extended/indefinite anticoagulation considered more strongly for homozygotes, recurrent VTE, or combined thrombophilias — confirm against current ASH/ACCP guidance for the specific scenario.

Prothrombin G20210A Mutation

Type

Hereditary (~1–2% of the White population)

Mechanism

Mutation in the prothrombin gene 3' untranslated region → increased prothrombin mRNA stability → elevated plasma prothrombin levels → increased thrombin generation.

Risk magnitude

Heterozygous carriers: ~2–3x increased VTE risk; second most common inherited thrombophilia after Factor V Leiden.

Testing pearls

Direct genetic testing (PCR) — not affected by acute thrombosis or anticoagulation.

Management

Similar principle to Factor V Leiden — thrombophilia positivity alone does not usually change anticoagulation duration after a single provoked VTE, but factors into shared decision-making about extended prophylaxis, especially if combined with other risk factors.

Protein C Deficiency

Type

Hereditary (autosomal dominant) or acquired (liver disease, warfarin, DIC, sepsis, L-asparaginase)

Mechanism

Protein C, once activated by thrombomodulin-bound thrombin, degrades factors Va and VIIIa. Deficiency removes this brake on coagulation.

Risk magnitude

Heterozygous hereditary deficiency: ~7–10x VTE risk. Homozygous/severe deficiency: neonatal purpura fulminans (life-threatening).

Testing pearls

Functional protein C activity assay — falsely LOW during acute thrombosis (consumption) and while on warfarin (which also suppresses protein C synthesis) — test at least 2 weeks after completing anticoagulation and away from acute thrombosis for accurate hereditary-deficiency diagnosis.

Management

Classic pearl: warfarin-induced skin necrosis risk is highest in protein C (and protein S) deficient patients started on warfarin without adequate heparin/DOAC bridging — because protein C has a short half-life and falls before the procoagulant factors (II, IX, X) do, creating a transient hypercoagulable window.

Protein S Deficiency

Type

Hereditary (autosomal dominant) or acquired (pregnancy, OCPs, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, warfarin)

Mechanism

Protein S is the cofactor for activated protein C — deficiency impairs factor Va/VIIIa degradation, similar downstream effect to protein C deficiency.

Risk magnitude

Similar magnitude to protein C deficiency; risk is difficult to quantify precisely due to variable free vs. total protein S assay interpretation.

Testing pearls

Free protein S antigen (most reliable) — also falsely low in pregnancy, OCP use, acute thrombosis, and warfarin therapy; testing timing matters as much as for protein C.

Management

Same warfarin-induced skin necrosis risk consideration as protein C deficiency; always bridge with heparin/LMWH when initiating warfarin in known or suspected deficiency.

Antithrombin III Deficiency

Type

Hereditary (autosomal dominant, rarer but highest-risk inherited thrombophilia) or acquired (nephrotic syndrome — urinary loss, liver disease — reduced synthesis, DIC — consumption, L-asparaginase therapy)

Mechanism

Antithrombin is the natural inhibitor of thrombin and factor Xa; heparin/LMWH work BY POTENTIATING antithrombin — severe deficiency causes heparin resistance.

Risk magnitude

Highest relative risk among the classic hereditary thrombophilias (~along the lines of 10–20x); higher rate of VTE at a young age and with weaker provoking factors.

Testing pearls

Functional antithrombin activity assay — falsely LOW during acute thrombosis, with heparin therapy (consumption), and with DIC/liver disease — avoid testing during acute anticoagulation when possible.

Management

Antithrombin concentrate available for severe deficiency during high-risk periods (surgery, pregnancy, acute VTE with heparin resistance) to restore heparin responsiveness; lifelong anticoagulation more strongly considered given the higher risk magnitude.

Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS)

Type

Acquired autoimmune — primary or secondary to SLE/other autoimmune disease.

Mechanism

Antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2-glycoprotein I) promote both venous AND arterial thrombosis plus placental thrombosis/insufficiency (recurrent miscarriage).

Risk magnitude

Recurrent thrombosis risk is substantial, especially with triple antibody positivity (all three antibody types positive) — associated with the highest-risk phenotype including catastrophic APS (multi-organ thrombosis, high mortality).

Testing pearls

2023 ACR/EULAR classification criteria require ≥1 clinical criterion (macrovascular VTE, arterial thrombosis, microvascular, obstetric, cardiac valve, or hematologic domain) plus ≥1 laboratory criterion (lupus anticoagulant, or moderate/high-titer IgG/IgM anticardiolipin or anti-β2GPI) within 3 years of each other, with weighted point totals ≥ 3 in both the clinical and laboratory domains. Requires repeat testing ≥12 weeks apart to confirm persistence (transient antibodies from infection are common and not diagnostic).

Management

Warfarin (INR target typically 2–3, though some high-risk/arterial or recurrent cases target higher) remains standard — DOACs are generally AVOIDED in APS, particularly triple-positive disease, based on trial data (e.g. TRAPS trial) showing higher thrombotic event rates with rivaroxaban vs. warfarin. This is an important safety point: APS is one of the few situations where warfarin is preferred over a DOAC for long-term anticoagulation.

Venous Stasis vs. Arterial Occlusion

Two fundamentally different clots, risk-factor profiles, treatment approaches, and mortality patterns.

Venous Thrombosis

PathophysiologyDominated by stasis and coagulation cascade activation (Virchow's triad: stasis, hypercoagulability, endothelial injury) — clot is fibrin- and RBC-rich (“red clot”), forms in low-flow/low-shear conditions typically starting in venous valve pockets.

Risk factorsImmobility/travel, surgery (especially orthopedic), malignancy, pregnancy/postpartum, OCP/HRT use, prior VTE, thrombophilia, obesity, central venous catheters, heart failure.

PresentationDVT: unilateral leg swelling, warmth, tenderness, Homans sign (unreliable). PE: dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, hypoxia; massive PE can cause obstructive shock/sudden death.

First-line treatmentAnticoagulation is primary therapy (DOAC preferred first-line per current ACCP/ASH guidance for most patients without cancer or APS) — antiplatelets are NOT effective primary treatment for VTE.

MortalityPE is the more immediately lethal presentation — untreated symptomatic PE historically carried up to ~30% mortality; with treatment, case-fatality drops to low single digits, though massive/high-risk PE with hemodynamic instability still carries substantial short-term mortality.

Arterial Thrombosis

PathophysiologyDominated by endothelial injury and atherosclerotic plaque rupture/erosion exposing subendothelial collagen and tissue factor → rapid platelet-driven thrombus formation (“white clot,” platelet- and fibrin-rich) in high-shear/high-flow conditions.

Risk factorsHypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, smoking, family history of premature CAD, age, atrial fibrillation (source of cardioembolic arterial events specifically), APS.

PresentationACS: chest pain, diaphoresis, dyspnea. Ischemic stroke: focal neuro deficit, sudden onset. Acute limb ischemia: the 6 P's — pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia.

First-line treatmentAntiplatelet therapy is primary/foundational (aspirin ± P2Y12 inhibitor) for atherosclerotic arterial disease; anticoagulation is added specifically for cardioembolic sources (AFib, LV thrombus, mechanical valve) rather than for atherosclerotic plaque itself. Acute management often includes revascularization (PCI, thrombolysis, thrombectomy) rather than anticoagulation alone.

MortalityArterial events carry high acute mortality/morbidity concentrated at the moment of the event (sudden cardiac death, large-vessel stroke) — overall, cardiovascular disease (arterial) remains the leading cause of death worldwide, outweighing VTE mortality in absolute population terms, even though an individual untreated massive PE has a strikingly high case-fatality rate.

Antiplatelets prevent arterial (platelet-driven) clots; anticoagulants prevent venous (coagulation-cascade-driven) clots and cardioembolic events — using the wrong class for the wrong clot type (e.g., aspirin alone for DVT prophylaxis in a high-risk surgical patient) is a recognized source of preventable VTE.

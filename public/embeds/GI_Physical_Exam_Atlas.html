Inspection, Auscultation & Percussion

The three exam components that must come before palpation — palpating first (especially deep palpation) can artificially stimulate bowel sounds and invalidate your auscultation findings. Sequence matters: inspect, then auscultate, then percuss, then palpate.

Named landmarks and special-sign locations referenced throughout this atlas — worth orienting to before working through the rest of the domains below.
Inspection Auscultation Percussion Ascites Assessment

Inspection

What to assess, systematically

  • Contour: flat, scaphoid (sunken — consider malnutrition, bowel obstruction with proximal decompression), or distended (consider the classic “5 F's”: fat, fluid, flatus, feces, fetus — plus a mass/fibroid as a less-mnemonic sixth possibility)
  • Symmetry: asymmetric distension raises concern for a mass, organomegaly, or a localized process rather than diffuse ascites/obesity
  • Skin findings: striae (rapid distension — ascites, pregnancy, weight change), caput medusae (dilated periumbilical veins radiating from the umbilicus — portal hypertension), surgical scars (note location and ask what each was for — relevant to adhesion-related SBO risk), visible peristalsis (can indicate obstruction, especially in a thin patient), and ecchymosis (see Cullen's and Grey Turner's signs in the Special Signs domain)
  • Visible pulsations: a visible, prominent epigastric pulsation can be a normal finding in thin patients but should prompt consideration of an abdominal aortic aneurysm, especially if it appears widened or expansile rather than simply transmitted
  • Hernia bulges: inspect standing and supine, and with Valsalva/cough — many hernias are only visible with increased intra-abdominal pressure (see the Hernia Examination domain for full technique)

Auscultation

Technique and what you're listening for

Auscultate before percussion and palpation, since manipulating the abdomen can transiently alter bowel sound activity. Listen in at least one location (classically the RLQ, since bowel sounds are transmitted throughout the abdomen and a single representative location is generally sufficient) for a full 30–60 seconds before characterizing sounds as normal, hyperactive, hypoactive, or absent — and if truly assessing for “absent” bowel sounds (e.g., suspected ileus), the traditional teaching is to listen for a full 2–5 minutes, since bowel sounds can be intermittent even in a normal gut.

⚠ A modern caveat worth knowing

Contemporary literature has increasingly questioned the diagnostic reliability of bowel sound auscultation — interrater agreement is poor, and the presence or absence of bowel sounds correlates weakly with the presence of obstruction or ileus on imaging. Use auscultation as one data point alongside the rest of the exam and clinical picture, not as a standalone rule-in/rule-out test.

Bowel sounds

  • Normal: intermittent gurgles/clicks, roughly 5–34 per minute
  • Hyperactive: loud, frequent, high-pitched (“borborygmi”) — early mechanical obstruction, gastroenteritis, GI bleeding (blood is a cathartic)
  • Hypoactive/absent: post-operative ileus, peritonitis, late/complete obstruction, advanced mesenteric ischemia
  • High-pitched, tinkling, or rushing sounds with visible peristalsis: classically associated with mechanical small bowel obstruction

Bruits

  • Aortic (midline, epigastric): consider aneurysm or significant atherosclerotic disease
  • Renal artery (each upper quadrant, paramedian): associated with renovascular hypertension
  • Hepatic (over the liver): can be heard with hepatocellular carcinoma or a large hepatic arteriovenous malformation
  • Mesenteric/iliac: consider in unexplained abdominal pain with vascular risk factors, though a bruit's absence does not exclude mesenteric ischemia

Percussion

General technique

Percuss all four quadrants first for a general sense of tympany (gas-filled, the expected predominant sound) versus dullness (fluid, mass, organ, or stool). Then proceed to specific organ percussion.

Liver span

Percuss down from clear lung resonance in the right midclavicular line to the point where resonance changes to dullness (upper border), then percuss up from the abdomen from tympany to dullness (lower border). Normal span is roughly 6–12cm in the midclavicular line, with substantial individual variation — trend serial exams in the same patient over a single measurement compared against a population "normal."

Splenic percussion (Castell's sign / Traube's space)

Castell's sign: percuss the lowest intercostal space in the left anterior axillary line (Traube's space) at end-expiration (should be tympanic) and at full inspiration (a change to dullness on inspiration is a positive sign, suggesting splenomegaly — inspiratory splenic descent brings an enlarged spleen into the percussed space). This is generally considered more sensitive than physical palpation for detecting mild-to-moderate splenomegaly.

Ascites Assessment: Shifting Dullness & Fluid Wave

Shifting dullness — full technique

  1. With the patient supine, percuss from the umbilicus laterally toward each flank, marking the point where tympany transitions to dullness. In ascites, fluid layers dependently, so the flanks are dull and the periumbilical area (where bowel loops float on top of the fluid) remains tympanic.
  2. Have the patient roll onto one side (lateral decubitus), wait roughly 30–60 seconds for fluid to redistribute, then repercuss the same points.
  3. Positive shifting dullness: the border of dullness shifts toward the now-dependent side (fluid has moved with gravity) — supports ascites over other causes of distension.

Fluid wave test

Have the patient (or an assistant) place the ulnar edge of a hand firmly along the midline of the abdomen to dampen transmission through subcutaneous fat. Tap firmly on one flank while palpating the opposite flank for a transmitted impulse/wave. A palpable wave on the opposite side supports ascites, though this test has a notably high false-positive rate in patients with significant abdominal wall fat even with the dampening maneuver — shifting dullness is generally considered the more reliable bedside test of the two.

💡 Pearl — how much fluid do you need to detect it?

Shifting dullness typically requires at least 1500mL of ascitic fluid to be reliably detected on exam; smaller volumes will be missed by physical exam alone regardless of technique quality. Bedside ultrasound has far greater sensitivity for smaller volumes and should be used liberally when ascites is suspected but the exam is equivocal.

⚠ Distinguishing ascites from other causes of distension

Central tympany with dependent flank dullness that shifts with position favors ascites. Diffuse tympany throughout (including the flanks) favors gas/obstruction. A fixed, non-shifting area of dullness favors a mass, organomegaly, or a loculated (non-mobile) fluid collection rather than free ascitic fluid.

Palpation & Tenderness Grading

Light palpation before deep, and always palpate the area of reported pain last — starting there both hurts unnecessarily and can cause guarding that limits the rest of your exam. This domain covers organ-specific palpation technique and, just as importantly, a structured way to actually gauge and grade what you find.

Light & Deep Palpation Organ-Specific Technique Grading Tenderness

Light & Deep Palpation

Light palpation

Gentle, superficial pressure (1–2cm depth) across all quadrants, saving the painful quadrant for last. Purpose: identify superficial tenderness, involuntary guarding, and any superficial masses, while also acclimating an anxious patient to your touch before deeper palpation.

Deep palpation

Firmer, bimanual pressure (with one hand reinforcing the other) to assess for organomegaly, deep masses, and deep tenderness. Deep palpation over the area of maximal pain is where you'll elicit findings like true peritoneal signs — but again, do this last.

💡 Pearl — the distraction technique

Engage the patient in conversation while palpating, or ask them to point with one finger to the location of maximal pain before you begin (rather than you finding it by causing pain repeatedly). A patient who winces reflexively during a conversational exam but has no findings when distracted is giving you useful information about the voluntary-versus-involuntary distinction covered below.

Organ-Specific Palpation Technique

Liver

Place your left hand posteriorly under the patient's right lower ribs to support and slightly lift the flank, and your right hand on the abdomen below the anticipated liver edge (below the percussed lower border). Ask the patient to take a deep breath while you press inward and slightly upward, feeling for the liver edge to slide down against your fingertips. Characterize the edge: smooth and soft (normal, or early congestion), firm and sharp (chronic disease, e.g., cirrhosis), nodular (concerning for malignancy or macronodular cirrhosis), or tender (hepatitis, congestion, abscess). The hooking technique (standing at the patient's shoulder facing their feet, hooking fingers under the right costal margin and asking them to breathe deeply) is a useful alternative, particularly in obese patients.

Spleen

Same bimanual principle as the liver, but starting palpation low in the RLQ-mirrored LLQ region and working upward toward the left costal margin so an unexpectedly enlarged spleen isn't missed by starting too high. Sensitivity improves substantially with the patient in the right lateral decubitus position (rolled onto their right side with knees slightly flexed), which brings the spleen forward and inferior. A normal spleen is not palpable — any palpable spleen tip is a positive finding worth pursuing, though a small percentage of normal thin patients have a palpable tip without pathology.

Gallbladder / Murphy's point

Full technique detailed in the Special Signs domain — in brief, hook your fingers under the right costal margin at the midclavicular line and ask the patient to take a deep breath; sudden inspiratory arrest from pain (rather than pain alone) is what defines a positive Murphy's sign.

Kidneys (ballottement)

Place one hand posteriorly at the costovertebral angle and the other anteriorly below the costal margin on the same side. Press the posterior hand upward in a quick, firm motion while feeling with the anterior hand for the kidney to be “bounced” against it. The right kidney is more often palpable than the left given its slightly lower position beneath the liver.

Aorta

Palpate firmly in the epigastrium, slightly left of midline, with both hands to assess the width of the aortic pulsation between your fingers (rather than just detecting that a pulse is present). A width greater than roughly 3cm raises concern for aneurysm and should prompt further imaging. Sensitivity is significantly reduced in obese patients — a reassuring exam in this population should not be treated as excluding an aneurysm the way it might in a thin patient.

How to Gauge and Grade Abdominal Tenderness

A practical severity framework

GradeFindingWhat it suggests
MildTenderness to palpation only, no guarding, patient can be distracted from it, normal facial expression with routine palpationOften functional, mild inflammatory, or early-stage disease
ModerateTenderness with voluntary guarding (see below), patient winces but tolerates deeper palpation, no reboundMore significant inflammation, warrants further workup
SevereTenderness with involuntary guarding and/or rebound, patient resists exam, may have rigidityConcerning for peritoneal irritation — escalate workup and consider surgical consultation
Rigid abdomenBoard-like, diffusely involuntarily tense abdominal wall, often with a still, guarded body positionPeritonitis until proven otherwise — a surgical emergency

⚠ Voluntary vs. involuntary guarding — the distinction that actually matters

Voluntary guarding is a conscious, anticipatory muscle contraction in response to (or anticipation of) palpation — it can often be reduced by distraction, warming your hands, having the patient flex their knees to relax the abdominal wall, or palpating more gently and gradually. Involuntary guarding is a true reflexive muscle spasm from underlying peritoneal irritation that persists despite these techniques and despite the patient's own efforts to relax. This distinction is the difference between an anxious but reassuring exam and one that's flagging real peritonitis — don't stop at "the patient is guarding" without characterizing which kind.

Weak documentation Abdomen tender, guarding. Started on pain meds.
Bulletproof documentation Abdomen soft on inspection, moderate tenderness to deep palpation in the RLQ, voluntary guarding present that improved with distraction and knee flexion, no involuntary guarding, no rebound, no rigidity. Rovsing's sign negative, psoas/obturator signs negative. Findings do not currently suggest peritonitis; will trend serial exams given ongoing symptoms and pursue imaging as above.

Special / Named Signs

16 eponymous and named physical exam findings for the GI/abdominal exam — each with the exact technique, the physiologic reason it works, what a positive finding means, and a clinical pearl. This is the section built for genuine expert-level recall, not a superficial list.

Rovsing's sign

+
CategoryPeritoneal irritation
TechniquePalpate deeply in the LLQ and observe for pain referred to the RLQ.
Physiology / mechanismPalpation pressure and gas movement in the left colon transmits mechanical stimulation across the peritoneum to an already-inflamed right-sided structure.
Clinical meaningSupports acute appendicitis when positive, though a negative result does not exclude it.
Pearl: Pair with the rest of the appendicitis exam battery (McBurney's point tenderness, psoas, obturator) — no single sign here has high enough sensitivity to stand alone.

Rebound tenderness (Blumberg's sign)

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryPeritoneal irritation
TechniquePress slowly and deeply into the abdomen, then release quickly, and ask whether pain is worse with the pressing or the sudden release.
Physiology / mechanismRapid release causes the inflamed peritoneum to rebound and rub against itself, producing sharp pain distinct from the pain of direct pressure.
Clinical meaningA classic marker of peritoneal irritation, historically weighted heavily in appendicitis scoring systems (including the Alvarado score).
Pearl: Consider the gentler alternative below (Markle sign / percussion tenderness) for patients in whom deliberately causing rebound pain feels unnecessarily uncomfortable — they often correlate.

Markle sign (heel-drop / jump test)

+
CategoryPeritoneal irritation
TechniqueHave the patient stand on their toes and drop suddenly onto their heels, or gently strike the patient's heel with your hand while they lie supine with legs extended; observe for abdominal pain.
Physiology / mechanismThe jarring transmits an impulse through the body to the peritoneum, analogous to rebound tenderness but without the discomfort of direct abdominal palpation and release.
Clinical meaningA gentler surrogate for rebound tenderness/peritoneal irritation — useful in children or particularly guarded/anxious patients.
Pearl: Genuinely useful when a patient is too tense or fearful for a reliable direct rebound exam — it gets at the same physiologic principle from a different angle.

Psoas sign (iliopsoas test)

+
CategoryRetroperitoneal/pelvic irritation
TechniqueWith the patient lying on their left side, passively extend the right hip; alternatively, with the patient supine, have them actively flex the right hip against your resisting hand.
Physiology / mechanismThe psoas muscle lies retroperitoneally and is in direct proximity to a retrocecal appendix; stretching or contracting it against an inflamed adjacent structure reproduces pain.
Clinical meaningSuggests a retrocecal (or otherwise retroperitoneal) inflammatory process, classically retrocecal appendicitis, but also seen with psoas abscess.
Pearl: A positive psoas sign in a febrile patient with hip flexion pain and a limp should also raise psoas abscess as a distinct diagnosis, not just appendicitis — see the psoas abscess entry in the GI differential atlas.

Obturator sign

+
CategoryPelvic irritation
TechniqueWith the patient supine and the right hip and knee both flexed to 90°, internally rotate the hip.
Physiology / mechanismThe obturator internus muscle lies adjacent to the pelvic structures; internal rotation stretches it against an inflamed pelvic appendix, pelvic abscess, or other pelvic inflammatory process.
Clinical meaningSuggests a pelvic inflammatory process — classically a pelvic-position appendix, but also pelvic abscess or adnexal pathology.
Pearl: Particularly useful when the appendix's position is atypical (pelvic rather than the more common retrocecal/anterior positions), since McBurney's point tenderness can be subtle or absent in that scenario.

Murphy's sign

+
CategoryBiliary
TechniqueHook your fingers under the right costal margin at the midclavicular line and ask the patient to take a deep breath.
Physiology / mechanismOn inspiration, the diaphragm descends and pushes an inflamed gallbladder down onto your fingers, producing sudden pain and a reflexive stop in inspiration.
Clinical meaningA positive sign is inspiratory arrest from pain, not simply pain with palpation — this distinction matters and is frequently documented incorrectly.
Pearl: Reasonably specific but only moderately sensitive for acute cholecystitis — a negative Murphy's sign does not reliably exclude cholecystitis, particularly early in the disease course or in elderly/diabetic patients with blunted pain responses.

Courvoisier's sign

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryBiliary/pancreatic
TechniquePalpate the RUQ for a non-tender, palpably enlarged gallbladder in a patient with jaundice.
Physiology / mechanismA gallbladder that has gradually distended against a slowly progressive, non-inflammatory obstruction (classically a pancreatic head mass) does not develop the fibrotic contraction seen with chronic gallstone disease, so it remains soft, distended, and non-tender.
Clinical meaningClassically taught as: painless jaundice with a palpable gallbladder suggests malignant (not gallstone) obstruction — gallstones typically cause a chronically scarred, non-distensible gallbladder that can't enlarge even against obstruction.
Pearl: The classic teaching (“Courvoisier's law”) has real exceptions — don't treat it as fully ruling gallstone disease in or out, but do let a painless palpable gallbladder with jaundice raise malignancy on your differential rather than defaulting to stones.

Cullen's sign

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryRetroperitoneal/intraperitoneal hemorrhage
TechniqueInspect (don't need to palpate) the periumbilical skin for bluish-purple discoloration.
Physiology / mechanismBlood tracking from a retroperitoneal or intraperitoneal hemorrhage dissects along fascial planes (specifically the falciform ligament) to reach the periumbilical subcutaneous tissue, where hemoglobin breakdown produces the discoloration.
Clinical meaningAssociated with hemorrhagic pancreatitis, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and other causes of significant intra-abdominal/retroperitoneal hemorrhage.
Pearl: Takes 24–48 hours to develop after the hemorrhagic event — its absence early in a presentation does not exclude a hemorrhagic process; this is a delayed, not immediate, finding.

Grey Turner's sign

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryRetroperitoneal hemorrhage
TechniqueInspect the flanks (bilaterally) for bluish-purple discoloration.
Physiology / mechanismSame fascial-plane blood-tracking mechanism as Cullen's sign, but dissecting laterally toward the flank rather than toward the umbilicus — the two signs often, but not always, occur together.
Clinical meaningClassically associated with hemorrhagic/necrotizing pancreatitis and other retroperitoneal hemorrhage.
Pearl: Like Cullen's sign, this takes 24–48 hours to appear — both are late markers of severity in hemorrhagic pancreatitis, not early diagnostic findings.

Kehr's sign

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryReferred diaphragmatic irritation
TechniqueAsk about (this is a history/symptom finding as much as an exam maneuver) pain referred to the left shoulder, sometimes reproduced or worsened with Trendelenburg positioning or palpation of the LUQ.
Physiology / mechanismDiaphragmatic irritation (classically from free blood pooling under the left diaphragm after splenic injury) refers pain via the phrenic nerve (C3–C5) to the C3–C5 shoulder dermatome.
Clinical meaningClassically associated with splenic rupture, but also seen with any process irritating the diaphragm's undersurface (free intraperitoneal blood or air from other sources).
Pearl: A patient with abdominal trauma and new left shoulder pain out of proportion to any shoulder injury should prompt consideration of splenic injury even without dramatic abdominal findings yet.

Carnett's sign

+
CategoryAbdominal wall vs. intra-abdominal pain
TechniqueLocalize the point of maximal tenderness, then have the patient tense their abdominal wall (lift their head off the bed, or lift both legs while lying flat) and re-palpate the same point.
Physiology / mechanismTensing the rectus abdominis muscles brings the abdominal wall forward, protecting deeper intra-abdominal structures from your palpating fingers — so if the pain source is in the abdominal wall itself (not the peritoneal cavity), tensing will not reduce, and often increases, the pain there.
Clinical meaningPositive (pain unchanged or worse with tensing) suggests an abdominal wall source (muscle strain, nerve entrapment, rectus sheath hematoma). Negative (pain improves with tensing) suggests an intra-abdominal source.
Pearl: One of the most underused signs in this entire atlas — abdominal wall pain is a genuinely common and often-missed cause of chronic abdominal pain, and a positive Carnett's sign can spare a patient an unnecessary extensive intra-abdominal workup.

Fothergill's sign

+
CategoryRectus sheath hematoma
TechniqueWith the point of tenderness identified, have the patient tense the rectus abdominis (as in Carnett's sign above) and note whether the mass/tenderness remains palpable and fails to cross the midline.
Physiology / mechanismA rectus sheath hematoma sits within the muscle itself, so it remains palpable (or becomes more distinct) with muscle contraction and is bounded by the linea alba, so it does not cross the midline — an intra-abdominal mass, by contrast, typically becomes less distinct or is “protected” from your fingers when the muscle tenses in front of it.
Clinical meaningSupports rectus sheath hematoma, particularly relevant in anticoagulated patients, after forceful coughing/straining, or after minor trauma.
Pearl: Consider this specifically in an anticoagulated patient with acute abdominal wall pain and a palpable mass — it's a mechanical/vascular diagnosis, not an inflammatory one, and doesn't need antibiotics.

Sister Mary Joseph nodule

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryMetastatic malignancy
TechniqueInspect and palpate the umbilicus for a firm, often irregular nodule.
Physiology / mechanismRepresents metastatic spread to the umbilicus via peritoneal, lymphatic, or vascular routes from an intra-abdominal or pelvic primary malignancy (classically gastric, pancreatic, ovarian, or colonic).
Clinical meaningAn ominous sign generally indicating advanced, often terminal, intra-abdominal malignancy.
Pearl: A firm umbilical nodule in a patient without an established cancer diagnosis should prompt an urgent malignancy workup rather than being dismissed as a benign umbilical finding (hernia, cyst).

Virchow's node

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryMetastatic malignancy
TechniquePalpate the left supraclavicular fossa for a firm, non-tender lymph node.
Physiology / mechanismThe thoracic duct drains into the left venous system near this location, providing a pathway for abdominal/pelvic malignancies (classically gastric) to metastasize to a palpable, distant site.
Clinical meaningSuggests metastatic intra-abdominal malignancy, most classically gastric cancer.
Pearl: Also called “Troisier's sign” when described as part of the broader physical finding — worth palpating the left supraclavicular fossa specifically (not just generally examining the neck) in a patient with concerning GI symptoms and weight loss.

Caput medusae

+
CategoryPortal hypertension
TechniqueInspect the periumbilical skin for dilated, tortuous veins radiating outward from the umbilicus.
Physiology / mechanismPortal hypertension reopens the paraumbilical veins (a fetal remnant of the umbilical vein/ligamentum teres), creating a portosystemic collateral pathway visible at the skin surface.
Clinical meaningA visible sign of significant portal hypertension, usually in the setting of established cirrhosis.
Pearl: Distinguish flow direction if needed by using two fingers to empty a segment of vein, then releasing one end at a time to see which direction refills it faster — flow away from the umbilicus in all directions is the expected pattern with portal hypertension (distinct from IVC obstruction, where flow in the lower abdominal wall runs upward toward the umbilicus).

Kennedy terminal ulcer

CAN'T MISS / HIGH-YIELD
+
CategoryEnd-of-life skin finding
TechniqueInspect the sacrum/coccyx and buttock region for a rapidly evolving ulcer, often shaped like a butterfly, pear, or horseshoe, with a mix of colors (red, yellow, black, purple) developing over hours to days.
Physiology / mechanismBelieved to represent skin failure as part of the overall organ failure process at the end of life — similar in concept to other organs failing in a dying patient, rather than a preventable pressure-injury/neglect finding, even though it can superficially resemble one.
Clinical meaningA recognized marker of impending death (often within days to a couple of weeks), first described in hospice literature.
Pearl: Distinguishing this from a preventable pressure injury matters enormously for both clinical framing and family/caregiver communication — documenting it accurately (rather than as a standard “stage IV pressure ulcer”) avoids implying a preventable care failure when this is better understood as a terminal, physiologic skin-failure event. Not itself a G.I.-specific sign, but included here given how often it's misidentified during abdominal/skin exams in hospice and inpatient settings.

Sensitivity and specificity for these signs vary considerably by study and by underlying pathology — use them to raise or lower suspicion within a broader clinical picture, not as standalone diagnostic tests.

Hernia Examination

Anatomy first, then technique — the direct-versus-indirect and inguinal-versus-femoral distinctions all come down to the same handful of landmarks, and the exam technique is built directly around locating them.

Anatomy: Hesselbach's Triangle & the Femoral Canal

Right groin, anterior view. The inferior epigastric vessels are the key landmark separating direct from indirect hernias.

Hesselbach's triangle is bounded by the inferior epigastric vessels (laterally), the rectus abdominis/linea semilunaris (medially), and the inguinal ligament (inferiorly). A hernia through this triangle — medial to the inferior epigastric vessels — is direct. A hernia through the internal inguinal ring (lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels), following the path of the spermatic cord/round ligament down the inguinal canal, is indirect.

The femoral canal sits below the inguinal ligament, medial to the femoral vein — remember it with the mnemonic NAVEL (lateral to medial: Nerve, Artery, Vein, Empty space, Lymphatics), where the femoral canal is the “empty space” that a femoral hernia occupies.

Examination Technique

General approach

  1. Examine the patient both standing and supine — many hernias are only apparent or only reducible in one position, and a supine-only exam will miss hernias that reduce spontaneously when lying down.
  2. Inspect first for an obvious bulge, then ask the patient to cough or perform Valsalva while you observe and then palpate — increased intra-abdominal pressure will produce a palpable or visible impulse at the hernia site, which is one of the most reliable confirmatory findings.
  3. For the inguinal canal specifically: invaginate the scrotal skin with your index finger, following the spermatic cord upward to the external inguinal ring, and gently advance your fingertip into the canal. Ask the patient to cough again and feel for an impulse against your fingertip.

Distinguishing direct from indirect at the bedside

With your finger in the inguinal canal (as above) or occluding the internal inguinal ring with two fingers placed over the mid-point of the inguinal ligament (roughly 1–2cm above the femoral pulse), ask the patient to cough again:

  • If the bulge is controlled/prevented by occluding the internal ring, this favors an indirect hernia (it requires passage through that ring).
  • If the bulge persists despite occlusion of the internal ring, this favors a direct hernia (it's bulging through Hesselbach's triangle directly, not through the ring you're occluding).

This bedside test is classic teaching but has real limitations in practice — many surgeons note that reliably distinguishing direct from indirect hernias by exam alone is difficult even for experienced examiners, and the distinction is often definitively made intraoperatively rather than preoperatively. Don't over-promise diagnostic certainty from this maneuver alone.

Femoral hernia palpation

Palpate below the inguinal ligament, medial to the femoral pulse (which you should locate first as your landmark). Femoral hernias are more common in women than inguinal hernias are (relative to men), and are notably more prone to incarceration and strangulation than inguinal hernias given the rigid, unforgiving boundaries of the femoral canal — a small, easily-overlooked femoral bulge deserves real diagnostic respect.

🚩 Can't miss — strangulation

An irreducible hernia with overlying skin changes, severe/disproportionate tenderness, and systemic toxicity signals compromised bowel blood supply — this is a surgical emergency, not a "try to reduce it again" situation. Attempting forceful reduction of a strangulated hernia risks reducing non-viable bowel back into the abdomen, which is worse than leaving it in place until surgery.

Documentation

Weak documentation Hernia present in groin. Reducible. Follow up with surgery.
Bulletproof documentation Right groin: 2cm reducible bulge palpable above the inguinal ligament, positive cough impulse, no overlying skin changes, non-tender, easily reducible with gentle pressure, no bowel sounds auscultated over the bulge. No femoral bulge. Left groin without bulge or impulse. Consistent with a reducible inguinal hernia; no signs of incarceration/strangulation. Elective surgical referral arranged; return precautions given for irreducibility, increasing pain, or overlying skin changes.

Rectal Examination

Technique, what to actually assess for, and how to document it — an exam that's frequently rushed or skipped despite carrying real diagnostic value in the right clinical context.

Indications

  • GI bleeding (evaluate for gross blood, melena, or occult blood; assess for a bleeding anorectal source such as hemorrhoids or a fissure)
  • Unexplained abdominal pain, particularly lower abdominal/pelvic pain, where rectal tenderness or a mass may localize the process (e.g., a pelvic appendix, pelvic abscess)
  • Suspected prostate pathology in males (BPH, prostatitis, malignancy)
  • Evaluation of anorectal complaints directly (pain, bleeding, discharge, prolapse)
  • Assessment of sphincter tone in the context of spinal cord injury or concern for cauda equina syndrome

Technique

Positioning

Left lateral decubitus (Sims) position is standard for a bedside exam, with hips and knees flexed; knee-chest or standing-bent-over positioning is an alternative depending on setting and patient mobility. Always explain the exam clearly beforehand and drape appropriately.

Step-by-step

  1. Inspect first — perianal skin for external hemorrhoids, fissures, fistula openings, skin tags, excoriation, or visible masses/prolapse, before ever touching the area with a gloved finger.
  2. Apply lubricant generously and warn the patient before insertion.
  3. Assess sphincter tone on initial insertion — note whether it is normal, reduced (concern for neurologic injury/cauda equina in the right context), or increased (can suggest anal fissure with reflexive spasm).
  4. Systematically palpate all quadrants of the rectal vault for masses, irregularities, or focal tenderness.
  5. In males, palpate the prostate anteriorly — note size, symmetry, contour (smooth vs. nodular), and tenderness (a boggy, exquisitely tender prostate suggests acute prostatitis, where vigorous prostatic massage should be avoided given bacteremia risk).
  6. Note stool character on the withdrawn glove — color, consistency, and presence of gross blood — and send for occult blood testing if indicated.

Findings & What They Suggest

Internal/external hemorrhoids

External hemorrhoids are visible on inspection, distal to the dentate line, and can thrombose (presenting as an acutely painful, firm, bluish perianal nodule). Internal hemorrhoids are proximal to the dentate line, generally painless (visceral innervation), and present with painless bright red bleeding, particularly with defecation.

Anal fissure

A tear in the anoderm, classically posterior midline, causing severe pain with defecation and bright red blood on the stool surface or toilet paper; often accompanied by reflexive sphincter spasm/increased tone on exam. A fissure in an atypical location (off the midline) should raise concern for an underlying process (Crohn's disease, malignancy, infection) rather than a simple mechanical fissure.

Perianal abscess / fistula-in-ano

A tender, fluctuant perianal mass suggests abscess; a chronically draining external opening with an identifiable tract suggests fistula-in-ano, often as a sequela of a prior abscess. Both warrant surgical evaluation, and fistulizing disease should prompt consideration of underlying Crohn's disease if recurrent or atypical.

Mass / polyp

A palpable rectal mass warrants further evaluation (endoscopic and/or imaging) regardless of the patient's age, though the differential shifts with age and risk factors — don't dismiss a palpable mass as "probably a hemorrhoid" without a clear visual/exam correlation.

Occult blood

A positive fecal occult blood test in the absence of an obvious anorectal source (fissure, hemorrhoid) should prompt further GI workup rather than being attributed reflexively to hemorrhoids, particularly in patients at appropriate colorectal cancer screening age or with other risk factors.

Localized rectal tenderness

Focal anterior or right-sided rectal tenderness can localize a pelvic inflammatory process — classically a pelvic-position appendicitis or a pelvic abscess — that might otherwise be missed on abdominal exam alone given the deep pelvic location.

Documentation

Weak documentation Rectal exam done, guaiac negative, no masses.
Bulletproof documentation Perianal inspection: no external hemorrhoids, fissures, or fistula openings. Digital rectal exam: normal sphincter tone, no focal masses or irregularities in any quadrant, no rectal tenderness. Prostate (if applicable): normal size, smooth contour, non-tender. Stool brown, formed, guaiac negative. No gross blood.

A single guaiac test has real limitations (false positives from dietary factors, false negatives from intermittent bleeding) — document it as one data point in the broader evaluation, not as a definitive rule-out for GI bleeding.

The Bulletproof Pattern, Applied to Physical Exam

The same core doctrine applies here as elsewhere in this atlas library: avoid conclusory language that claims more than your exam actually established. "Abdomen benign" or "no peritonitis" are conclusions, not findings — the defensible pattern documents the actual maneuvers performed and their actual results, then states your clinical impression separately and explicitly.

⚠ A frequent documentation trap in this exam specifically

Charting "guarding" without specifying voluntary versus involuntary is one of the most common under-specified findings in abdominal exam documentation — the two carry very different clinical weight, and a chart note that just says "guarding present" doesn't tell the next clinician (or a reviewer) which one you meant. Always specify.

Bulletproof documentation Abdomen: normal contour, no distension, no visible peristalsis or pulsation. Bowel sounds normoactive in all quadrants. A Transparent Note on Reference Sourcing

⚠ Worth knowing before you cite this atlas to a colleague

Physical exam technique and eponymous signs are fundamentally different from the drug-dosing and treatment-algorithm content elsewhere in this atlas library. There isn't a single "GI society guideline" or "surgery society guideline" that defines how to perform Murphy's sign or grade abdominal tenderness — this content is drawn from classical clinical semiology (the physical diagnosis teaching tradition found in texts like Bates' Guide to Physical Examination and DeGowin's Diagnostic Examination) and from the diagnostic literature evaluating each sign's sensitivity/specificity, rather than from a single authoritative body.

Where a genuine professional-society guideline does apply — for example, the American College of Gastroenterology's positions on GI bleeding workup, or the American College of Surgeons' guidance on hernia management — that guideline governs the downstream workup and treatment decision, not the physical exam maneuver itself. It's worth keeping that distinction clear rather than attributing exam technique to a guideline body that doesn't actually publish exam-technique standards.

What this means practically

  • Use this atlas confidently for exam technique, sign recognition, and documentation language.
  • When a finding here should trigger a specific workup or treatment pathway (e.g., a positive Murphy's sign prompting RUQ ultrasound and cholecystitis management, or hernia incarceration prompting surgical referral), cross-reference the relevant guideline-based content in the GI Atlas's differential and pharmacology domains rather than treating this exam-technique atlas as the guideline source itself.
  • Sensitivity/specificity figures for individual signs vary meaningfully across studies and patient populations — treat the numbers referenced throughout this atlas as general teaching estimates, and verify against current literature if precision matters for a specific clinical or academic purpose.

Shoulder & Clavicle

8 findings

1. Proximal humerus fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the proximal humerus, with CT used to characterize comminution and fragment displacement per the Neer classification when surgical planning is needed.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture lines through the surgical neck, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, and/or articular surface, with CT best demonstrating the number of displaced fragments.

Differential
  • 2-part, 3-part, or 4-part fracture (Neer classification, guides surgical vs. conservative management)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for preoperative planning in comminuted or displaced fractures.

2. Glenoid fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the glenoid, often associated with shoulder dislocation, best characterized by CT for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the glenoid rim or fossa, with CT quantifying the percentage of glenoid bone loss relevant to stability and surgical decision-making.

Differential
  • Bony Bankart lesion (anteroinferior glenoid rim fracture from dislocation)
  • Glenoid fossa fracture (intra-articular extension)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT-based glenoid bone loss quantification guides surgical approach.

3. Scapular fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the scapula, uncommon and associated with high-energy trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the scapular body, neck, spine, or glenoid, with CT best demonstrating full fracture extent and displacement.

Differential
  • High-energy traumatic fracture (marker of significant force, prompts search for associated thoracic injuries)
Next Step

Trauma survey for associated pulmonary/vascular injury; orthopedic referral.

4. Clavicle fracture, CT

Variable

Fracture of the clavicle, with CT reserved for complex or medial/sternoclavicular-involving fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the clavicle, with CT particularly useful for characterizing medial clavicle fractures and sternoclavicular joint involvement.

Differential
  • Midshaft fracture (most common, usually managed with plain film alone)
  • Medial/sternoclavicular fracture-dislocation (CT preferred for characterization)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for posterior sternoclavicular dislocation given proximity to mediastinal structures.

5. Sternoclavicular joint dislocation, CT

Concerning

Dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint, with posterior dislocation carrying risk of mediastinal great vessel/airway injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Malalignment of the medial clavicle relative to the sternum; posterior dislocation shows the clavicular head displaced behind the sternum, potentially compressing mediastinal structures.

Differential
  • Anterior dislocation (more common, lower risk)
  • Posterior dislocation (higher risk given proximity to great vessels/trachea)
Next Step

CT angiography if posterior dislocation to assess for vascular compromise; orthopedic/trauma surgery consultation.

6. Hill-Sachs lesion, CT

Variable

Compression fracture of the posterolateral humeral head from impaction against the glenoid rim during anterior shoulder dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped impaction defect in the posterolateral humeral head, best assessed for size and engagement risk with the glenoid defect on CT.

Differential
  • Hill-Sachs lesion (marker of prior anterior dislocation)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; size and 'engaging' potential relative to the glenoid defect influences surgical decision-making for recurrent instability.

7. Glenohumeral osteoarthritis, CT

Benign

Degenerative change of the glenohumeral joint, with CT used primarily for preoperative arthroplasty planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis, with CT particularly useful for assessing glenoid version and bone stock before shoulder arthroplasty.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Post-traumatic arthritis
  • Rotator cuff arthropathy (superior humeral head migration)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for arthroplasty planning if CT is being obtained for this purpose.

8. Scapulothoracic dissociation

Concerning

Severe, high-energy injury with lateral displacement of the scapula and disruption of the surrounding neurovascular structures, essentially a closed forequarter amputation.

Imaging Characteristics

Lateral displacement of the scapula relative to the midline compared to the contralateral side, often with associated clavicle/rib fractures and vascular injury.

Differential
  • Scapulothoracic dissociation (limb-threatening injury)
Next Step

Emergent vascular surgery/orthopedic trauma consultation; CT angiography to assess the subclavian/axillary vessels.

Elbow & Forearm

7 findings

1. Radial head fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the radial head, with CT reserved for complex or comminuted fractures for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the radial head/neck, with CT best demonstrating fragment number and displacement in comminuted (Mason type III) fractures.

Differential
  • Mason type I (non-displaced)
  • Mason type II (displaced, simple)
  • Mason type III (comminuted, may require surgical management)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for surgical planning in comminuted fractures.

2. Coronoid process fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the ulnar coronoid process, an important stabilizer of the elbow, often part of complex instability patterns (terrible triad injury).

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the coronoid process, classified by the Regan-Morrey or O'Driscoll systems based on fragment size and involvement.

Differential
  • Isolated coronoid fracture
  • Terrible triad injury (radial head fracture, coronoid fracture, and elbow dislocation)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; assess for associated elbow instability given the coronoid's stabilizing role.

3. Complex elbow fracture-dislocation, CT

Concerning

Severe elbow injury with combined fracture and instability, requiring CT for full characterization of all injured components.

Imaging Characteristics

Combination of fractures (radial head, coronoid, olecranon) with joint malalignment, best fully characterized with CT and 3D reconstruction for surgical planning.

Differential
  • Terrible triad injury
  • Monteggia fracture-dislocation variant
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning.

4. Distal humerus fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the distal humerus, often intercondylar/comminuted, with CT used for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the distal humeral condyles, with CT best demonstrating articular fragment number and displacement.

Differential
  • Intercondylar (Y or T-shaped) fracture
  • Single condyle fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning given the frequent need for open reduction internal fixation.

5. Both-bone forearm fracture, CT

Concerning

Combined radius and ulna shaft fracture, with CT reserved for complex or intra-articular extension.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture of both the radius and ulna shafts, assessed for displacement, angulation, and rotational alignment.

Differential
  • Both-bone forearm fracture (typically requires surgical fixation in adults)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral.

6. Monteggia fracture-dislocation, CT

Concerning

Ulnar fracture with associated radial head dislocation, easily missed if the elbow is not adequately imaged.

Imaging Characteristics

Ulnar shaft fracture with radial head dislocation from the capitellum, best confirmed by ensuring the radial head aligns with the capitellum on all views/planes.

Differential
  • Monteggia fracture-dislocation (high index of suspicion needed to avoid missing the radial head dislocation component)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; often requires surgical fixation, particularly in adults.

7. Elbow heterotopic ossification

Variable

Abnormal bone formation in the soft tissues around the elbow, a recognized complication of elbow trauma or surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Mature or immature bone formation within the soft tissues surrounding the elbow joint, which can restrict range of motion.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic heterotopic ossification
  • Post-surgical heterotopic ossification
Next Step

Orthopedic referral if causing significant motion restriction; surgical excision considered once the HO has matured.

Wrist & Hand

8 findings

1. Scaphoid fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the scaphoid, with CT used when plain film is negative but clinical suspicion remains high, or for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the scaphoid waist (most common), proximal pole, or distal pole, with CT more sensitive than plain film, particularly for subtle or occult fractures.

Differential
  • Scaphoid waist fracture (most common, higher avascular necrosis risk given retrograde blood supply)
  • Proximal pole fracture (highest AVN risk)
Next Step

Orthopedic/hand surgery referral given the avascular necrosis and nonunion risk with missed or inadequately treated scaphoid fractures.

2. Distal radius fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the distal radius, with CT reserved for intra-articular or comminuted fractures for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the distal radius metaphysis, with CT best demonstrating articular step-off and fragment displacement in complex fractures.

Differential
  • Colles fracture (dorsal displacement)
  • Smith fracture (volar displacement)
  • Intra-articular comminuted fracture (CT for surgical planning)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning if intra-articular or significantly displaced.

3. Perilunate/lunate dislocation, CT

Concerning

Severe carpal instability injury, easily missed on plain film, with CT clarifying the full extent of carpal malalignment.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption of the normal carpal alignment, with the lunate maintaining its relationship to the radius but the capitate displaced dorsally (perilunate) or the lunate itself displaced (lunate dislocation, 'spilled teacup' sign).

Differential
  • Perilunate dislocation
  • Lunate dislocation (more severe)
Next Step

Emergent hand surgery/orthopedic consultation given median nerve injury risk and the need for prompt reduction.

4. Carpal fracture, other (triquetrum, capitate, hamate)

Variable

Fracture of a carpal bone other than the scaphoid, with CT used for subtle or complex fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the affected carpal bone, with CT improving detection of subtle fractures not well seen on plain film.

Differential
  • Triquetral fracture (often a dorsal chip fracture)
  • Hamate hook fracture (associated with racquet/bat sports)
  • Capitate fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic/hand surgery referral.

5. Metacarpal fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of a metacarpal, with CT reserved for complex, comminuted, or intra-articular fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the metacarpal, with CT best demonstrating articular involvement (e.g., Bennett/Rolando fracture patterns at the thumb base).

Differential
  • Boxer's fracture (5th metacarpal neck)
  • Bennett fracture (intra-articular thumb base)
  • Rolando fracture (comminuted intra-articular thumb base)
Next Step

Orthopedic/hand surgery referral for intra-articular or significantly displaced fractures.

6. Carpal instability, CT (SLAC/SLIL)

Variable

Scapholunate ligament injury leading to progressive carpal malalignment and arthritis if untreated.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened scapholunate interval and altered carpal bone alignment, with CT providing detailed bony assessment though MRI/arthrography better assesses the ligament itself.

Differential
  • Scapholunate ligament injury/instability
  • Scapholunate advanced collapse (SLAC) pattern (chronic, arthritic end-stage)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; MRI or arthrography for direct ligament assessment if not already obtained.

7. Phalanx fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of a finger phalanx, with CT reserved for complex intra-articular fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through a phalanx, with CT best demonstrating articular step-off in complex fractures.

Differential
  • Simple phalanx fracture (usually managed with plain film alone)
  • Intra-articular comminuted fracture (CT for surgical planning)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral for intra-articular or significantly displaced fractures.

8. Distal radioulnar joint instability, CT

Variable

Instability of the distal radioulnar joint, assessed with comparative bilateral CT in pronation/supination.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal ulnar position relative to the sigmoid notch of the radius on axial CT, compared to the contralateral normal side in matched positions.

Differential
  • Distal radioulnar joint instability (often associated with TFCC injury)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; correlate with TFCC assessment (MRI/arthrography).

Pelvis & Hip

10 findings

1. Pelvic ring fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the bony pelvis, with CT as the standard for full characterization and hemorrhage assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the pubic rami, ilium, or sacrum, with CT assessing ring stability (anterior and posterior injury) and any associated active contrast extravasation.

Differential
  • Stable, isolated fracture (e.g., single pubic ramus)
  • Unstable ring disruption (anterior-posterior compression, lateral compression, or vertical shear patterns)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma consultation; assess hemodynamic stability and screen for active bleeding requiring IR embolization.

2. Acetabular fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the acetabulum, with CT essential for classification (Judet-Letournel system) and surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the acetabular columns/walls, with CT and 3D reconstruction best demonstrating the specific fracture pattern and articular congruity.

Differential
  • Anterior column, posterior column, or combined pattern fractures (per Judet-Letournel classification)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma referral for surgical planning.

3. Femoral neck fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the femoral neck, with CT used for occult fracture detection or surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the femoral neck, with CT more sensitive than plain film for subtle, non-displaced fractures.

Differential
  • Femoral neck fracture (higher avascular necrosis risk given the femoral head's blood supply)
  • Occult fracture (if plain film negative but clinical suspicion high)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; MRI remains more sensitive than CT for truly occult fractures if CT is also negative.

4. Hip dislocation, CT

Concerning

Dislocation of the femoral head from the acetabulum, with post-reduction CT used to assess for associated fractures and intra-articular fragments.

Imaging Characteristics

Femoral head displaced from the acetabular socket (posterior dislocation more common), with CT assessing for associated acetabular wall fractures and loose intra-articular fragments after reduction.

Differential
  • Posterior dislocation (most common)
  • Anterior dislocation
Next Step

Orthopedic emergency for reduction if not already performed; post-reduction CT to assess congruity and loose bodies.

5. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head, CT

Concerning

Ischemic bone death of the femoral head, with CT less sensitive than MRI but able to show more advanced findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Subchondral lucency and collapse ('crescent sign') in more advanced disease; CT is less sensitive than MRI for early-stage AVN.

Differential
  • Avascular necrosis (steroid use, alcohol use, trauma, sickle cell disease among risk factors)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; MRI is the preferred modality for early detection given greater sensitivity.

6. Hip osteoarthritis, CT

Benign

Degenerative change of the hip joint, with CT used primarily for preoperative arthroplasty planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis/cysts, with CT useful for assessing acetabular bone stock and version before arthroplasty.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Secondary osteoarthritis (dysplasia, prior trauma, avascular necrosis)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for arthroplasty planning if CT obtained for this purpose.

7. Femoroacetabular impingement, CT morphology

Variable

Abnormal hip morphology causing abutment between the femur and acetabulum, a recognized cause of early hip osteoarthritis.

Imaging Characteristics

Cam morphology: aspherical femoral head-neck junction. Pincer morphology: acetabular overcoverage. Both assessed on CT with alpha angle and other morphologic measurements.

Differential
  • Cam-type impingement
  • Pincer-type impingement
  • Mixed pattern
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; correlate with hip pain symptoms and consider MRI for associated labral assessment.

8. Sacroiliac joint fracture/disruption, CT

Concerning

Injury to the sacroiliac joint, part of the posterior pelvic ring, assessed for stability.

Imaging Characteristics

Widening or fracture through the sacroiliac joint complex, assessed alongside the anterior pelvic ring injury for overall pelvic stability.

Differential
  • SI joint disruption (posterior pelvic ring instability)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma consultation for pelvic stability assessment.

9. Hip prosthesis evaluation, CT

Variable

Assessment of a hip replacement for complications, with CT (often using metal artifact reduction techniques) evaluating component position and surrounding bone/soft tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of component positioning, periprosthetic lucency (loosening), fracture, or soft-tissue collections around the prosthesis.

Differential
  • Well-positioned, stable prosthesis
  • Periprosthetic loosening
  • Periprosthetic fracture
  • Adverse local tissue reaction (with metal-on-metal implants)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral if complications identified.

10. Sacral insufficiency fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the sacrum from bone fragility (osteoporosis, prior pelvic radiation) rather than acute trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertical or H-shaped fracture line through the sacral ala, often bilateral, without a clear traumatic mechanism.

Differential
  • Osteoporotic insufficiency fracture
  • Post-radiation insufficiency fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine referral; osteoporosis workup and pain management.

Femur & Knee

10 findings

1. Femoral shaft fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the femoral shaft, with CT reserved for complex or pathologic fracture characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the femoral diaphysis, assessed for comminution and, if atypical, for an underlying lesion suggesting a pathologic fracture.

Differential
  • Traumatic femoral shaft fracture
  • Pathologic fracture (underlying lesion, atypical femur fracture from bisphosphonate use)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma referral; evaluate for an underlying lesion if the fracture pattern or history is atypical (e.g., transverse fracture with lateral cortical thickening in bisphosphonate-associated atypical femur fractures).

2. Tibial plateau fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the tibial plateau, with CT essential for full characterization of articular depression and comminution.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the tibial plateau with variable depression/displacement, best characterized by CT for surgical planning (Schatzker classification).

Differential
  • Schatzker type I-VI fracture patterns
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning.

3. Distal femur fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the distal femur, often intercondylar/comminuted, with CT used for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the distal femoral condyles/metaphysis, with CT best demonstrating articular fragment number and displacement.

Differential
  • Supracondylar fracture
  • Intercondylar (T or Y-shaped) fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning.

4. Patella fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the patella, with CT reserved for comminuted or complex fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the patella, with CT best demonstrating fragment number and articular surface involvement in comminuted fractures.

Differential
  • Transverse patella fracture (most common)
  • Comminuted/stellate fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning if displaced or comminuted.

5. Osteochondral defect, CT

Variable

Focal defect of the articular cartilage and subchondral bone, from trauma or osteochondritis dissecans.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal subchondral bone defect, sometimes with a visible loose fragment, most often at the femoral condyles or talar dome.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic osteochondral defect
  • Osteochondritis dissecans
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MRI for cartilage and fragment stability assessment.

6. Knee osteoarthritis, CT

Benign

Degenerative change of the knee joint, with CT used primarily for preoperative arthroplasty planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis, with CT useful for bone stock and alignment assessment before arthroplasty.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Post-traumatic arthritis
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for arthroplasty planning if CT obtained for this purpose.

7. Chondrocalcinosis, CT

Variable

Calcification of hyaline and/or fibrocartilage, most often from calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear calcification paralleling the articular cartilage or within the meniscus, most often in the knee.

Differential
  • Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD, most common cause)
  • Hemochromatosis-associated (consider in atypical distribution)
Next Step

Correlate with joint aspiration if acute pseudogout is suspected clinically.

8. Patellar dislocation, CT (post-reduction)

Variable

Lateral patellar dislocation, with post-reduction CT assessing for osteochondral fragments and predisposing anatomy.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment for osteochondral fragments (from the medial patellar facet or lateral femoral condyle) and predisposing factors (trochlear dysplasia, patella alta) after reduction.

Differential
  • Lateral patellar dislocation, reduced
  • Associated osteochondral fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MRI often complementary for soft-tissue (MPFL) injury assessment.

9. Knee prosthesis evaluation, CT

Variable

Assessment of a knee replacement for complications, using metal artifact reduction techniques.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of component alignment/rotation, periprosthetic lucency (loosening), fracture, or soft-tissue abnormality around the prosthesis.

Differential
  • Well-positioned, stable prosthesis
  • Periprosthetic loosening or infection
  • Periprosthetic fracture
  • Component malrotation (a recognized cause of post-arthroplasty pain)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral if complications identified.

10. Tibial tubercle avulsion fracture

Concerning

Avulsion fracture of the tibial tubercle at the patellar tendon insertion, most common in adolescent athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture/avulsion of the tibial tubercle apophysis, with CT used for complex or displaced fractures for surgical planning.

Differential
  • Tibial tubercle avulsion fracture (adolescent athletes, jumping sports)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical fixation if significantly displaced.

Tib-Fib & Ankle

8 findings

1. Ankle fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the ankle mortise (distal tibia/fibula), with CT reserved for complex, comminuted, or intra-articular fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture of the malleoli and/or distal tibial plafond, with CT best demonstrating articular involvement in pilon-type fractures.

Differential
  • Weber A, B, or C fibular fracture patterns
  • Pilon fracture (tibial plafond, higher energy)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning if intra-articular or unstable.

2. Pilon fracture, CT

Concerning

High-energy fracture of the distal tibial plafond with articular comminution, requiring CT for surgical planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Comminuted fracture of the distal tibial articular surface, with CT and 3D reconstruction essential for characterizing the fracture pattern.

Differential
  • Pilon (tibial plafond) fracture (axial loading mechanism)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma referral for staged surgical management given soft-tissue injury risk with these high-energy fractures.

3. Talus fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the talus, with CT important given avascular necrosis risk and the complexity of talar anatomy.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the talar neck, body, or dome, with CT best demonstrating displacement relevant to the avascular necrosis risk (Hawkins classification for neck fractures).

Differential
  • Talar neck fracture (Hawkins classification correlates with AVN risk)
  • Talar dome osteochondral fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral given significant avascular necrosis risk, particularly with displaced talar neck fractures.

4. Calcaneus fracture, CT characterization

Concerning

Fracture of the calcaneus, often from axial loading (fall from height), with CT essential for characterizing subtalar joint involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the calcaneus, with CT best demonstrating subtalar joint congruity and the degree of comminution (Sanders classification).

Differential
  • Intra-articular calcaneus fracture (Sanders type I-IV, involves the subtalar joint)
  • Extra-articular calcaneus fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical planning; assess for associated lumbar spine fracture given the shared axial loading mechanism.

5. Maisonneuve fracture, CT

Concerning

Proximal fibular fracture associated with syndesmotic and deltoid ligament injury, easily missed if only the ankle is imaged.

Imaging Characteristics

Proximal fibular shaft fracture with associated ankle mortise widening/syndesmotic injury, requiring imaging of the full length of the fibula to identify.

Differential
  • Maisonneuve fracture (high index of suspicion needed with ankle instability but a normal-appearing ankle fracture pattern)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; image the entire fibula when this injury pattern is suspected clinically.

6. Tib-fib shaft fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the tibial and/or fibular shaft, with CT reserved for complex or comminuted fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the tibia and/or fibula shaft, assessed for comminution, displacement, and any articular extension.

Differential
  • Traumatic tib-fib shaft fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; monitor for compartment syndrome given the high-risk anatomic location.

7. Ankle osteoarthritis, CT

Benign

Degenerative change of the ankle joint, often post-traumatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis at the tibiotalar joint.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (most common cause at the ankle, unlike the hip/knee where primary OA predominates)
  • Primary osteoarthritis (less common at this joint)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management.

8. Os trigonum, CT

Benign

Accessory ossicle posterior to the talus, a common normal variant that can become symptomatic with posterior ankle impingement.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-corticated accessory ossicle posterior to the talar dome, distinct from an acute fracture fragment by its smooth, rounded margins.

Differential
  • Os trigonum (normal variant, usually incidental)
  • Acute posterior talar process fracture (less well-corticated margins)
Next Step

No action needed for an incidental os trigonum; orthopedic referral if causing symptomatic posterior impingement.

Foot

6 findings

1. Lisfranc fracture-dislocation, CT

Concerning

Injury to the tarsometatarsal joint complex, often subtle and easily missed, with CT improving detection of associated fractures and malalignment.

Imaging Characteristics

Malalignment of the tarsometatarsal joints, with CT better demonstrating subtle fractures and joint widening than plain film.

Differential
  • Lisfranc fracture-dislocation (high index of suspicion needed given subtle presentation)
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral given the risk of chronic pain and post-traumatic arthritis if missed or inadequately treated.

2. Jones fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction of the fifth metatarsal, with higher nonunion risk than a simple avulsion fracture given the watershed blood supply.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture at the junction of the proximal metaphysis and diaphysis of the fifth metatarsal, distinct from the more proximal, better-healing avulsion fracture at the tuberosity.

Differential
  • Jones fracture (higher nonunion risk, watershed vascular zone)
  • Fifth metatarsal tuberosity avulsion fracture (better healing potential)
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral; Jones fractures often require surgical fixation, particularly in athletes, given the nonunion risk with conservative management.

3. Midfoot/hindfoot fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the midfoot or hindfoot bones, with CT used for complex or comminuted fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the navicular, cuboid, or cuneiform bones, with CT best demonstrating articular involvement and comminution.

Differential
  • Navicular fracture
  • Cuboid fracture
  • Cuneiform fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral for surgical planning if displaced or intra-articular.

4. Forefoot fracture, CT characterization

Variable

Fracture of the metatarsals or phalanges, with CT reserved for complex fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through a metatarsal or phalanx, with CT best demonstrating comminution and displacement in complex fractures.

Differential
  • Metatarsal fracture
  • Phalangeal fracture
Next Step

Podiatric/orthopedic referral for displaced or intra-articular fractures.

5. Accessory navicular, CT

Benign

Accessory ossicle at the medial navicular, a common normal variant that can become symptomatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-corticated accessory ossicle or bony prominence at the medial aspect of the navicular, at the posterior tibial tendon insertion.

Differential
  • Accessory navicular (type I, II, or III, normal variant)
Next Step

No action needed if asymptomatic; orthopedic/podiatric referral if causing symptomatic medial midfoot pain.

6. Charcot foot, CT

Concerning

Neuropathic joint destruction, most often from diabetic peripheral neuropathy, which can closely mimic osteomyelitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint destruction, fragmentation, and malalignment (often midfoot), sometimes with debris and bone fragments, in a patient with known peripheral neuropathy.

Differential
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy (acute inflammatory phase can closely mimic osteomyelitis)
  • Osteomyelitis (key differential, particularly with an overlying ulcer)
Next Step

Endocrine/podiatric/orthopedic referral; MRI or nuclear medicine studies may help distinguish from osteomyelitis when overlap is present.

Bone Tumors & Lesions

12 findings

1. Osteochondroma, CT

Benign

Most common benign bone tumor, a cartilage-capped bony projection continuous with the underlying bone marrow.

Imaging Characteristics

Bony outgrowth with cortical and medullary continuity with the parent bone, directed away from the nearest joint, often at the metaphysis of long bones.

Differential
  • Osteochondroma (benign, most common bone tumor)
  • Multiple hereditary exostoses (if numerous, associated with malignant transformation risk)
Next Step

No treatment needed for an asymptomatic, classic osteochondroma; orthopedic referral if growing after skeletal maturity (concerning for malignant transformation) or symptomatic.

2. Enchondroma, CT

Benign

Common benign intramedullary cartilage tumor, most often in the hand phalanges or long bones.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined lytic lesion with characteristic 'rings and arcs' or popcorn-like chondroid matrix calcification, without cortical destruction or a soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Enchondroma (benign)
  • Low-grade chondrosarcoma (larger, endosteal scalloping, cortical involvement raises concern, particularly in long bones)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic, asymptomatic hand enchondroma; orthopedic referral for lesions with aggressive features or in long bones given the differential with chondrosarcoma.

3. Non-ossifying fibroma

Benign

Common benign fibrous bone lesion in children and adolescents, often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, eccentric, lytic lesion with a sclerotic, scalloped border, most often in the metaphysis of long bones.

Differential
  • Non-ossifying fibroma (benign, common incidental pediatric finding)
  • Fibrous cortical defect (smaller version of the same lesion spectrum)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic, asymptomatic lesion; most resolve spontaneously with growth.

4. Simple (unicameral) bone cyst

Variable

Benign, fluid-filled bone lesion, most common in the proximal humerus or femur of children.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, central, lytic lesion with a thin cortex, sometimes with a 'fallen fragment sign' if pathologically fractured (a fragment settling dependently within the fluid-filled cyst).

Differential
  • Simple bone cyst (benign)
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst (more expansile, may have fluid-fluid levels)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, particularly if there is pathologic fracture risk given the lesion's location and size.

5. Aneurysmal bone cyst, CT

Variable

Benign but locally aggressive, expansile, blood-filled cystic bone lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Expansile, multiloculated lytic lesion with a thin cortical rim ('blow-out' appearance) and fluid-fluid levels reflecting layered blood products.

Differential
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst (primary or secondary to an underlying lesion)
  • Telangiectatic osteosarcoma (important malignant mimic to exclude)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral; biopsy to exclude an underlying or malignant process before definitive treatment.

6. Osteoid osteoma, CT

Benign

Benign bone-forming tumor, classically causing pain worse at night and relieved by NSAIDs.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round lucent nidus (typically <1.5 cm) with surrounding dense reactive sclerosis, best characterized on CT which is the most sensitive modality for nidus detection.

Differential
  • Osteoid osteoma (classic NSAID-responsive night pain)
  • Osteoblastoma (larger version, >1.5-2 cm)
  • Brodie abscess (subacute osteomyelitis, can mimic)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; radiofrequency ablation is a common effective treatment for persistent symptoms.

7. Giant cell tumor of bone

Concerning

Locally aggressive, typically benign tumor most common in young adults at the epiphysis of long bones (classically distal femur/proximal tibia).

Imaging Characteristics

Eccentric, lytic, epiphyseal-extending-to-metaphyseal lesion with a narrow zone of transition and, often, cortical thinning/expansion without a sclerotic rim.

Differential
  • Giant cell tumor of bone (locally aggressive, can rarely metastasize despite benign histology)
  • Clear cell chondrosarcoma (also epiphyseal, key differential)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and resection/curettage planning.

8. Osteosarcoma, CT

Concerning

Most common primary malignant bone tumor, most often in adolescents at the metaphysis of long bones.

Imaging Characteristics

Aggressive, mixed lytic-sclerotic lesion with cortical destruction, an associated soft-tissue mass, and periosteal reaction (Codman triangle or 'sunburst' pattern).

Differential
  • Osteosarcoma (classic in adolescent long bone metaphysis)
  • Ewing sarcoma (key differential, more often diaphyseal)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; staging workup (chest CT for metastases, MRI for local extent) and biopsy.

9. Ewing sarcoma, CT

Concerning

Aggressive malignant bone tumor, most common in children and adolescents, classically diaphyseal.

Imaging Characteristics

Permeative, moth-eaten lytic lesion with an aggressive periosteal reaction (lamellated 'onion skin' pattern) and often a large associated soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Ewing sarcoma (classic diaphyseal location)
  • Osteomyelitis (can closely mimic clinically and on imaging)
  • Osteosarcoma
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; staging workup and biopsy.

10. Chondrosarcoma, CT

Concerning

Malignant cartilage-forming tumor, most common in older adults, can arise de novo or from malignant transformation of a benign cartilage lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Lytic lesion with chondroid ('rings and arcs') matrix calcification, endosteal scalloping, and cortical destruction/soft-tissue extension in higher-grade lesions.

Differential
  • Chondrosarcoma (grade correlates with aggressiveness)
  • Enchondroma (benign counterpart, key differential based on aggressive features)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

11. Fibrous dysplasia, CT

Variable

Benign fibro-osseous developmental lesion where normal bone is replaced by fibrous tissue, monostotic or polyostotic.

Imaging Characteristics

Expansile lesion with a characteristic 'ground-glass' matrix, well-defined margins, and a thin, intact cortex, most often in the femur, tibia, or ribs.

Differential
  • Fibrous dysplasia (monostotic or polyostotic)
  • McCune-Albright syndrome (if polyostotic with café-au-lait spots and endocrinopathy)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for monitoring; usually managed conservatively unless causing significant deformity or fracture risk.

12. Bone metastasis, CT

Concerning

Secondary malignant bone lesion, the most common malignant bone tumor overall in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Lytic, sclerotic, or mixed lesion, which can be solitary or multiple, with cortical destruction and sometimes a soft-tissue mass in aggressive lesions.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common, especially breast, lung, prostate, renal, thyroid primaries)
  • Multiple myeloma
  • Primary bone tumor (less likely if multiple lesions)
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging; correlate with known malignancy history.

Infection

5 findings

1. Osteomyelitis, CT

Concerning

Bone infection, with CT useful for assessing cortical destruction, sequestrum, and soft-tissue extent.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical destruction with adjacent periosteal reaction and soft-tissue swelling; a sequestrum (necrotic bone fragment) and involucrum (surrounding reactive new bone) may be seen in chronic osteomyelitis.

Differential
  • Acute osteomyelitis
  • Chronic osteomyelitis (sequestrum/involucrum pattern)
Next Step

Orthopedic/infectious disease referral; MRI remains more sensitive for early marrow changes, while CT better shows cortical detail and sequestrum.

2. Septic arthritis, CT

Concerning

Joint infection, a surgical emergency given cartilage destruction risk, with CT showing effusion and bony changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint effusion with adjacent bony erosion/destruction in more advanced or chronic cases; CT is less sensitive than ultrasound/MRI for early joint effusion detection.

Differential
  • Septic arthritis (surgical emergency requiring prompt drainage)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic consultation for joint aspiration/washout; do not delay treatment for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

3. Necrotizing fasciitis, CT

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection with fascial plane involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Fascial thickening and fluid tracking along fascial planes, with soft-tissue gas being a specific but insensitive finding (its absence does not exclude the diagnosis).

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
  • Simple cellulitis/abscess (less extensive fascial involvement)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation for debridement; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation if clinical suspicion is high.

4. Soft tissue abscess, CT

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, low-attenuation fluid collection within the soft tissues, sometimes with internal gas.

Differential
  • Soft tissue abscess
  • Infected hematoma/seroma
Next Step

Incision and drainage plus antibiotics.

5. Diabetic foot osteomyelitis, CT

Concerning

Bone infection in the diabetic foot, often from a contiguous overlying ulcer, complicated by the differential with Charcot neuroarthropathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical destruction adjacent to a soft-tissue ulcer/sinus tract, though distinguishing infection from Charcot changes can be challenging on CT alone.

Differential
  • Diabetic foot osteomyelitis
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy (key differential, particularly in the acute phase)
Next Step

MRI or nuclear medicine studies often needed to help distinguish from Charcot changes; podiatric/infectious disease referral.

Metabolic & Systemic Bone Disease

6 findings

1. Osteoporosis, CT assessment

Variable

Reduced bone mineral density, with CT (including opportunistic screening on studies obtained for other indications) able to suggest but not formally diagnose osteoporosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced bone attenuation and cortical thinning, with vertebral body Hounsfield unit measurements on abdominal/chest CT increasingly used for opportunistic osteoporosis screening.

Differential
  • Osteoporosis (formal diagnosis requires DEXA)
  • Osteopenia
Next Step

DEXA scan for formal bone density assessment and correlate with fracture risk; osteoporosis workup and treatment as indicated.

2. Paget disease, CT

Variable

Chronic disorder of bone remodeling, causing enlarged, disorganized, and structurally weakened bone.

Imaging Characteristics

Bone expansion with thickened, coarsened trabeculae and cortex, in a pattern that can be lytic (early/active phase), sclerotic (later phase), or mixed.

Differential
  • Paget disease (correlate with elevated alkaline phosphatase)
Next Step

Correlate with alkaline phosphatase; orthopedic referral for monitoring and treatment (bisphosphonates) if active/symptomatic; rare risk of malignant transformation to osteosarcoma.

3. Hyperparathyroidism, skeletal changes on CT

Variable

Skeletal manifestations of excess parathyroid hormone, ranging from subtle to severe (brown tumors).

Imaging Characteristics

Subperiosteal bone resorption (classically at the radial aspect of the middle phalanges), and in severe cases, lytic 'brown tumors' from osteoclastic bone resorption.

Differential
  • Primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism
Next Step

Endocrine referral; correlate with calcium and PTH levels.

4. Osteomalacia/rickets, CT signs

Variable

Impaired bone mineralization from vitamin D deficiency or other metabolic causes, causing bone softening.

Imaging Characteristics

Looser zones (pseudofractures, radiolucent bands perpendicular to the cortex), bone deformity, and reduced bone density.

Differential
  • Vitamin D deficiency (most common cause)
  • Renal osteodystrophy
  • Hypophosphatemic disorders
Next Step

Correlate with vitamin D, calcium, phosphate, and PTH levels; endocrine referral.

5. Renal osteodystrophy, CT

Variable

Skeletal changes from chronic kidney disease-related mineral and bone disorder.

Imaging Characteristics

Combination of findings that can include subperiosteal resorption (secondary hyperparathyroidism), osteosclerosis ('rugger jersey spine' pattern), and soft-tissue calcification.

Differential
  • Renal osteodystrophy (correlate with chronic kidney disease history)
Next Step

Nephrology/endocrine referral for management of the underlying mineral and bone disorder.

6. Diffuse skeletal metastatic disease, CT

Concerning

Widespread bony metastatic involvement, seen on staging CT for a known malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple lytic, sclerotic, or mixed lesions scattered throughout the visualized skeleton.

Differential
  • Widespread metastatic disease
  • Multiple myeloma
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging and systemic treatment planning; assess for pathologic fracture risk at weight-bearing sites.

Soft Tissue & Miscellaneous

10 findings

1. Soft tissue sarcoma, CT characterization

Concerning

Malignant soft-tissue tumor, with CT used for local staging and to assess for pulmonary metastases.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, heterogeneous, often deep soft-tissue mass with variable enhancement; MRI is generally preferred for local characterization, while CT chest is standard for metastatic staging.

Differential
  • Soft tissue sarcoma
  • Benign soft-tissue mass (lipoma, less heterogeneous)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral; MRI for local staging and CT chest for pulmonary metastasis screening.

2. Lipoma, CT characterization

Benign

Common benign fat-containing soft-tissue tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, fat-attenuation mass with a thin, imperceptible capsule and no enhancing soft-tissue components.

Differential
  • Simple lipoma (benign)
  • Well-differentiated liposarcoma (larger, with thick septa or soft-tissue nodularity - key distinguishing features)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous, small lipoma; further characterization for larger or heterogeneous fatty masses.

3. Myositis ossificans, CT

Benign

Heterotopic bone formation within muscle, typically following trauma, which can mimic a malignant tumor early in its course.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral ('zonal') pattern of calcification/ossification with a lucent center, evolving over weeks from an initially ill-defined soft-tissue mass to a well-organized, peripherally calcified lesion.

Differential
  • Myositis ossificans (peripheral zonal maturation pattern is characteristic and distinguishes it from malignancy)
  • Extraskeletal osteosarcoma (early lesions can mimic before the characteristic zonal pattern develops)
Next Step

Serial imaging over several weeks to confirm the characteristic maturation pattern if the lesion is imaged early/atypically.

4. Tumoral calcinosis

Variable

Large, periarticular calcified soft-tissue masses, associated with chronic renal failure/hyperphosphatemia or as an idiopathic familial condition.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, lobulated, densely calcified periarticular soft-tissue masses, often with fluid-calcium levels, most common around the hip, shoulder, or elbow.

Differential
  • Tumoral calcinosis (renal failure-associated or idiopathic/familial)
Next Step

Correlate with renal function and phosphate levels; nephrology referral if renal failure-associated.

5. Compartment syndrome, CT signs

Concerning

Elevated pressure within a fascial compartment compromising perfusion, primarily a clinical diagnosis, though CT may show supportive findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Muscle swelling/edema within a fascial compartment, sometimes with reduced enhancement suggesting compromised perfusion; CT is not the primary diagnostic tool for this clinical diagnosis.

Differential
  • Compartment syndrome (surgical emergency, diagnosed primarily by clinical exam and compartment pressure measurement)
Next Step

Do not delay fasciotomy for imaging confirmation; compartment syndrome remains a clinical diagnosis.

6. Foreign body, soft tissue CT

Variable

Radiopaque or CT-visible foreign material within the soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete foreign material within the soft tissues, with CT able to detect radiopaque objects (metal, glass, some plastics) that may be missed on plain film.

Differential
  • Retained foreign body (glass, metal, gravel)
  • Organic material (wood, some plastics - may be poorly visualized even on CT)
Next Step

Surgical/wound care referral for removal if symptomatic or at risk of infection/migration.

7. Gas gangrene, CT

Concerning

Clostridial (or other gas-forming organism) soft-tissue infection with characteristic soft-tissue gas.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive soft-tissue and intramuscular gas tracking along fascial planes, often with muscle edema/necrosis.

Differential
  • Gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis, surgical emergency)
  • Other gas-forming necrotizing soft-tissue infection
Next Step

Emergent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics; do not delay treatment for further imaging.

8. Peripheral nerve sheath tumor, CT

Variable

Tumor arising from a peripheral nerve, benign (schwannoma, neurofibroma) or malignant.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform soft-tissue mass in continuity with a peripheral nerve, with MRI generally preferred for detailed characterization over CT.

Differential
  • Schwannoma
  • Neurofibroma (associated with neurofibromatosis if multiple)
  • Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (larger, more heterogeneous, faster growing)
Next Step

MRI for further characterization; surgical referral for symptomatic or growing lesions.

9. Heterotopic ossification, general

Variable

Abnormal bone formation within soft tissue, from trauma, surgery, burns, or neurologic injury (spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury).

Imaging Characteristics

Mature or immature bone formation within the soft tissues, most often periarticular, which can restrict joint motion.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic/post-surgical heterotopic ossification
  • Neurogenic heterotopic ossification (spinal cord injury, TBI)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral if causing significant motion restriction; surgical excision considered once mature.

10. Hematoma, soft tissue CT

Variable

Blood collection within the soft tissues, from trauma, anticoagulation, or post-procedural bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, often high-attenuation collection within the soft tissues, with active extravasation appearing as a focus matching arterial blood pool attenuation.

Differential
  • Traumatic or spontaneous (anticoagulation-related) hematoma
  • Active extravasation (higher-attenuation focus within the hematoma)
Next Step

Correlate with anticoagulation status and hemodynamic stability; IR embolization for active extravasation or an expanding hematoma.

Shoulder & Clavicle

13 findings

1. Clavicle fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of the clavicle, most common at the middle third, usually diagnosed and managed with plain film alone.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the clavicle, most often at the middle third; assess displacement and shortening for management decisions.

Differential
  • Midshaft fracture (most common)
  • Distal clavicle fracture (higher nonunion risk, may need surgery)
  • Medial clavicle fracture (less common, correlate with sternoclavicular joint)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; most midshaft fractures managed conservatively with a sling.

2. Anterior shoulder dislocation

Concerning

Displacement of the humeral head anteriorly relative to the glenoid, the most common direction of shoulder dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Humeral head displaced anteroinferiorly and medially relative to the glenoid, often subcoracoid in position.

Differential
  • Anterior dislocation (>95% of shoulder dislocations)
  • Associated Hill-Sachs lesion or bony Bankart fracture
Next Step

Prompt reduction; post-reduction films to confirm relocation and assess for associated fracture.

3. Posterior shoulder dislocation

Concerning

Displacement of the humeral head posteriorly, uncommon and easily missed, classically associated with seizures or electrical injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Humeral head displaced posteriorly, with the classic 'light bulb sign' (fixed internal rotation) on the AP view and loss of the normal glenohumeral overlap.

Differential
  • Posterior dislocation (classic association: seizure, electrocution)
Next Step

Prompt reduction; often missed on a single AP view alone, so a scapular Y or axillary view is important when suspected.

4. Proximal humerus fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the proximal humerus, common in older adults after a fall, classified by the Neer system.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line(s) through the surgical neck, greater tuberosity, lesser tuberosity, and/or anatomic neck.

Differential
  • 2-part, 3-part, or 4-part fracture (Neer classification)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for surgical planning if comminuted or displaced.

5. AC joint separation

Variable

Injury to the acromioclavicular joint ligaments, graded by the Rockwood classification based on displacement.

Imaging Characteristics

Widening of the AC joint and/or increased coracoclavicular distance, with weighted/stress views sometimes used historically to assess for subtle instability.

Differential
  • Type I-II (mild, usually managed conservatively)
  • Type III-VI (greater displacement, surgical management sometimes considered for higher grades)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; management depends on the Rockwood grade.

6. Scapular fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the scapula, uncommon and associated with high-energy trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the scapular body, neck, spine, or glenoid.

Differential
  • High-energy traumatic fracture (marker of significant force)
Next Step

Trauma survey for associated thoracic injuries; orthopedic referral.

7. Hill-Sachs lesion, plain film

Variable

Compression fracture of the posterolateral humeral head from anterior dislocation, best seen on internal rotation views.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped impaction defect in the posterolateral humeral head.

Differential
  • Hill-Sachs lesion (marker of prior anterior dislocation)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for size assessment if recurrent instability is a concern.

8. Bony Bankart lesion

Variable

Fracture of the anteroinferior glenoid rim associated with anterior shoulder dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture fragment from the anteroinferior glenoid rim, best assessed on axillary or CT imaging.

Differential
  • Bony Bankart lesion (associated with recurrent instability risk)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for glenoid bone loss quantification if recurrent instability is a concern.

9. Rotator cuff calcific tendinitis, plain film

Variable

Calcium hydroxyapatite deposition within the rotator cuff tendons, most often the supraspinatus.

Imaging Characteristics

Amorphous or well-defined calcific density within the expected location of a rotator cuff tendon, typically near the greater tuberosity insertion.

Differential
  • Calcific tendinitis (can be acutely painful during the resorptive phase)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; conservative management (NSAIDs, physical therapy) or barbotage/injection for symptomatic cases.

10. Glenohumeral osteoarthritis, plain film

Benign

Degenerative change of the glenohumeral joint.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Post-traumatic arthritis
  • Rotator cuff arthropathy (superior humeral head migration with chronic massive rotator cuff tear)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management.

11. Sternoclavicular joint dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Dislocation of the sternoclavicular joint, difficult to assess on standard plain film given overlapping structures.

Imaging Characteristics

Asymmetry of the sternoclavicular joints, though a dedicated serendipity view or CT is often needed for confident assessment.

Differential
  • Anterior dislocation (more common)
  • Posterior dislocation (higher risk given proximity to mediastinal structures)
Next Step

CT for definitive characterization given plain film limitations; orthopedic/trauma consultation.

12. Little Leaguer's shoulder

Variable

Stress injury to the proximal humeral physis in young throwing athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Widening and irregularity of the proximal humeral physis, correlating with repetitive throwing stress.

Differential
  • Little Leaguer's shoulder (proximal humeral epiphysiolysis)
Next Step

Activity modification and sports medicine referral.

13. Os acromiale

Benign

Unfused accessory ossification center of the acromion, a normal variant that can be associated with rotator cuff impingement.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent line across the acromion, typically at the junction of the meso- and meta-acromion, distinct from an acute fracture by its smooth, corticated margins.

Differential
  • Os acromiale (normal variant, present in a subset of the population)
  • Acute acromial fracture (less well-corticated margins, trauma history)
Next Step

No action needed for an incidental finding; correlate with impingement symptoms.

Elbow & Forearm

13 findings

1. Anterior fat pad sign (sail sign)

Concerning

Elevation of the anterior fat pad from an elbow joint effusion, a sensitive sign of intra-articular pathology (often occult fracture) even without a visible fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Elevated, triangular anterior fat pad ('sail sign') displaced away from the humerus by joint fluid; a visible posterior fat pad is always abnormal.

Differential
  • Occult fracture (most common cause in adults, radial head; in children, supracondylar)
  • Septic arthritis
  • Other cause of joint effusion
Next Step

Treat as an occult fracture (immobilization, orthopedic follow-up) even if no fracture line is visible, particularly in the appropriate trauma context.

2. Posterior fat pad sign

Concerning

Visualization of the posterior fat pad, always considered abnormal and indicating a joint effusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Visible lucent posterior fat pad displaced away from the distal humerus, normally not visible when the joint is non-effusive.

Differential
  • Elbow joint effusion (occult fracture is the most common underlying cause)
Next Step

Treat as an occult fracture; orthopedic follow-up.

3. Supracondylar fracture, pediatric

Concerning

Fracture of the distal humerus above the condyles, the most common pediatric elbow fracture, with neurovascular injury risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the distal humeral metaphysis, with the anterior humeral line (drawn along the anterior humeral cortex) normally intersecting the middle third of the capitellum; displacement of this relationship suggests fracture.

Differential
  • Supracondylar fracture (Gartland type I-III based on displacement)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic referral given the anterior interosseous nerve and brachial artery injury risk, particularly with displaced fractures.

4. Radial head fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of the radial head, often subtle and inferred from a joint effusion (positive fat pad sign) if a fracture line is not directly visible.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the radial head/neck; a positive fat pad sign without a visible fracture line still suggests an occult radial head fracture in adults.

Differential
  • Mason type I (non-displaced)
  • Mason type II (displaced)
  • Mason type III (comminuted)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; management depends on displacement and comminution.

5. Olecranon fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of the olecranon process, often from a direct fall onto the elbow or triceps avulsion.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the olecranon, assessed for displacement and articular involvement.

Differential
  • Olecranon fracture (displacement guides surgical vs. conservative management)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for displaced fractures typically requiring surgical fixation.

6. Elbow dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Displacement of the radius and ulna relative to the humerus, most often posterior.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal radiocapitellar and ulnohumeral joint alignment, most often with posterior displacement of the forearm relative to the humerus.

Differential
  • Posterior dislocation (most common)
  • Associated fractures (radial head, coronoid - 'terrible triad')
Next Step

Prompt reduction; post-reduction films and assessment for associated fractures and neurovascular injury.

7. Monteggia fracture-dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Ulnar fracture with associated radial head dislocation, easily missed if the radiocapitellar line is not checked.

Imaging Characteristics

Ulnar shaft fracture with radial head dislocation; the radiocapitellar line (drawn through the radial shaft) should intersect the capitellum on all views - failure to do so indicates dislocation.

Differential
  • Monteggia fracture-dislocation
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; always assess the radiocapitellar line when an ulnar fracture is identified.

8. Galeazzi fracture-dislocation

Concerning

Distal radial shaft fracture with associated distal radioulnar joint dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Distal radial shaft fracture with widening or dislocation of the distal radioulnar joint.

Differential
  • Galeazzi fracture-dislocation
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, typically requiring surgical fixation in adults.

9. Nursemaid's elbow (radial head subluxation)

Benign

Subluxation of the radial head from the annular ligament, common in young children after axial traction on the arm, usually a clinical diagnosis without a plain film abnormality.

Imaging Characteristics

Plain film findings are typically normal or show subtle radiocapitellar alignment change; the diagnosis is primarily clinical.

Differential
  • Radial head subluxation (nursemaid's elbow, clinical diagnosis)
Next Step

Closed reduction (supination-flexion or hyperpronation technique); imaging is often unnecessary if the clinical history and exam are classic.

10. Both-bone forearm fracture, plain film

Concerning

Combined radius and ulna shaft fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture of both the radius and ulna shafts, assessed for displacement, angulation, and rotational alignment.

Differential
  • Both-bone forearm fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, typically requiring surgical fixation in adults.

11. Panner disease

Benign

Osteochondrosis of the capitellum in young children, a self-limited condition distinct from osteochondritis dissecans.

Imaging Characteristics

Fragmentation, sclerosis, and irregularity of the capitellar ossification center in a young child (typically 5-10 years).

Differential
  • Panner disease (self-limited, younger age group)
  • Osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum (older adolescents, throwing athletes, higher risk of loose body formation)
Next Step

Activity modification; generally self-limited with good prognosis given the young patient's remodeling potential.

12. Osteochondritis dissecans, capitellum

Variable

Focal subchondral bone injury of the capitellum, most common in adolescent throwing/gymnastics athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal lucency or flattening of the capitellar articular surface, sometimes with a visible loose fragment.

Differential
  • Osteochondritis dissecans (stable vs. unstable fragment affects management)
Next Step

Sports medicine/orthopedic referral; MRI for fragment stability assessment.

13. Elbow osteoarthritis, plain film

Benign

Degenerative change of the elbow joint, less common than at weight-bearing joints, often post-traumatic or occupational.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and loose bodies.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (most common cause at the elbow)
  • Primary osteoarthritis (less common)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management.

Wrist & Hand

15 findings

1. Distal radius fracture, Colles pattern

Variable

Dorsally displaced/angulated distal radius fracture, the most common wrist fracture, classically from a fall on an outstretched hand.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the distal radial metaphysis with dorsal displacement/angulation, sometimes with an associated ulnar styloid fracture.

Differential
  • Colles fracture (dorsal displacement)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; closed reduction and casting for acceptable alignment, surgical fixation for unstable or unacceptable reduction.

2. Distal radius fracture, Smith pattern

Concerning

Volarly displaced/angulated distal radius fracture, less common than Colles, from a fall onto a flexed wrist.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the distal radial metaphysis with volar displacement/angulation (opposite direction from Colles fracture).

Differential
  • Smith fracture (volar displacement, often more unstable than Colles)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; often requires surgical fixation given inherent instability.

3. Scaphoid fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the scaphoid, the most commonly fractured carpal bone, with a notoriously high false-negative rate on initial plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the scaphoid waist (most common location), proximal pole, or distal pole; dedicated scaphoid views improve detection but sensitivity remains imperfect.

Differential
  • Scaphoid waist fracture (higher AVN risk given retrograde blood supply)
  • Occult scaphoid fracture (negative initial plain film with anatomic snuffbox tenderness)
Next Step

Thumb spica immobilization for suspected occult fracture even with a normal initial film; repeat film in 10-14 days or MRI/CT for earlier definitive evaluation given avascular necrosis and nonunion risk if missed.

4. Perilunate dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Severe carpal instability injury with the capitate displaced dorsally relative to the lunate, which remains aligned with the radius.

Imaging Characteristics

On the lateral view, the lunate maintains its normal alignment with the radius, but the capitate is displaced (typically dorsally).

Differential
  • Perilunate dislocation
Next Step

Emergent hand surgery/orthopedic consultation given median nerve injury risk.

5. Lunate dislocation, plain film

Concerning

More severe carpal instability injury than perilunate dislocation, with the lunate itself displaced from its normal articulation with the radius.

Imaging Characteristics

'Spilled teacup' sign on the lateral view, with the lunate tilted and displaced volarly, no longer aligned with the radius.

Differential
  • Lunate dislocation (more severe than perilunate dislocation)
Next Step

Emergent hand surgery/orthopedic consultation given median nerve injury risk and the need for prompt reduction.

6. Scapholunate ligament injury (widening)

Variable

Injury to the scapholunate ligament, the most commonly injured carpal ligament, leading to progressive instability if untreated.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened scapholunate interval (>3 mm, the 'Terry Thomas sign'), best seen on a clenched-fist AP view which stresses the joint.

Differential
  • Scapholunate ligament tear/instability
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; MRI or arthrography for direct ligament assessment.

7. Boxer's fracture (5th metacarpal neck)

Variable

Fracture of the fifth metacarpal neck, classically from a closed-fist punching injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the fifth metacarpal neck with volar angulation of the distal fragment (apex dorsal angulation).

Differential
  • Boxer's fracture (angulation threshold guides management)
Next Step

Orthopedic/hand surgery referral if significantly angulated or rotated; assess for an associated 'fight bite' wound given infection risk.

8. Bennett fracture

Concerning

Intra-articular fracture-dislocation at the base of the thumb metacarpal, with a small volar fragment remaining attached to the ligament while the metacarpal shaft subluxates.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the base of the first metacarpal with a small, ligament-attached volar fragment and proximal/radial subluxation of the metacarpal shaft.

Differential
  • Bennett fracture (intra-articular, inherently unstable)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral, typically requiring surgical fixation given instability.

9. Rolando fracture

Concerning

Comminuted, Y- or T-shaped intra-articular fracture at the base of the thumb metacarpal, a more complex variant of the Bennett fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Comminuted, intra-articular fracture at the thumb metacarpal base with a Y- or T-shaped fracture pattern.

Differential
  • Rolando fracture (comminuted, generally worse prognosis than Bennett fracture)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral for surgical planning given the comminution.

10. Gamekeeper's/skier's thumb (UCL injury)

Concerning

Injury to the ulnar collateral ligament of the thumb MCP joint, from forced radial deviation/abduction of the thumb.

Imaging Characteristics

May show a small avulsion fracture fragment at the base of the proximal phalanx, or plain film may be normal with the diagnosis made clinically/by stress testing or MRI.

Differential
  • UCL sprain/tear (Stener lesion if the torn ligament is displaced superficial to the adductor aponeurosis, preventing healing without surgery)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; MRI or ultrasound to assess for a Stener lesion, which requires surgical repair.

11. Mallet finger

Concerning

Injury to the terminal extensor tendon at the DIP joint, with or without an associated avulsion fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

May show a dorsal avulsion fracture fragment at the base of the distal phalanx; if purely tendinous, plain film may be normal with a clinically evident extensor lag.

Differential
  • Bony mallet finger (avulsion fracture)
  • Tendinous mallet finger (no fracture, clinical diagnosis)
Next Step

Hand surgery/orthopedic referral; splinting in extension for most cases, surgery for large fracture fragments or joint subluxation.

12. Jersey finger (FDP avulsion)

Concerning

Avulsion of the flexor digitorum profundus tendon from its distal phalanx insertion, classically from grabbing a jersey during sports.

Imaging Characteristics

May show a small avulsion fracture fragment at the volar base of the distal phalanx, which can retract proximally into the palm depending on injury severity.

Differential
  • FDP avulsion (jersey finger); fragment retraction level affects surgical urgency
Next Step

Urgent hand surgery referral, particularly with proximal fragment retraction, given the time-sensitive nature of tendon repair.

13. Phalanx fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of a finger phalanx, common from direct trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through a phalanx, assessed for angulation, rotation, and intra-articular extension.

Differential
  • Simple phalanx fracture
Next Step

Hand surgery/orthopedic referral for angulated, rotated, or intra-articular fractures.

14. Hand osteoarthritis (Heberden/Bouchard nodes)

Benign

Degenerative change of the hand interphalangeal joints, extremely common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing and osteophyte formation at the DIP joints (Heberden nodes) and/or PIP joints (Bouchard nodes).

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis (classic distribution)
Next Step

Symptomatic management; correlate with hand function and consider rheumatology referral if the pattern is atypical for primary OA.

15. Thumb carpometacarpal osteoarthritis

Benign

Degenerative change of the first CMC joint, a very common site of hand osteoarthritis, particularly in postmenopausal women.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, subluxation, and osteophyte formation at the first carpometacarpal joint, sometimes with a 'shoulder sign' from radial subluxation of the metacarpal base.

Differential
  • Primary CMC osteoarthritis (very common, classic location)
Next Step

Symptomatic management (splinting, injections); hand surgery referral for advanced/refractory cases.

Pelvis & Hip

11 findings

1. Pelvic ring fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the bony pelvis, with plain film as an initial screening tool though CT is standard for full characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the pubic rami, ilium, or sacrum; assess for a second break to determine ring stability.

Differential
  • Stable, isolated fracture (single pubic ramus)
  • Unstable ring disruption (higher-energy mechanism)
Next Step

CT for full characterization and hemorrhage assessment; orthopedic trauma consultation.

2. Femoral neck fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the femoral neck, common in older adults after a fall, with avascular necrosis risk given the femoral head's blood supply.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the femoral neck, sometimes subtle in non-displaced (Garden I-II) fractures.

Differential
  • Displaced femoral neck fracture (Garden III-IV)
  • Non-displaced/impacted fracture (Garden I-II, may be subtle)
  • Occult fracture (if plain film negative but clinical suspicion high)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for prompt surgical management; MRI or CT if plain film is negative but suspicion remains high.

3. Intertrochanteric fracture

Concerning

Fracture through the intertrochanteric region of the proximal femur, common in older adults, extracapsular (lower AVN risk than femoral neck fractures).

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line extending from the greater to lesser trochanter, often comminuted in osteoporotic bone.

Differential
  • Intertrochanteric fracture (extracapsular, lower AVN risk than femoral neck fracture)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical fixation (typically intramedullary nail or sliding hip screw).

4. Hip dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Dislocation of the femoral head from the acetabulum, most often posterior.

Imaging Characteristics

Femoral head displaced from the acetabular socket; posterior dislocation shows the femoral head appearing smaller and more superolateral than the contralateral side.

Differential
  • Posterior dislocation (most common, often with posterior wall acetabular fracture)
  • Anterior dislocation (less common)
Next Step

Orthopedic emergency for prompt reduction to reduce avascular necrosis risk.

5. Acetabular fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the acetabulum, often from high-energy trauma, with plain film as an initial screening tool.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption of the normal acetabular lines (iliopectineal, ilioischial, anterior/posterior walls) on the AP pelvis view; Judet (oblique) views further characterize the fracture.

Differential
  • Various patterns per the Judet-Letournel classification
Next Step

CT for definitive characterization and surgical planning; orthopedic trauma referral.

6. Slipped capital femoral epiphysis

Concerning

Displacement of the proximal femoral epiphysis relative to the metaphysis through the growth plate, occurring in adolescents, classically obese males.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened, irregular physis with the femoral epiphysis displaced posteromedially relative to the femoral neck; Klein's line (drawn along the superior femoral neck) normally intersects a portion of the epiphysis, failure to do so suggests SCFE.

Differential
  • Slipped capital femoral epiphysis (bilateral in a significant minority, warranting contralateral hip imaging)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic referral; non-weight-bearing status until treated given risk of progression to complete slip and avascular necrosis.

7. Legg-Calve-Perthes disease

Concerning

Idiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral capital epiphysis in children, typically ages 4-8.

Imaging Characteristics

Femoral head fragmentation, flattening, and sclerosis in the earlier stages, progressing through defined radiographic stages (initial, fragmentation, reossification, healed).

Differential
  • Legg-Calve-Perthes disease (idiopathic)
  • Other causes of pediatric AVN (steroid use, sickle cell disease)
Next Step

Pediatric orthopedic referral for staging and management (observation vs. containment bracing/surgery depending on age and severity).

8. Avascular necrosis of the femoral head, plain film

Concerning

Ischemic bone death of the femoral head, with plain film findings often lagging behind MRI by months.

Imaging Characteristics

Subchondral lucency ('crescent sign') and, in later stages, femoral head flattening/collapse; early-stage AVN is often plain-film occult.

Differential
  • Avascular necrosis (steroid use, alcohol use, trauma, sickle cell disease among risk factors)
Next Step

MRI for earlier detection given greater sensitivity than plain film; orthopedic referral.

9. Hip osteoarthritis, plain film

Benign

Degenerative change of the hip joint.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing (often superolateral), osteophyte formation, subchondral sclerosis and cysts.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Secondary osteoarthritis (dysplasia, prior trauma, avascular necrosis, femoroacetabular impingement)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management, including consideration of arthroplasty for advanced disease.

10. Developmental dysplasia of the hip, plain film

Concerning

Abnormal hip joint development, ranging from mild acetabular dysplasia to frank dislocation, screened for in infancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Delayed femoral head ossification, a shallow/sloped acetabulum, and abnormal alignment relative to Hilgenreiner's and Perkin's lines in older infants (ultrasound is preferred before the femoral head ossifies).

Differential
  • Developmental dysplasia of the hip (spectrum from mild dysplasia to complete dislocation)
Next Step

Pediatric orthopedic referral; ultrasound is the preferred initial imaging modality in infants under approximately 4-6 months before adequate femoral head ossification for plain film assessment.

11. Sacroiliitis, plain film

Variable

Inflammation of the sacroiliac joints, from inflammatory or infectious causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, subchondral erosions, or sclerosis at the sacroiliac joints, often bilateral and symmetric in inflammatory spondyloarthropathy.

Differential
  • Ankylosing spondylitis/spondyloarthropathy (bilateral, symmetric)
  • Infectious sacroiliitis (usually unilateral)
  • Degenerative change (asymmetric, older patient)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral for inflammatory pattern; further imaging (MRI) for early/equivocal disease.

Femur & Knee

14 findings

1. Femoral shaft fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the femoral shaft, typically from high-energy trauma in younger patients or a low-energy mechanism in the setting of an underlying lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the femoral diaphysis, assessed for comminution and pattern.

Differential
  • Traumatic femoral shaft fracture
  • Pathologic fracture (underlying lesion, atypical femur fracture from prolonged bisphosphonate use)
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma referral; evaluate for an underlying lesion if the fracture pattern or history is atypical.

2. Atypical femur fracture (bisphosphonate-associated)

Concerning

Transverse or short oblique femoral shaft fracture associated with prolonged bisphosphonate use, occurring with minimal or no trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Transverse or short oblique fracture at the subtrochanteric or diaphyseal femur, with characteristic lateral cortical thickening ('beaking') at the fracture site.

Differential
  • Atypical femur fracture (bisphosphonate-associated)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; assess the contralateral femur for similar changes given bilaterality risk, and reconsider ongoing bisphosphonate therapy.

3. Tibial plateau fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the tibial plateau, often from axial loading with a valgus or varus component.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the tibial plateau, with depression or displacement; CT is often obtained for full characterization and surgical planning.

Differential
  • Lateral plateau fracture (more common)
  • Medial or bicondylar plateau fracture (higher-energy mechanism)
Next Step

CT for surgical planning; orthopedic referral.

4. Segond fracture

Concerning

Small avulsion fracture of the lateral tibial condyle, a marker of associated anterior cruciate ligament injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, curvilinear avulsion fracture fragment from the lateral proximal tibia, just below the joint line.

Differential
  • Segond fracture (highly associated with ACL tear, warrants MRI evaluation)
Next Step

MRI to assess for the associated ACL tear and other internal derangement; orthopedic/sports medicine referral.

5. Patella fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture of the patella, from direct trauma or a forceful quadriceps contraction.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the patella, most often transverse; assess for displacement and comminution.

Differential
  • Transverse patella fracture (most common)
  • Comminuted/stellate fracture
  • Bipartite patella (normal variant, smooth corticated margins, key differential)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for displaced or comminuted fractures.

6. Bipartite patella

Benign

Unfused accessory ossification center of the patella, a common normal variant, typically at the superolateral pole.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, well-corticated separate ossicle at the superolateral pole of the patella, distinguishing it from an acute fracture fragment.

Differential
  • Bipartite patella (normal variant, usually asymptomatic and bilateral)
Next Step

No action needed for an incidental, asymptomatic finding.

7. Patellar dislocation, plain film

Variable

Lateral displacement of the patella from the trochlear groove, often self-reduced by the time of imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

May show the patella frankly dislocated laterally, or (if spontaneously reduced) subtle findings of a prior dislocation such as an osteochondral fracture fragment.

Differential
  • Lateral patellar dislocation
  • Post-reduction changes (osteochondral fragment from the medial patellar facet)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MRI often obtained for soft-tissue (MPFL) and cartilage assessment.

8. Knee osteoarthritis, plain film

Benign

Degenerative change of the knee joint, best assessed on weight-bearing views.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing (often medial compartment predominant), osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis.

Differential
  • Primary osteoarthritis
  • Post-traumatic arthritis
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management, including consideration of arthroplasty for advanced disease.

9. Knee effusion, lipohemarthrosis

Concerning

Fat-fluid level within the knee joint from an intra-articular fracture, indicating marrow fat and blood mixing within the joint.

Imaging Characteristics

Horizontal fat-fluid level on a cross-table lateral view, with fat (less dense) layering above blood, a sensitive indirect sign of an occult intra-articular fracture.

Differential
  • Occult intra-articular fracture (tibial plateau most common)
Next Step

CT for identification of the occult fracture if not visible on plain film; orthopedic referral.

10. Osgood-Schlatter disease

Benign

Traction apophysitis of the tibial tubercle at the patellar tendon insertion, common in adolescent athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fragmentation, irregularity, and soft-tissue swelling at the tibial tubercle apophysis.

Differential
  • Osgood-Schlatter disease (self-limited, activity-related)
Next Step

Activity modification and symptomatic management; generally self-limited with skeletal maturity.

11. Chondrocalcinosis, plain film

Variable

Calcification of cartilage, most often from calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear calcification paralleling the articular cartilage or within the meniscus, most often in the knee.

Differential
  • Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD)
Next Step

Correlate with joint aspiration if acute pseudogout is suspected clinically.

12. Loose body, knee

Variable

Free intra-articular osseous or osteochondral fragment, from trauma, osteochondritis dissecans, or synovial osteochondromatosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete calcified/ossified body within the joint space, mobile and not attached to bone.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic loose body
  • Osteochondritis dissecans fragment
  • Synovial osteochondromatosis (multiple loose bodies)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; arthroscopic removal if causing mechanical symptoms (locking, catching).

13. Osteochondritis dissecans, knee

Variable

Focal subchondral bone injury, most common at the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle, in adolescents and young adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal lucency or flattening of the subchondral bone, sometimes with a visible loose fragment.

Differential
  • Osteochondritis dissecans (stable vs. unstable fragment affects management)
Next Step

Sports medicine/orthopedic referral; MRI for fragment stability assessment.

14. Tibial tubercle avulsion fracture, plain film

Concerning

Avulsion fracture of the tibial tubercle at the patellar tendon insertion, most common in adolescent jumping athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture/avulsion of the tibial tubercle apophysis, graded by displacement severity.

Differential
  • Tibial tubercle avulsion fracture (distinct from the more common, self-limited Osgood-Schlatter apophysitis)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for surgical fixation if significantly displaced.

Tib-Fib & Ankle

11 findings

1. Ankle fracture, Weber classification

Variable

Fracture of the lateral malleolus (fibula), classified by the level relative to the syndesmosis, which correlates with syndesmotic injury likelihood.

Imaging Characteristics

Weber A: fracture below the syndesmosis (syndesmosis typically intact). Weber B: fracture at the level of the syndesmosis (variable syndesmotic involvement). Weber C: fracture above the syndesmosis (syndesmosis typically disrupted).

Differential
  • Weber A fracture (usually stable)
  • Weber B fracture (variable stability)
  • Weber C fracture (usually unstable, syndesmotic injury)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; management depends on stability assessment and syndesmotic involvement.

2. Bimalleolar/trimalleolar ankle fracture

Concerning

Fracture involving two or three of the malleoli (medial, lateral, and posterior), representing a more unstable injury pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the medial and lateral malleoli (bimalleolar), with the posterior malleolus also involved in trimalleolar fractures.

Differential
  • Bimalleolar fracture
  • Trimalleolar fracture (least stable pattern)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, typically requiring surgical fixation given inherent instability.

3. Maisonneuve fracture, plain film

Concerning

Proximal fibular fracture associated with syndesmotic injury, easily missed if only the ankle is imaged without visualizing the proximal fibula.

Imaging Characteristics

Proximal fibular shaft fracture with associated ankle mortise widening; requires dedicated imaging of the proximal fibula/knee region to identify.

Differential
  • Maisonneuve fracture (high index of suspicion needed with ankle instability but a deceptively normal-appearing ankle)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; image the entire fibula (including the knee) when ankle instability is present without an obvious ankle fracture.

4. Syndesmotic injury (widened mortise)

Concerning

Injury to the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis, assessed by mortise widening on plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened tibiofibular clear space and/or medial clear space on the mortise view, indicating syndesmotic ligament disruption.

Differential
  • Syndesmotic (high ankle) sprain/injury
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; may require surgical fixation (syndesmotic screw) depending on stability.

5. Talus fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the talus, uncommon but with significant avascular necrosis risk given its retrograde blood supply.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the talar neck, body, or dome; CT is often obtained for full characterization given the complexity of talar anatomy.

Differential
  • Talar neck fracture (Hawkins classification correlates with AVN risk)
  • Talar dome osteochondral fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; CT for further characterization given significant AVN risk with displaced fractures.

6. Calcaneus fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the calcaneus, often from a fall from height (axial loading), assessed with Bohler's angle.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through the calcaneus, with a reduced Bohler's angle (normally 20-40 degrees) suggesting joint depression/comminution.

Differential
  • Intra-articular calcaneus fracture (involves the subtalar joint)
  • Extra-articular calcaneus fracture
Next Step

CT for full characterization; assess for associated lumbar spine fracture given the shared axial loading mechanism ('lover's fracture' pattern).

7. Ankle dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Displacement of the talus relative to the ankle mortise, almost always associated with a fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal talar alignment within the ankle mortise, typically with an associated malleolar fracture.

Differential
  • Ankle fracture-dislocation
Next Step

Prompt reduction, particularly if there is skin tenting or neurovascular compromise; orthopedic referral.

8. Pediatric ankle physeal fracture (Salter-Harris)

Variable

Fracture involving the growth plate in a child, classified by the Salter-Harris system, with implications for growth disturbance risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture through or adjacent to the physis, classified I-V based on the relationship to the growth plate and metaphysis/epiphysis.

Differential
  • Salter-Harris type I-V (increasing growth disturbance risk with higher types, particularly types III-V)
Next Step

Pediatric orthopedic referral; growth disturbance monitoring, particularly for higher Salter-Harris types.

9. Ankle osteoarthritis, plain film

Benign

Degenerative change of the ankle joint, often post-traumatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, osteophyte formation, and subchondral sclerosis at the tibiotalar joint.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis (most common cause at the ankle)
  • Primary osteoarthritis (less common at this joint)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic management.

10. Os trigonum, plain film

Benign

Accessory ossicle posterior to the talus, a common normal variant.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-corticated accessory ossicle posterior to the talar dome.

Differential
  • Os trigonum (normal variant)
  • Acute posterior talar process fracture (less well-corticated margins)
Next Step

No action needed for an incidental finding; orthopedic referral if causing symptomatic posterior impingement.

11. Achilles tendon calcification/enthesopathy

Variable

Calcification at the Achilles tendon insertion, associated with chronic enthesopathy or, in some cases, seronegative spondyloarthropathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Calcific density at or near the calcaneal insertion of the Achilles tendon, sometimes with an associated Haglund deformity (posterosuperior calcaneal prominence).

Differential
  • Chronic Achilles enthesopathy/insertional tendinopathy
  • Seronegative spondyloarthropathy-associated enthesitis (if multiple sites involved)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; conservative management (physical therapy, activity modification).

Foot

10 findings

1. Lisfranc fracture-dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Injury to the tarsometatarsal joint complex, often subtle and easily missed on plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Malalignment of the tarsometatarsal joints, with a subtle but important sign being loss of alignment between the medial base of the second metatarsal and the medial edge of the middle cuneiform; a small avulsion fragment in this location ('fleck sign') is a classic subtle clue.

Differential
  • Lisfranc fracture-dislocation (high index of suspicion needed given subtle presentation)
Next Step

Weight-bearing views or CT if plain film is equivocal but suspicion remains; orthopedic/podiatric referral given long-term arthritis risk if missed.

2. Jones fracture, plain film

Variable

Fracture at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction of the fifth metatarsal, with higher nonunion risk than a simple avulsion fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture at the junction of the proximal metaphysis and diaphysis of the fifth metatarsal.

Differential
  • Jones fracture (higher nonunion risk, watershed vascular zone)
  • Fifth metatarsal tuberosity avulsion fracture (more proximal, better healing)
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral; Jones fractures often require surgical fixation, particularly in athletes.

3. Fifth metatarsal tuberosity avulsion fracture

Variable

Avulsion fracture at the base of the fifth metatarsal from the peroneus brevis tendon or lateral cord of the plantar fascia, distinct from and with a better prognosis than a Jones fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture fragment at the proximal fifth metatarsal tuberosity, typically transverse or oblique in orientation, distinct from the more distal Jones fracture location.

Differential
  • Fifth metatarsal tuberosity avulsion fracture (good healing potential, usually managed conservatively)
Next Step

Symptomatic management (weight-bearing as tolerated in a supportive shoe/boot); orthopedic referral if displaced.

4. March (stress) fracture, metatarsal

Variable

Overuse stress fracture, most common in the second or third metatarsal shaft, classically in runners or military recruits.

Imaging Characteristics

Often initially normal on plain film; may show periosteal reaction or a subtle lucent line 2-3 weeks after symptom onset as healing begins.

Differential
  • Metatarsal stress fracture (plain film often lags clinical symptoms by weeks)
Next Step

Activity modification; MRI or bone scan for earlier detection if plain film is initially normal but suspicion remains high.

5. Turf toe (first MTP joint sprain)

Variable

Hyperextension injury to the first metatarsophalangeal joint plantar plate/capsule, common in athletes on artificial turf.

Imaging Characteristics

Plain film may be normal or show a small avulsion fragment at the plantar aspect of the proximal phalanx base; soft-tissue swelling may be the only visible finding.

Differential
  • Turf toe (plantar plate injury, clinical diagnosis often with normal or subtle plain film)
Next Step

Sports medicine/podiatric referral; MRI for further characterization if severe or persistent symptoms.

6. Sesamoid fracture/pathology, great toe

Variable

Fracture or chronic stress injury of the sesamoid bones beneath the first metatarsal head.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line or fragmentation of a sesamoid bone; distinguish from a normal bipartite sesamoid (smooth, corticated margins) which is a common variant.

Differential
  • Sesamoid fracture
  • Bipartite sesamoid (normal variant, often bilateral)
  • Sesamoiditis (chronic stress injury without a discrete fracture)
Next Step

Podiatric referral; correlate with symptoms and consider MRI if diagnosis is unclear.

7. Hallux valgus

Benign

Lateral deviation of the great toe at the first MTP joint with medial deviation of the first metatarsal, a common acquired foot deformity.

Imaging Characteristics

Lateral angulation of the proximal phalanx relative to the first metatarsal, with an increased intermetatarsal angle between the first and second metatarsals.

Differential
  • Hallux valgus (bunion deformity)
Next Step

Podiatric/orthopedic referral for symptomatic management (conservative or surgical correction based on severity).

8. Calcaneal spur

Benign

Bony spur at the plantar calcaneus, associated with (but not diagnostic of) plantar fasciitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Bony spur projecting from the plantar aspect of the calcaneus at the plantar fascia origin.

Differential
  • Plantar calcaneal spur (common, often incidental, and not always symptomatic even when plantar fasciitis is present)
Next Step

Correlate with heel pain symptoms; conservative management (stretching, orthotics) for symptomatic plantar fasciitis regardless of spur presence.

9. Accessory navicular, plain film

Benign

Accessory ossicle at the medial navicular, a common normal variant that can become symptomatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-corticated accessory ossicle or bony prominence at the medial aspect of the navicular.

Differential
  • Accessory navicular (type I, II, or III, normal variant)
Next Step

No action needed if asymptomatic; orthopedic/podiatric referral if causing symptomatic medial midfoot pain.

10. Charcot foot, plain film

Concerning

Neuropathic joint destruction, most often from diabetic peripheral neuropathy, which can closely mimic osteomyelitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint destruction, fragmentation, subluxation, and malalignment (often midfoot), in a patient with known peripheral neuropathy.

Differential
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy (acute inflammatory phase can closely mimic osteomyelitis)
  • Osteomyelitis (key differential, particularly with an overlying ulcer)
Next Step

Endocrine/podiatric/orthopedic referral; MRI or nuclear medicine studies may help distinguish from osteomyelitis when overlap is present.

Bone Tumors & Lesions

10 findings

1. Osteochondroma, plain film

Benign

Most common benign bone tumor, a cartilage-capped bony projection continuous with the underlying bone.

Imaging Characteristics

Bony outgrowth with cortical and medullary continuity with the parent bone, directed away from the nearest joint.

Differential
  • Osteochondroma (benign)
  • Multiple hereditary exostoses (if numerous)
Next Step

No treatment needed for an asymptomatic, classic lesion; orthopedic referral if growing after skeletal maturity or symptomatic.

2. Enchondroma, plain film

Benign

Common benign intramedullary cartilage tumor, most often in the hand phalanges or long bones.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined lytic lesion with characteristic 'rings and arcs' or popcorn-like chondroid matrix calcification.

Differential
  • Enchondroma (benign)
  • Low-grade chondrosarcoma (larger, endosteal scalloping raises concern, particularly in long bones)
Next Step

No treatment needed for a classic, asymptomatic hand enchondroma; further workup for lesions with aggressive features or in long bones.

3. Non-ossifying fibroma, plain film

Benign

Common benign fibrous bone lesion in children and adolescents, often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, eccentric, lytic lesion with a sclerotic, scalloped border, most often in the metaphysis of long bones.

Differential
  • Non-ossifying fibroma (benign, common incidental pediatric finding)
Next Step

No treatment needed; most resolve spontaneously with growth.

4. Simple (unicameral) bone cyst, plain film

Variable

Benign, fluid-filled bone lesion, most common in the proximal humerus or femur of children.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, central, lytic lesion with a thin cortex, sometimes with a 'fallen fragment sign' if pathologically fractured.

Differential
  • Simple bone cyst (benign)
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst (more expansile)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, particularly if there is pathologic fracture risk.

5. Osteoid osteoma, plain film

Benign

Benign bone-forming tumor, classically causing pain worse at night and relieved by NSAIDs.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round lucent nidus with surrounding dense reactive sclerosis; CT is more sensitive for nidus detection.

Differential
  • Osteoid osteoma (classic NSAID-responsive night pain)
  • Osteoblastoma (larger version)
  • Stress fracture (can mimic the reactive sclerosis)
Next Step

CT for definitive nidus characterization; orthopedic referral for treatment (often radiofrequency ablation).

6. Bone metastasis, plain film

Concerning

Secondary malignant bone lesion, the most common malignant bone tumor overall in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Lytic, sclerotic, or mixed lesion, which can be solitary or multiple.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common, especially breast, lung, prostate, renal, thyroid primaries)
  • Multiple myeloma
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging; correlate with known malignancy history.

7. Osteosarcoma, plain film

Concerning

Most common primary malignant bone tumor, most often in adolescents at the metaphysis of long bones.

Imaging Characteristics

Aggressive, mixed lytic-sclerotic lesion with cortical destruction, periosteal reaction (Codman triangle or 'sunburst' pattern), and often a soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Osteosarcoma (classic in adolescent long bone metaphysis)
  • Ewing sarcoma (key differential)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; MRI and staging workup.

8. Ewing sarcoma, plain film

Concerning

Aggressive malignant bone tumor, most common in children and adolescents, classically diaphyseal.

Imaging Characteristics

Permeative, moth-eaten lytic lesion with an aggressive periosteal reaction (lamellated 'onion skin' pattern).

Differential
  • Ewing sarcoma (classic diaphyseal location)
  • Osteomyelitis (can closely mimic)
  • Osteosarcoma
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; MRI and staging workup.

9. Fibrous dysplasia, plain film

Variable

Benign fibro-osseous developmental lesion where normal bone is replaced by fibrous tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Expansile lesion with a characteristic 'ground-glass' matrix, well-defined margins, and a thin, intact cortex.

Differential
  • Fibrous dysplasia (monostotic or polyostotic)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for monitoring; usually managed conservatively unless causing significant deformity or fracture risk.

10. Periosteal reaction patterns

Concerning

Bone's response to an underlying process, with the pattern helping distinguish benign from aggressive causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid/benign patterns include thick, uniform periosteal new bone. Aggressive patterns include lamellated ('onion skin'), spiculated ('sunburst'), and interrupted (Codman triangle) periosteal reactions.

Differential
  • Benign process (healing fracture, chronic stress) - solid pattern
  • Aggressive process (malignancy, infection) - lamellated, spiculated, or interrupted pattern
Next Step

Correlate the pattern with the overall lesion and clinical context; aggressive patterns warrant prompt further workup.

Infection

5 findings

1. Osteomyelitis, plain film

Concerning

Bone infection, with plain film often lagging clinical symptoms by 1-2 weeks before visible changes appear.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical destruction, periosteal reaction, and adjacent soft-tissue swelling; early osteomyelitis may show only soft-tissue swelling with normal bone.

Differential
  • Acute osteomyelitis (plain film often initially normal or subtle)
  • Chronic osteomyelitis (sequestrum/involucrum pattern)
Next Step

MRI for earlier and more sensitive detection; orthopedic/infectious disease referral.

2. Septic arthritis, plain film

Concerning

Joint infection, a surgical emergency, with plain film primarily showing joint effusion and, in later stages, joint space narrowing/destruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space widening (effusion) early, progressing to joint space narrowing and periarticular osteoporosis/erosion in more advanced or delayed presentations.

Differential
  • Septic arthritis (surgical emergency requiring prompt drainage)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic consultation for joint aspiration/washout; do not delay treatment for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

3. Diabetic foot osteomyelitis, plain film

Concerning

Bone infection in the diabetic foot, often from a contiguous overlying ulcer.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical destruction and periosteal reaction adjacent to a soft-tissue ulcer, though plain film sensitivity is limited, particularly early in the disease course.

Differential
  • Diabetic foot osteomyelitis
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy (key differential, particularly in the acute phase)
Next Step

MRI for more sensitive detection and to help distinguish from Charcot changes; podiatric/infectious disease referral.

4. Soft tissue gas, plain film

Concerning

Gas within the soft tissues, a concerning sign for gas-forming infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent streaks or bubbles within the soft tissues, tracking along fascial planes in more extensive infection.

Differential
  • Necrotizing soft-tissue infection/gas gangrene (surgical emergency)
  • Recent surgery/trauma with introduced air (benign in the appropriate context)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation if infection is suspected; correlate with recent surgical/traumatic history.

5. Tuberculous osteomyelitis/arthritis

Concerning

Skeletal tuberculosis, with a more indolent presentation than pyogenic infection, most often affecting the spine (see also the neuro atlas) but also peripheral joints.

Imaging Characteristics

Slowly progressive joint space narrowing and periarticular osteopenia/erosion, often with a large associated soft-tissue abscess out of proportion to the bony changes.

Differential
  • Tuberculous arthritis/osteomyelitis (endemic area exposure history important)
  • Pyogenic osteomyelitis/septic arthritis (more acute presentation)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation.

Metabolic & Systemic Bone Disease

8 findings

1. Osteoporosis, plain film

Variable

Reduced bone mineral density, suggested but not formally diagnosed by plain film appearance.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced bone density with thinned cortices and accentuated trabecular pattern; plain film is insensitive for detecting mild-to-moderate osteoporosis.

Differential
  • Osteoporosis (formal diagnosis requires DEXA)
  • Osteopenia
Next Step

DEXA scan for formal bone density assessment; osteoporosis workup and treatment as indicated.

2. Insufficiency fracture pattern

Variable

Fracture occurring in weakened (osteoporotic or otherwise abnormal) bone from normal or minimal stress.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line in a location and pattern atypical for the reported mechanism, in a patient with risk factors for bone fragility.

Differential
  • Osteoporotic insufficiency fracture
  • Other metabolic bone disease-related fragility fracture
Next Step

DEXA scan and osteoporosis workup; orthopedic referral for fracture management.

3. Paget disease, plain film

Variable

Chronic disorder of bone remodeling, causing enlarged, disorganized, and structurally weakened bone.

Imaging Characteristics

Bone expansion with thickened, coarsened trabeculae and cortex; can be lytic (early/active, 'blade of grass' or 'flame-shaped' advancing lucent margin), sclerotic, or mixed.

Differential
  • Paget disease (correlate with elevated alkaline phosphatase)
Next Step

Correlate with alkaline phosphatase; orthopedic referral for monitoring and treatment if active/symptomatic.

4. Hyperparathyroidism, plain film changes

Variable

Skeletal manifestations of excess parathyroid hormone.

Imaging Characteristics

Subperiosteal bone resorption (classically at the radial aspect of the middle phalanges), 'salt and pepper' skull, and in severe cases, lytic 'brown tumors.'

Differential
  • Primary or secondary hyperparathyroidism
Next Step

Endocrine referral; correlate with calcium and PTH levels.

5. Rickets, plain film

Concerning

Impaired bone mineralization in children from vitamin D deficiency or other metabolic causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened, irregular, cupped, and frayed physes, most notable at the wrists and knees, with generalized osteopenia and bowing deformities.

Differential
  • Nutritional vitamin D deficiency rickets (most common)
  • Renal osteodystrophy/renal rickets
  • Hypophosphatemic rickets
Next Step

Pediatric endocrine referral; correlate with vitamin D, calcium, and phosphate levels.

6. Osteomalacia, plain film (Looser zones)

Concerning

Adult counterpart of rickets, from impaired bone mineralization.

Imaging Characteristics

Looser zones (pseudofractures): radiolucent bands perpendicular to the cortex, often bilateral and symmetric, classically at the femoral neck, pubic rami, and scapula.

Differential
  • Osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency, renal osteodystrophy, hypophosphatemic disorders)
Next Step

Endocrine referral; correlate with vitamin D, calcium, phosphate, and PTH levels.

7. Gout, plain film

Concerning

Crystal deposition arthropathy from monosodium urate, with characteristic chronic changes on plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, 'punched-out' erosions with sclerotic, overhanging margins, often para-articular rather than purely intra-articular, with preserved joint space until late in the disease and adjacent soft-tissue tophi.

Differential
  • Chronic tophaceous gout
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with serum uric acid and joint aspiration during acute flares.

8. Rheumatoid arthritis, plain film

Concerning

Chronic inflammatory arthropathy with characteristic symmetric, erosive changes, classically in the hands and feet.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, marginal erosions, periarticular osteopenia, and joint space narrowing, classically at the MCP and PIP joints of the hands and MTP joints of the feet, with relative sparing of the DIP joints.

Differential
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (classic distribution)
  • Other inflammatory arthropathies (psoriatic arthritis, and others)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral for diagnosis confirmation and disease-modifying treatment.

Soft Tissue & Miscellaneous

10 findings

1. Soft tissue swelling, nonspecific

Variable

Localized or diffuse soft-tissue swelling identified on plain film, a nonspecific finding requiring clinical correlation.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased soft-tissue density/thickness compared to the expected normal contour, without further specificity as to cause.

Differential
  • Trauma-related soft-tissue injury
  • Infection (cellulitis, abscess)
  • Inflammatory process (gout flare, and others)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam; further imaging (ultrasound, MRI) if a specific diagnosis needs to be established.

2. Foreign body, soft tissue plain film

Variable

Radiopaque foreign material within the soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete radiopaque object within the soft tissues; glass is often visible (contrary to common belief) while wood and some plastics are frequently radiolucent and not well seen.

Differential
  • Retained radiopaque foreign body (glass, metal, gravel)
  • Radiolucent foreign body (wood, some plastics - may require ultrasound or CT for detection)
Next Step

Ultrasound or CT if a radiolucent foreign body (wood, plastic) is suspected despite a negative plain film.

3. Myositis ossificans, plain film

Benign

Heterotopic bone formation within muscle following trauma, which can mimic a malignant tumor early in its course.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral ('zonal') pattern of calcification/ossification with a lucent center, best appreciated after several weeks of maturation.

Differential
  • Myositis ossificans (peripheral zonal maturation pattern is characteristic)
  • Extraskeletal osteosarcoma (early lesions can mimic before the characteristic zonal pattern develops)
Next Step

Serial imaging over several weeks to confirm the characteristic maturation pattern if the lesion is imaged early/atypically.

4. Vascular calcification, extremity

Benign

Calcification of peripheral arteries, common with age, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear, tram-track calcification following the course of peripheral arteries.

Differential
  • Atherosclerotic calcification
  • Medial arterial calcification (Monckeberg sclerosis, associated with diabetes/CKD)
Next Step

Correlate with vascular risk factors and peripheral perfusion status if clinically relevant.

5. Heterotopic ossification, plain film

Variable

Abnormal bone formation within soft tissue, from trauma, surgery, burns, or neurologic injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Mature or immature bone formation within the soft tissues, most often periarticular.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic/post-surgical heterotopic ossification
  • Neurogenic heterotopic ossification (spinal cord injury, traumatic brain injury)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral if causing significant motion restriction.

6. Growth plate assessment / bone age

Variable

Assessment of skeletal maturity, most often using a hand/wrist plain film compared to standardized reference atlases.

Imaging Characteristics

Comparison of ossification centers and physeal appearance to age-based reference standards (Greulich and Pyle atlas).

Differential
  • Normal skeletal maturation for age
  • Delayed or advanced bone age (endocrine or constitutional causes)
Next Step

Pediatric endocrine referral if bone age is significantly discordant with chronological age.

7. Chondrocalcinosis, hand/wrist (CPPD)

Variable

Calcium pyrophosphate deposition within cartilage, common at the triangular fibrocartilage complex of the wrist.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear calcification within the triangular fibrocartilage complex and/or other hand/wrist cartilage.

Differential
  • Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease
Next Step

Correlate with joint symptoms; joint aspiration during acute flares (pseudogout) for definitive crystal identification.

8. Erosive osteoarthritis, hand

Variable

Inflammatory subset of hand osteoarthritis with characteristic central erosions, distinct from typical nodal OA.

Imaging Characteristics

Central ('gull-wing') erosions at the DIP and PIP joints, distinct from the peripheral osteophyte-predominant pattern of typical nodal osteoarthritis.

Differential
  • Erosive osteoarthritis (more inflammatory subset)
  • Psoriatic arthritis (key differential, correlate with skin findings)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral, particularly to distinguish from psoriatic arthritis given differing treatment implications.

9. Psoriatic arthritis, plain film

Concerning

Inflammatory arthropathy associated with psoriasis, with characteristic 'pencil-in-cup' deformity and other distinctive features.

Imaging Characteristics

'Pencil-in-cup' deformity (central joint erosion with a cupped appearance), asymmetric distribution, DIP joint involvement (in contrast to rheumatoid arthritis), and periostitis/new bone formation.

Differential
  • Psoriatic arthritis (correlate with psoriasis skin findings)
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (typically spares DIP joints, more symmetric)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with skin and nail findings.

10. Ankylosing spondylitis, peripheral joint changes

Concerning

Peripheral joint and enthesis involvement in ankylosing spondylitis, in addition to the characteristic axial (spine/SI joint) disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Enthesitis (inflammation at tendon/ligament insertions) with associated erosion and new bone formation, most often at the calcaneus (Achilles and plantar fascia insertions).

Differential
  • Ankylosing spondylitis-associated enthesitis/peripheral arthritis
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with axial symptoms and HLA-B27 status.

Shoulder - Rotator Cuff & Labrum

12 findings

1. Supraspinatus tendinopathy

Variable

Degenerative or inflammatory change of the supraspinatus tendon without a full-thickness tear.

Imaging Characteristics

Intrasubstance increased T2 signal within the tendon, without a discrete fluid-signal defect extending through its full thickness.

Differential
  • Tendinopathy (degenerative, impingement-related)
  • Partial-thickness tear (higher-grade signal change, key differential)
Next Step

Conservative management (physical therapy, subacromial injection); orthopedic/sports medicine referral if refractory.

2. Partial-thickness rotator cuff tear

Concerning

Incomplete tear of a rotator cuff tendon, articular-sided, bursal-sided, or intrasubstance.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal fluid-signal defect involving part of the tendon thickness, most often at the articular surface of the supraspinatus (critical zone).

Differential
  • Articular-sided partial tear (most common)
  • Bursal-sided partial tear
  • Intrasubstance partial tear
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; conservative management vs. surgical repair based on size and symptoms.

3. Full-thickness rotator cuff tear

Concerning

Complete tear of a rotator cuff tendon extending through its full thickness, most often the supraspinatus.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid-signal defect extending completely through the tendon, with tendon retraction assessed and graded (Patte classification) as this affects reparability.

Differential
  • Full-thickness supraspinatus tear (most common)
  • Full-thickness tear involving multiple tendons
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral for surgical repair consideration; retraction degree and muscle atrophy affect reparability.

4. Massive rotator cuff tear with fatty atrophy

Concerning

Chronic, large rotator cuff tear with secondary muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration, affecting surgical reparability and outcomes.

Imaging Characteristics

Full-thickness tear involving two or more tendons with significant retraction, accompanied by muscle belly atrophy and fatty infiltration (Goutallier classification) on oblique sagittal imaging.

Differential
  • Chronic massive rotator cuff tear (higher-grade fatty infiltration associated with worse surgical outcomes)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; may be a candidate for reverse total shoulder arthroplasty rather than repair given chronicity and atrophy.

5. Rotator cuff tendinopathy with impingement

Variable

Rotator cuff tendon changes associated with subacromial impingement, from anatomic or degenerative narrowing of the subacromial space.

Imaging Characteristics

Supraspinatus tendinopathy or tearing with associated subacromial-subdeltoid bursitis and, often, a low-lying or hooked acromion contributing to mechanical impingement.

Differential
  • Subacromial impingement syndrome
Next Step

Conservative management or subacromial decompression for refractory cases.

6. Superior labral anterior-posterior (SLAP) tear

Concerning

Tear of the superior labrum extending anterior to posterior, at the biceps anchor, common in overhead athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal within the superior labrum extending into the biceps anchor, best assessed with MR arthrography for improved sensitivity.

Differential
  • SLAP tear (multiple subtypes based on morphology and biceps anchor involvement)
  • Sublabral recess/foramen (normal variant, smooth margins, key differential)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MR arthrography improves diagnostic accuracy over conventional MRI.

7. Bankart lesion (labral)

Concerning

Tear of the anteroinferior labrum from anterior shoulder dislocation, a major contributor to recurrent instability.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption/detachment of the anteroinferior labrum from the glenoid rim, best assessed with MR arthrography.

Differential
  • Bankart lesion (soft-tissue)
  • Bony Bankart lesion (associated glenoid rim fracture)
  • Perthes lesion / ALPSA lesion (variant patterns of anteroinferior labral injury)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral for instability management.

8. Posterior labral tear

Concerning

Tear of the posterior labrum, less common than anterior tears, associated with posterior instability.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption of the posterior labrum, sometimes with an associated posterior humeral head impaction fracture (reverse Hill-Sachs).

Differential
  • Posterior labral tear/instability
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral.

9. Biceps tendon pathology (long head), MRI

Variable

Abnormality of the long head of the biceps tendon within the bicipital groove, ranging from tendinopathy to tear/dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendinopathy shows increased signal and tendon thickening; tears show discontinuity or absence of the tendon within the groove; medial dislocation occurs with subscapularis tendon tears.

Differential
  • Biceps tendinopathy
  • Partial or full-thickness biceps tear
  • Biceps tendon dislocation (associated with subscapularis tear)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; often assessed alongside rotator cuff pathology given frequent co-occurrence.

10. Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), MRI

Variable

Inflammatory fibrosis of the glenohumeral joint capsule causing progressive stiffness, a largely clinical diagnosis with supportive MRI findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening of the joint capsule and coracohumeral ligament, particularly in the rotator interval, with associated inflammatory signal change.

Differential
  • Adhesive capsulitis (primary/idiopathic or secondary to another shoulder condition)
Next Step

Physical therapy and corticosteroid injection; orthopedic referral for refractory cases (manipulation or capsular release).

11. Glenohumeral instability, MR arthrography findings

Concerning

Constellation of findings supporting glenohumeral instability, best assessed with MR arthrography.

Imaging Characteristics

Combination of labral tear, capsular laxity/redundancy, and associated bony injury (Hill-Sachs, bony Bankart) supporting a diagnosis of instability.

Differential
  • Anterior instability (most common)
  • Posterior or multidirectional instability (less common patterns)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral for stabilization procedure consideration.

12. Suprascapular nerve entrapment, shoulder MRI

Variable

Compression of the suprascapular nerve, often at the suprascapular or spinoglenoid notch, causing infraspinatus (and sometimes supraspinatus) weakness.

Imaging Characteristics

Denervation edema/atrophy of the supraspinatus and/or infraspinatus muscles, sometimes with an identifiable compressive lesion (paralabral cyst from an associated labral tear) at the notch.

Differential
  • Suprascapular nerve entrapment (often from a paralabral cyst secondary to a labral tear)
  • Idiopathic/overuse-related entrapment (overhead athletes)
Next Step

Neurology/orthopedic referral; address any underlying compressive lesion (cyst decompression) if identified.

Elbow - Ligament & Tendon

9 findings

1. Ulnar collateral ligament injury, elbow

Concerning

Injury to the ulnar (medial) collateral ligament, classically in throwing athletes from repetitive valgus stress.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal, thickening, or discontinuity of the ulnar collateral ligament, most often at its humeral or ulnar attachment, sometimes with an associated 'T-sign' from contrast/fluid extending beneath the ligament on arthrography.

Differential
  • UCL sprain (partial)
  • UCL tear (complete, may require reconstruction in throwing athletes)
Next Step

Sports medicine/orthopedic referral; UCL reconstruction ('Tommy John surgery') considered for complete tears in throwing athletes.

2. Lateral collateral ligament complex injury, elbow

Concerning

Injury to the lateral (radial) collateral ligament complex, associated with posterolateral rotatory instability.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity of the lateral ulnar collateral ligament, often associated with elbow dislocation history.

Differential
  • Lateral collateral ligament injury/posterolateral rotatory instability
Next Step

Orthopedic referral, particularly with recurrent instability symptoms.

3. Distal biceps tendon tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the distal biceps tendon at its radial tuberosity insertion, most often in middle-aged men, from a forceful eccentric contraction.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the distal biceps tendon with proximal retraction, best assessed on the FABS (flexion, abduction, supination) view or sagittal/axial sequences.

Differential
  • Complete distal biceps tear (typically requires surgical repair)
  • Partial distal biceps tear
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic/sports medicine referral given time-sensitive surgical repair window for complete tears.

4. Triceps tendon tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the triceps tendon at its olecranon insertion, uncommon but likely underdiagnosed.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the triceps tendon at or near its olecranon insertion, with variable retraction.

Differential
  • Complete triceps tear
  • Partial triceps tear
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; complete tears typically require surgical repair.

5. Common extensor tendinopathy (lateral epicondylitis), MRI

Variable

Degenerative change of the common extensor tendon origin at the lateral epicondyle, 'tennis elbow.'

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal and thickening of the common extensor tendon origin, particularly the extensor carpi radialis brevis component, without a discrete tear in milder cases.

Differential
  • Lateral epicondylitis/tendinopathy (degenerative, despite the '-itis' name true inflammation is often minimal)
  • Partial or full-thickness tear (more severe signal change)
Next Step

Conservative management (physical therapy, activity modification); orthopedic/sports medicine referral for refractory cases.

6. Common flexor tendinopathy (medial epicondylitis), MRI

Variable

Degenerative change of the common flexor tendon origin at the medial epicondyle, 'golfer's elbow.'

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal and thickening of the common flexor tendon origin at the medial epicondyle.

Differential
  • Medial epicondylitis/tendinopathy
Next Step

Conservative management; orthopedic/sports medicine referral for refractory cases.

7. Elbow osteochondritis dissecans, MRI

Variable

Focal subchondral bone and cartilage injury of the capitellum, with MRI assessing fragment stability.

Imaging Characteristics

Subchondral signal abnormality at the capitellum, with fragment stability assessed by the presence/absence of surrounding fluid signal undermining the fragment.

Differential
  • Stable OCD lesion (intact overlying cartilage, no undermining fluid)
  • Unstable OCD lesion (fluid undermining the fragment, higher risk of loose body formation)
Next Step

Sports medicine/orthopedic referral; management depends on fragment stability.

8. Elbow joint effusion / synovitis, MRI

Variable

Fluid distension of the elbow joint capsule, from a range of traumatic, infectious, or inflammatory causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid signal distending the anterior and/or posterior joint recesses, with synovial thickening/enhancement suggesting a more inflammatory or infectious process.

Differential
  • Traumatic effusion (occult fracture)
  • Inflammatory synovitis
  • Septic arthritis (correlate with clinical infection signs)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; aspiration if infection is a concern.

9. Ulnar nerve subluxation/instability, elbow MRI

Variable

Abnormal movement of the ulnar nerve out of the cubital tunnel with elbow flexion, which can contribute to ulnar neuropathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Ulnar nerve positioned anterior to the medial epicondyle on flexion imaging, rather than remaining within the cubital tunnel.

Differential
  • Ulnar nerve subluxation/dislocation
Next Step

Correlate with clinical symptoms of ulnar neuropathy; orthopedic/neurology referral if symptomatic.

10 findings

1. Triangular fibrocartilage complex (TFCC) tear

Concerning

Tear of the TFCC, the primary stabilizer of the distal radioulnar joint and ulnar-sided wrist structure, from trauma or degenerative change.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity within the TFCC, classified as traumatic (Palmer class 1) or degenerative (Palmer class 2), with MR arthrography improving sensitivity.

Differential
  • Traumatic TFCC tear (typically in younger patients, acute injury)
  • Degenerative TFCC tear (older patients, associated with ulnar impaction)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; management depends on tear location, chronicity, and ulnar variance.

2. Scapholunate ligament tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the scapholunate interosseous ligament, the most commonly injured carpal ligament.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity or increased signal within the scapholunate ligament, with associated widening of the scapholunate interval in more severe/chronic injuries.

Differential
  • Scapholunate ligament tear (acute or chronic)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; MR arthrography improves diagnostic sensitivity over conventional MRI.

3. Lunotriquetral ligament tear, MRI

Variable

Tear of the lunotriquetral interosseous ligament, less common than scapholunate injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity or increased signal within the lunotriquetral ligament.

Differential
  • Lunotriquetral ligament tear
Next Step

Hand surgery referral.

4. De Quervain tenosynovitis, MRI

Variable

Inflammation of the first dorsal compartment tendons (abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis), though ultrasound is more commonly used clinically.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid signal surrounding the first dorsal compartment tendons with tendon sheath thickening, at the radial styloid.

Differential
  • De Quervain tenosynovitis
Next Step

Conservative management (splinting, NSAIDs, injection); hand surgery referral for refractory cases.

5. Flexor tendon injury, hand MRI

Concerning

Tear or laceration of a flexor tendon, with MRI used for complex or chronic cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of a flexor tendon with variable retraction, best characterized alongside clinical exam.

Differential
  • Complete flexor tendon laceration/rupture
  • Partial tendon injury
Next Step

Urgent hand surgery referral given the time-sensitive nature of tendon repair.

6. Extensor tendon injury, hand MRI

Concerning

Tear or laceration of an extensor tendon.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of an extensor tendon with variable retraction.

Differential
  • Complete extensor tendon laceration/rupture
  • Partial tendon injury
Next Step

Hand surgery referral.

7. Carpal tunnel syndrome, MRI

Variable

Median nerve compression at the wrist, primarily a clinical/electrodiagnostic diagnosis, with MRI reserved for atypical or refractory cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Median nerve flattening at the level of the hamate with proximal swelling, and sometimes an identifiable underlying cause (ganglion cyst, tenosynovitis, mass).

Differential
  • Idiopathic carpal tunnel syndrome (most common)
  • Secondary carpal tunnel syndrome (mass, tenosynovitis, anomalous muscle)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam and electrodiagnostic studies; hand surgery referral for refractory or secondary cases.

8. Ganglion cyst, wrist MRI

Benign

Common benign cystic lesion arising from a joint or tendon sheath, most often dorsal wrist at the scapholunate ligament.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense cystic lesion, often with a visible stalk connecting to the adjacent joint or tendon sheath.

Differential
  • Ganglion cyst (benign, extremely common)
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic; hand surgery referral for aspiration or excision if symptomatic.

9. Kienbock disease (lunate avascular necrosis)

Concerning

Avascular necrosis of the lunate, with MRI as the most sensitive modality for early detection.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely decreased T1 signal within the lunate in early disease, progressing to collapse and fragmentation in advanced stages (Lichtman staging).

Differential
  • Kienbock disease (idiopathic avascular necrosis of the lunate)
Next Step

Hand surgery referral; treatment depends on Lichtman stage, ranging from unloading procedures to salvage arthroplasty.

10. Trigger finger, MRI/tenosynovitis

Variable

Stenosing tenosynovitis of a flexor tendon at the A1 pulley, causing catching/locking, primarily a clinical diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening of the flexor tendon and/or A1 pulley with surrounding fluid, though ultrasound is more commonly used for this diagnosis clinically.

Differential
  • Trigger finger (stenosing flexor tenosynovitis)
Next Step

Conservative management (splinting, injection); hand surgery referral for refractory cases (A1 pulley release).

Hip - Labrum & Impingement

8 findings

1. Acetabular labral tear

Concerning

Tear of the acetabular labrum, a common cause of hip pain in young adults, often associated with femoroacetabular impingement or dysplasia.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity within the labrum, best assessed with MR arthrography, most often at the anterosuperior labrum.

Differential
  • Labral tear associated with femoroacetabular impingement (cam or pincer type)
  • Labral tear associated with hip dysplasia
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MR arthrography improves diagnostic sensitivity.

2. Femoroacetabular impingement, MRI morphology

Variable

Abnormal hip morphology causing abutment between the femur and acetabulum, assessed on MRI alongside associated labral/cartilage injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Cam morphology: aspherical femoral head-neck junction with a reduced offset (elevated alpha angle). Pincer morphology: acetabular overcoverage, often with an associated labral tear at the site of abutment.

Differential
  • Cam-type impingement
  • Pincer-type impingement
  • Mixed pattern
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; correlate with associated labral and cartilage injury.

3. Hip joint effusion, MRI

Variable

Fluid distension of the hip joint capsule, from a range of causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid signal distending the hip joint capsule, with synovial enhancement/thickening suggesting a more inflammatory or infectious process.

Differential
  • Traumatic effusion
  • Transient synovitis (pediatric)
  • Septic arthritis (correlate with clinical infection signs)
  • Inflammatory arthropathy
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; aspiration if infection is a concern, particularly in a child with fever.

4. Gluteus medius/minimus tendon tear ('rotator cuff of the hip')

Concerning

Tear of the gluteus medius and/or minimus tendon at its greater trochanteric insertion, an important cause of lateral hip pain, particularly in older women.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal, thinning, or discontinuity of the gluteus medius/minimus tendon at the greater trochanter, sometimes with adjacent trochanteric bursitis.

Differential
  • Gluteus medius/minimus tendinopathy or tear (sometimes termed the 'rotator cuff of the hip')
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; conservative management vs. surgical repair based on tear size and symptoms.

5. Hip adductor strain/tear, MRI

Variable

Injury to the adductor muscle group, common in athletes, ranging from strain to complete tear.

Imaging Characteristics

Edema and/or discontinuity within the adductor muscle-tendon unit, most often the adductor longus, graded by severity (strain grade I-III).

Differential
  • Adductor strain (grade I-III)
  • Complete adductor tear (avulsion)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; management depends on injury grade.

6. Athletic pubalgia ('sports hernia'), MRI

Variable

Chronic groin pain from injury to the pubic symphysis-related musculotendinous structures, without a true hernia.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal change and/or subtle disruption at the rectus abdominis-adductor longus aponeurosis at the pubic symphysis, sometimes with secondary cleft sign or pubic bone marrow edema.

Differential
  • Athletic pubalgia/sports hernia complex
  • Osteitis pubis (bone marrow edema-predominant)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; conservative management or surgical repair for refractory cases.

7. Osteitis pubis, MRI

Variable

Stress-related inflammatory change at the pubic symphysis, common in athletes with repetitive kicking/cutting motions.

Imaging Characteristics

Bone marrow edema on both sides of the pubic symphysis, with variable symphyseal widening or irregularity in more chronic cases.

Differential
  • Osteitis pubis (overuse-related)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; activity modification and physical therapy.

8. Piriformis syndrome, MRI

Variable

Sciatic nerve irritation from the piriformis muscle, a controversial and often clinical diagnosis with variable MRI correlation.

Imaging Characteristics

Piriformis muscle asymmetry, edema, or an anatomic variant relationship with the sciatic nerve (nerve coursing through or around the muscle), though findings can be subtle or absent.

Differential
  • Piriformis syndrome (largely a clinical diagnosis)
  • Other causes of sciatica (lumbar spine pathology should be excluded)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam; physical therapy and consider lumbar spine imaging to exclude a more common cause of sciatica.

Knee - Meniscus & Ligament

15 findings

1. Medial meniscus tear

Concerning

Tear of the medial meniscus, more common than lateral meniscus tears given its lesser mobility and stronger attachment to the joint capsule.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased intrameniscal signal extending to an articular surface (a horizontal, vertical, radial, or complex tear pattern), distinct from intrasubstance degenerative signal that does not reach a surface.

Differential
  • Horizontal tear
  • Vertical/longitudinal tear (bucket-handle if displaced)
  • Radial tear
  • Complex/degenerative tear pattern
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; management (conservative, partial meniscectomy, or repair) depends on tear pattern, location, and patient factors.

2. Lateral meniscus tear

Concerning

Tear of the lateral meniscus, less common than medial meniscus tears but with a stronger association with acute ACL injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased intrameniscal signal extending to an articular surface, with tear patterns similar to those described for the medial meniscus.

Differential
  • Lateral meniscus tear (higher association with acute ACL tear than medial meniscus tears)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral.

3. Bucket-handle meniscal tear

Concerning

Displaced longitudinal meniscal tear fragment flipped into the intercondylar notch, causing mechanical locking symptoms.

Imaging Characteristics

Displaced meniscal fragment within the intercondylar notch, often with the 'double PCL sign' (the displaced fragment lying parallel to and mimicking a second posterior cruciate ligament) and an associated absent/truncated meniscus at its native location.

Differential
  • Bucket-handle tear (mechanical locking symptoms common)
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic referral given mechanical locking and the need for prompt arthroscopic treatment.

4. Meniscal root tear

Concerning

Tear at the meniscal root attachment, functionally similar to a total meniscectomy given loss of hoop stress function, with accelerated cartilage degeneration risk if untreated.

Imaging Characteristics

Disruption of the meniscal root attachment, often with an associated 'ghost sign' (absence of the expected meniscal tissue) and meniscal extrusion.

Differential
  • Meniscal root tear (posterior medial meniscus root most common)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral for root repair consideration given the functional consequences of untreated root tears.

5. Meniscal extrusion

Variable

Displacement of the meniscus beyond the tibial plateau margin, associated with root tears, severe degenerative tears, or advanced osteoarthritis.

Imaging Characteristics

Meniscal body displaced >3 mm beyond the peripheral margin of the tibial plateau on coronal imaging.

Differential
  • Meniscal extrusion (associated with root tear, severe degenerative tear, or advanced OA)
Next Step

Correlate with the underlying cause; orthopedic referral as indicated.

6. Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) tear

Concerning

Tear of the ACL, one of the most common significant knee ligament injuries, particularly in pivoting sports.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity or non-visualization of normal ACL fibers, with secondary signs including bone contusions (lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibia), anterior tibial translation, and a buckled PCL.

Differential
  • Complete ACL tear
  • Partial ACL tear (less common, more subtle)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; reconstruction considered based on activity level and instability symptoms.

7. Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) tear

Concerning

Tear of the PCL, less common than ACL tears, often from a direct blow to the anterior tibia (dashboard injury).

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity or abnormal signal/contour of the normally taut, dark PCL fibers.

Differential
  • PCL tear (partial or complete)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; many PCL tears managed conservatively unless combined with other ligament injuries.

8. Medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury, MRI

Variable

Injury to the MCL, from a valgus stress mechanism, graded by severity.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal surrounding (grade I, sprain) or within (grade II, partial tear) the MCL, or frank discontinuity (grade III, complete tear).

Differential
  • Grade I MCL sprain
  • Grade II partial tear
  • Grade III complete tear
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; MCL injuries are frequently managed conservatively even when complete, given good intrinsic healing potential.

9. Lateral collateral ligament/posterolateral corner injury, knee

Concerning

Injury to the lateral collateral ligament and posterolateral corner structures, less common than MCL injury but associated with greater instability if missed.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity of the LCL and associated posterolateral corner structures (popliteus, popliteofibular ligament, biceps femoris).

Differential
  • Isolated LCL injury
  • Posterolateral corner injury complex (higher instability risk if missed)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; posterolateral corner injuries often require surgical repair given poor healing potential if untreated.

10. Multiligamentous knee injury / knee dislocation

Concerning

Injury to multiple knee ligaments simultaneously, associated with frank or spontaneously reduced knee dislocation, with significant neurovascular injury risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Combined disruption of two or more major knee ligaments (ACL, PCL, MCL, LCL/posterolateral corner), which should prompt evaluation for popliteal artery and peroneal nerve injury.

Differential
  • Multiligamentous knee injury (high-energy or low-energy in obese patients)
Next Step

Emergent vascular assessment (ankle-brachial index, CT angiography) given popliteal artery injury risk; orthopedic trauma referral.

11. Patellar tendon tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the patellar tendon, less common than quadriceps tendon tears, typically in patients under 40.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the patellar tendon with variable patella alta if complete.

Differential
  • Complete patellar tendon tear
  • Partial tear/tendinopathy (jumper's knee)
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic referral for complete tears given the need for prompt surgical repair.

12. Quadriceps tendon tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the quadriceps tendon, more common than patellar tendon tears, typically in patients over 40 with predisposing factors (diabetes, chronic kidney disease, steroid use).

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the quadriceps tendon just proximal to the patella, with variable patella baja if complete.

Differential
  • Complete quadriceps tendon tear
  • Partial tear/tendinopathy
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic referral for complete tears given the need for prompt surgical repair.

13. Chondral/osteochondral lesion, knee MRI

Variable

Focal cartilage or osteochondral injury, from acute trauma or osteochondritis dissecans.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal cartilage signal abnormality or defect, with an associated subchondral bone injury in osteochondral lesions; fragment stability is a key assessment in OCD.

Differential
  • Chondral defect (cartilage only)
  • Osteochondral defect (cartilage and bone)
  • Osteochondritis dissecans
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; management depends on lesion size, location, and stability.

14. Patellofemoral maltracking / MPFL injury

Variable

Abnormal patellar tracking within the trochlear groove, often related to medial patellofemoral ligament injury from prior dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

MPFL discontinuity or attenuation, trochlear dysplasia, and patellar lateral tilt/subluxation on axial imaging.

Differential
  • MPFL tear (from patellar dislocation)
  • Trochlear dysplasia (predisposing anatomic factor)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral for recurrent instability management.

15. Baker (popliteal) cyst, MRI

Benign

Fluid distension of the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa, commonly associated with an underlying intra-articular process.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense cystic collection in the posteromedial knee, communicating with the joint space through a characteristic neck between the semimembranosus and medial gastrocnemius tendons.

Differential
  • Baker cyst (commonly associated with an underlying meniscal tear or osteoarthritis)
Next Step

Treat the underlying intra-articular pathology; cyst often resolves once the primary process is addressed.

Ankle & Foot - Ligament & Tendon

11 findings

1. Anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injury

Variable

Injury to the ATFL, the most commonly injured ankle ligament, from an inversion mechanism.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal, thickening, or discontinuity of the ATFL, the most anterior of the lateral ankle ligament complex.

Differential
  • ATFL sprain/tear (most common ankle ligament injury)
Next Step

Conservative management for isolated injury; orthopedic/sports medicine referral for chronic instability.

2. Calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) injury

Variable

Injury to the CFL, often occurring alongside ATFL injury in more severe ankle sprains.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity of the CFL, assessed alongside the ATFL for overall lateral ligament complex integrity.

Differential
  • CFL sprain/tear (often combined with ATFL injury in higher-grade sprains)
Next Step

Conservative management; orthopedic referral for chronic instability.

3. Deltoid ligament injury, ankle

Concerning

Injury to the medial (deltoid) ligament complex of the ankle, from an eversion mechanism, less common than lateral ligament injury but associated with greater instability.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity of the deltoid ligament complex, correlate with associated syndesmotic and/or fibular injury.

Differential
  • Deltoid ligament sprain/tear
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; often associated with syndesmotic injury requiring further stability assessment.

4. Syndesmotic ligament injury, MRI

Concerning

Injury to the distal tibiofibular syndesmotic ligaments (anterior-inferior and posterior-inferior tibiofibular ligaments), a 'high ankle sprain.'

Imaging Characteristics

Increased signal or discontinuity of the anterior-inferior and/or posterior-inferior tibiofibular ligaments, with associated syndesmotic widening in more severe injuries.

Differential
  • Syndesmotic sprain (high ankle sprain, longer recovery than lateral ankle sprains)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; may require surgical stabilization depending on stability grade.

5. Achilles tendon tear, MRI

Concerning

Tear of the Achilles tendon, most common in the watershed hypovascular zone 2-6 cm proximal to the calcaneal insertion.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the Achilles tendon with a gap, most often in the hypovascular zone; partial tears show intrasubstance signal change without complete discontinuity.

Differential
  • Complete Achilles tear
  • Partial Achilles tear/tendinopathy
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic/sports medicine referral for complete tears given time-sensitive management decisions (surgical vs. functional bracing).

6. Achilles tendinopathy, MRI

Variable

Degenerative change of the Achilles tendon, either at the insertion (insertional) or mid-substance (non-insertional).

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening and increased intrasubstance signal without a discrete tear, at the insertion or mid-substance.

Differential
  • Insertional Achilles tendinopathy (often with associated Haglund deformity/retrocalcaneal bursitis)
  • Non-insertional (mid-substance) tendinopathy
Next Step

Conservative management (eccentric strengthening, activity modification); orthopedic/sports medicine referral for refractory cases.

7. Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, MRI

Concerning

Degeneration or tear of the posterior tibial tendon, the leading cause of acquired adult flatfoot deformity.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening, increased signal, or tearing of the posterior tibial tendon posterior to the medial malleolus, with associated flatfoot deformity in chronic cases.

Differential
  • Posterior tibial tendon tendinopathy/tear (progressive stages from tendinopathy to complete tear with rigid flatfoot deformity)
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral; management depends on the stage of dysfunction, from bracing to surgical reconstruction.

8. Peroneal tendon tear/subluxation, MRI

Concerning

Tear or instability of the peroneus longus and/or brevis tendons, often associated with chronic ankle instability or a superior peroneal retinaculum injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Longitudinal split tear (classically of the peroneus brevis) or tendon subluxation/dislocation from the retromalleolar groove with retinacular injury.

Differential
  • Peroneus brevis split tear (most common pattern)
  • Peroneal tendon subluxation/dislocation (retinacular injury)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral.

9. Plantar fasciitis, MRI

Variable

Degenerative/inflammatory change of the plantar fascia at its calcaneal origin, a common cause of heel pain, though ultrasound and clinical exam are often sufficient for diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening (>4 mm) and increased signal of the plantar fascia at its calcaneal origin, with adjacent bone marrow edema in more severe cases.

Differential
  • Plantar fasciitis (degenerative, despite the '-itis' name)
  • Plantar fascia tear (more focal discontinuity, less common)
Next Step

Conservative management (stretching, orthotics); further imaging only for refractory or atypical presentations.

10. Morton neuroma, MRI

Variable

Perineural fibrosis of an interdigital nerve, most common at the third webspace, causing forefoot pain.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T1-hypointense, variably enhancing mass-like lesion within the interdigital space, most often between the third and fourth metatarsal heads.

Differential
  • Morton neuroma (perineural fibrosis, not a true neoplasm)
  • Intermetatarsal bursitis (can coexist or mimic)
Next Step

Podiatric referral; conservative management (padding, injection) or surgical excision for refractory cases.

11. Ankle osteochondral lesion of the talus

Variable

Focal cartilage/subchondral bone injury of the talar dome, from trauma (often associated with ankle sprain) or osteochondritis dissecans.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal subchondral signal abnormality of the talar dome, most often anterolateral (traumatic) or posteromedial (idiopathic), with fragment stability assessed by surrounding fluid signal.

Differential
  • Osteochondral lesion of the talus (traumatic or idiopathic)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; management depends on lesion size, location, and stability.

Bone Marrow Edema, Stress & AVN

8 findings

1. Bone marrow edema, traumatic (bone bruise)

Variable

Microtrabecular injury from trauma without a discrete fracture line, common with acute ligamentous injuries.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, T2-hyperintense/STIR-bright marrow signal without a discrete fracture line, often in a characteristic 'kissing' pattern reflecting the impaction mechanism (e.g., lateral femoral condyle and posterolateral tibia with ACL tears).

Differential
  • Bone contusion/bone bruise (typically resolves over weeks to months)
Next Step

Correlate with the mechanism and associated soft-tissue injuries; usually managed conservatively as the marrow edema itself resolves over time.

2. Stress reaction / stress fracture, MRI

Variable

Overuse injury spectrum from mild marrow edema (stress reaction) to a discrete fracture line (stress fracture), with MRI the most sensitive modality for early detection.

Imaging Characteristics

Graded spectrum from mild periosteal/marrow edema (early stress reaction) to a discrete, low-signal fracture line surrounded by edema (established stress fracture).

Differential
  • Stress reaction (early, lower grade)
  • Stress fracture (established, higher grade)
Next Step

Activity modification per grade severity; higher-risk stress fractures (femoral neck tension side, anterior tibial cortex, navicular, fifth metatarsal) warrant closer orthopedic follow-up given complication risk.

3. Avascular necrosis, femoral head MRI

Concerning

Ischemic bone death of the femoral head, with MRI as the most sensitive modality for early detection, well before plain film changes appear.

Imaging Characteristics

Serpiginous, geographic subchondral signal abnormality with a characteristic low-signal rim ('double line sign' on T2), progressing to subchondral collapse in advanced stages.

Differential
  • Avascular necrosis (steroid use, alcohol use, trauma, sickle cell disease among risk factors)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; staging (Ficat/ARCO) guides management from core decompression to arthroplasty.

4. Transient osteoporosis of the hip

Variable

Self-limited condition with diffuse femoral head/neck marrow edema and pain, most common in middle-aged men or women in late pregnancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse marrow edema throughout the femoral head and neck without a discrete subchondral fracture line or the geographic pattern of AVN.

Differential
  • Transient osteoporosis of the hip (self-limited)
  • Early avascular necrosis (key differential, distinguished partly by the diffuse vs. geographic edema pattern)
  • Subchondral insufficiency fracture
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; conservative management (protected weight-bearing) as this is typically self-limited over several months.

5. Subchondral insufficiency fracture

Concerning

Fracture occurring in subchondral bone from normal stress on weakened (osteoporotic) bone, most common at the femoral head or medial tibial plateau in older adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear, subchondral low-signal fracture line paralleling the articular surface, with surrounding marrow edema, most often at the femoral head (SIFH) or medial tibial plateau.

Differential
  • Subchondral insufficiency fracture of the femoral head or knee
Next Step

Orthopedic referral; protected weight-bearing given collapse risk.

6. Bone infarct, MRI

Variable

Area of infarcted (dead) bone within the medullary cavity, from ischemia, often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Serpiginous, geographic area with a low-signal rim, most often in the metaphysis/diaphysis of long bones, distinct from epiphyseal AVN.

Differential
  • Bone infarct (often incidental, associated with steroid use, sickle cell disease, or idiopathic)
Next Step

Usually no action needed for an incidental, asymptomatic infarct; correlate with underlying risk factors.

7. Complex regional pain syndrome, MRI findings

Variable

Chronic pain syndrome with variable, nonspecific MRI findings, primarily a clinical diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Patchy marrow edema and soft-tissue edema/enhancement, without a specific diagnostic pattern; MRI is adjunctive to the clinical diagnosis.

Differential
  • Complex regional pain syndrome (largely a clinical diagnosis with supportive but nonspecific imaging)
Next Step

Pain management/physical therapy referral; imaging primarily helps exclude other structural causes of pain.

8. Sickle cell-related bone infarction/marrow changes

Concerning

Bone marrow and infarction changes from sickle cell disease, affecting both marrow signal and epiphyseal/metaphyseal regions.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely abnormal marrow signal from red marrow hyperplasia, with superimposed geographic infarcts showing the characteristic serpiginous, low-signal-rimmed pattern.

Differential
  • Sickle cell-related marrow hyperplasia and infarction
  • Superimposed osteomyelitis (important complication to distinguish given increased infection risk in sickle cell disease)
Next Step

Hematology referral; correlate with clinical presentation to distinguish infarction from superimposed osteomyelitis, which can be challenging.

Soft Tissue Tumors - Benign

8 findings

1. Lipoma, MRI characterization

Benign

Most common benign soft-tissue tumor, composed of mature adipose tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous fat signal on all sequences (following subcutaneous fat on every sequence, including suppression on fat-saturated images), with a thin, imperceptible capsule and no enhancing soft-tissue components or thick septa.

Differential
  • Simple lipoma (benign)
  • Well-differentiated liposarcoma (thick septa >2 mm, nodular/globular non-adipose components - key distinguishing features)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous lipoma; further workup (biopsy) for atypical features.

2. Hemangioma, soft tissue MRI

Benign

Benign vascular malformation/tumor of the soft tissues, most often intramuscular.

Imaging Characteristics

Lobulated, T2-hyperintense mass with serpiginous flow voids and often visible fat septa and phleboliths (a relatively specific finding when present).

Differential
  • Soft tissue hemangioma (phleboliths, when present, are a helpful specific sign)
  • Vascular malformation (venous, lymphatic, or mixed)
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic; interventional radiology or surgical referral for symptomatic lesions.

3. Peripheral nerve sheath tumor, benign (schwannoma/neurofibroma)

Variable

Benign tumor arising from a peripheral nerve.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform, well-defined mass in continuity with a nerve, with the 'target sign' (central low signal, peripheral high signal on T2) and 'split fat sign' (fat rim at the mass margins) as supportive features.

Differential
  • Schwannoma (eccentric to the nerve, often 'target sign' present)
  • Neurofibroma (nerve passes through the substance of the tumor, associated with neurofibromatosis if multiple)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or growing lesions; multiple lesions warrant neurofibromatosis workup.

4. Nodular fasciitis

Benign

Benign, rapidly growing reactive fibroblastic proliferation that can mimic a sarcoma clinically given its rapid growth.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined or infiltrative subcutaneous/fascial mass with variable, often heterogeneous signal and avid enhancement, typically <3 cm and rapidly growing (weeks).

Differential
  • Nodular fasciitis (self-limited, benign despite rapid growth)
  • Sarcoma (key differential given overlapping rapid growth; biopsy often needed to confirm benign nature)
Next Step

Biopsy is often pursued given the concerning rapid growth pattern despite the ultimately benign nature; surgical referral.

5. Giant cell tumor of tendon sheath

Benign

Common benign soft-tissue tumor, most often in the hand/fingers, arising from a tendon sheath.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated mass adjacent to a tendon sheath, characteristically low signal on T2 (from hemosiderin content) with variable enhancement.

Differential
  • Giant cell tumor of tendon sheath (benign, most common hand soft-tissue tumor after ganglion cyst)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision if symptomatic; recurrence is possible even after excision.

6. Fibroma / fibrous tumor, soft tissue

Benign

Benign fibrous soft-tissue tumor, various subtypes.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined mass with low-to-intermediate signal on T1/T2 (reflecting dense collagen content) and variable enhancement.

Differential
  • Benign fibroma
  • Desmoid-type fibromatosis (more locally aggressive, infiltrative margins - key differential)
Next Step

Surgical referral; biopsy for atypical or infiltrative-appearing lesions to exclude a more aggressive fibromatosis.

7. Desmoid-type fibromatosis

Concerning

Locally aggressive but non-metastasizing fibrous tumor with a high local recurrence rate.

Imaging Characteristics

Infiltrative, often ill-defined mass with variable T2 signal (low-signal bands reflecting dense collagen bundles), avid enhancement, and a tendency to infiltrate along fascial planes.

Differential
  • Desmoid-type fibromatosis (locally aggressive)
  • Soft-tissue sarcoma (key differential, biopsy often needed)
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral given high local recurrence risk even after resection; some centers favor observation for stable, asymptomatic lesions.

8. Intramuscular myxoma

Benign

Benign, gelatinous soft-tissue tumor within muscle, associated with fibrous dysplasia in Mazabraud syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, homogeneous, markedly T2-hyperintense (fluid-like) intramuscular mass with a thin enhancing rim/septa.

Differential
  • Intramuscular myxoma (benign)
  • Association with fibrous dysplasia (Mazabraud syndrome) if concurrent bone lesions present
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic and imaging is classic; surgical referral if symptomatic.

Soft Tissue Tumors - Malignant

6 findings

1. Soft tissue sarcoma, MRI characterization

Concerning

Malignant soft-tissue tumor, with MRI as the primary modality for local staging.

Imaging Characteristics

Large (often >5 cm), deep, heterogeneous soft-tissue mass with necrosis, avid and heterogeneous enhancement, and peritumoral edema; size, depth, and growth are the most reliable clinical red flags.

Differential
  • Soft tissue sarcoma (various histologic subtypes: liposarcoma, undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma, and others)
  • Benign mass with atypical features
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy (ideally coordinated with the treating surgical oncology team before biopsy) and staging.

2. Liposarcoma

Concerning

Malignant fat-containing (or fat-poor, dedifferentiated) soft-tissue tumor, the most common soft-tissue sarcoma in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-differentiated type: fat-predominant with thick septa (>2 mm) and nodular non-adipose components, closely mimicking a lipoma but with these atypical features. Dedifferentiated/pleomorphic types: predominantly non-fatty, heterogeneous soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Well-differentiated liposarcoma (atypical lipomatous tumor)
  • Dedifferentiated liposarcoma
  • Simple lipoma (key differential for well-differentiated subtype)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

3. Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma

Concerning

Common high-grade soft-tissue sarcoma in older adults, without a specific line of differentiation (formerly termed malignant fibrous histiocytoma).

Imaging Characteristics

Large, heterogeneous, deep soft-tissue mass with necrosis and avid, heterogeneous enhancement, without specific distinguishing imaging features from other high-grade sarcomas.

Differential
  • Undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma
  • Other high-grade soft-tissue sarcoma subtypes
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

4. Synovial sarcoma

Concerning

Soft-tissue sarcoma that, despite its name, does not typically arise from synovium, most common in young adults near large joints.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined or lobulated juxta-articular soft-tissue mass, often with heterogeneous signal from hemorrhage/necrosis/calcification ('triple sign') and sometimes fluid-fluid levels.

Differential
  • Synovial sarcoma (classic in young adults near the knee)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

5. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor

Concerning

Malignant tumor arising from a peripheral nerve, associated with neurofibromatosis type 1 in a substantial proportion of cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, rapidly growing fusiform mass along a nerve with heterogeneous signal, irregular margins, and often central necrosis, distinguishing it from a benign nerve sheath tumor.

Differential
  • Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (particularly concerning in a patient with NF1 and a rapidly growing lesion)
  • Benign nerve sheath tumor (key differential)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging; correlate with NF1 history and growth rate.

6. Soft tissue metastasis

Concerning

Secondary malignant deposit within the soft tissues, less common than bone metastases but important to recognize.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue mass with variable signal and enhancement, correlate with a known primary malignancy.

Differential
  • Soft tissue metastasis
  • Primary soft-tissue sarcoma (if no known primary)
Next Step

Oncology referral; biopsy if diagnosis is uncertain or would change management.

Bone Tumors, MRI Characterization

8 findings

1. Osteosarcoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Most common primary malignant bone tumor, with MRI essential for local staging (marrow and soft-tissue extent, joint involvement, skip lesions).

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous marrow-replacing mass with cortical destruction and a large associated soft-tissue component, with MRI best defining the intramedullary extent and any skip metastases.

Differential
  • Osteosarcoma
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; MRI of the entire involved bone to assess for skip lesions before surgical planning.

2. Ewing sarcoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Aggressive malignant bone tumor, with MRI essential for local staging given the often extensive soft-tissue component.

Imaging Characteristics

Marrow-replacing lesion with a large, often disproportionately extensive soft-tissue mass relative to the bony abnormality.

Differential
  • Ewing sarcoma
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic oncology referral; MRI for local staging and chest CT/bone scan or PET for metastatic staging.

3. Giant cell tumor of bone, MRI

Concerning

Locally aggressive, typically benign tumor at the epiphysis of long bones in young adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Epiphyseal-extending-to-metaphyseal lesion with heterogeneous signal, often with fluid-fluid levels from secondary aneurysmal bone cyst change, and cortical thinning/breakthrough.

Differential
  • Giant cell tumor of bone
  • Clear cell chondrosarcoma (also epiphyseal)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and resection/curettage planning.

4. Chondrosarcoma, MRI

Concerning

Malignant cartilage-forming tumor, with MRI assessing marrow extent and any cortical breakthrough/soft-tissue extension.

Imaging Characteristics

Lobulated, markedly T2-hyperintense (high water content of hyaline cartilage) mass with endosteal scalloping and, in higher-grade lesions, cortical destruction and soft-tissue extension.

Differential
  • Chondrosarcoma (grade correlates with the degree of aggressive features)
  • Enchondroma (benign counterpart)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

5. Vertebral/bone metastasis, MRI (extremity)

Concerning

Secondary malignant bone lesion in an extremity, with MRI as the most sensitive modality for marrow involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-hypointense, variable T2 signal replacing normal fatty marrow, which can be solitary or multiple.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease
  • Multiple myeloma
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging; assess for pathologic fracture risk at weight-bearing sites.

6. Multiple myeloma, MRI (extremity)

Concerning

Plasma cell malignancy with marrow-replacing lesions, assessable in the appendicular skeleton though the axial skeleton is more commonly involved.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse marrow replacement with low T1 signal, sometimes with a 'salt and pepper' heterogeneous pattern in diffuse disease.

Differential
  • Multiple myeloma
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral; whole-body MRI or PET-CT for staging.

7. Osteoid osteoma, MRI

Benign

Benign bone-forming tumor, with MRI showing prominent surrounding edema that can be disproportionate to the small nidus and occasionally mimic a more aggressive process.

Imaging Characteristics

Small nidus (better characterized on CT) with often extensive surrounding bone marrow and soft-tissue edema on MRI, which can be misleading if the nidus itself is not well appreciated.

Differential
  • Osteoid osteoma (the disproportionate surrounding edema on MRI is a recognized diagnostic pitfall that can suggest a more aggressive process)
Next Step

CT for definitive nidus characterization if MRI findings are ambiguous; orthopedic referral for treatment.

8. Enchondroma vs. low-grade chondrosarcoma, MRI distinction

Variable

Distinguishing a benign cartilage tumor from its low-grade malignant counterpart, a common diagnostic challenge.

Imaging Characteristics

Both show lobulated, markedly T2-hyperintense chondroid tissue; features favoring chondrosarcoma include larger size, endosteal scalloping >2/3 of the cortical thickness, cortical destruction, and an associated soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Enchondroma (benign)
  • Low-grade (grade 1) chondrosarcoma (overlapping imaging features, sometimes requiring biopsy or correlation with growth on serial imaging)
Next Step

Orthopedic oncology referral for lesions with any aggressive features; correlation with clinical symptoms and, when needed, biopsy.

Infection

6 findings

1. Osteomyelitis, MRI

Concerning

Bone infection, with MRI as the most sensitive and specific imaging modality for early detection.

Imaging Characteristics

Marrow edema (T1-hypointense, T2/STIR-hyperintense) with adjacent soft-tissue edema/enhancement; a sequestrum (non-enhancing necrotic bone) may be seen in more established infection.

Differential
  • Acute osteomyelitis
  • Chronic osteomyelitis (sequestrum present)
  • Bone marrow edema from another cause (stress reaction, reactive change) - key differential
Next Step

Orthopedic/infectious disease referral; blood cultures and possible biopsy for organism identification.

2. Septic arthritis, MRI

Concerning

Joint infection, with MRI showing joint effusion and synovial changes, a surgical emergency given cartilage destruction risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint effusion with synovial thickening and enhancement, sometimes with adjacent bone marrow edema (osteomyelitis) or a drainable abscess.

Differential
  • Septic arthritis (surgical emergency)
  • Inflammatory/reactive arthritis (less commonly shows adjacent osteomyelitis)
Next Step

Emergent orthopedic consultation for joint aspiration/washout; do not delay treatment for imaging.

3. Soft tissue abscess, MRI

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense fluid collection with central restricted diffusion, distinguishing it from a sterile fluid collection or hematoma.

Differential
  • Soft tissue abscess
  • Infected hematoma/seroma
  • Necrotic tumor (less common differential)
Next Step

Incision and drainage plus antibiotics.

4. Necrotizing fasciitis, MRI

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection with fascial plane involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive fascial thickening and T2-hyperintense signal tracking along fascial planes, with variable soft-tissue gas.

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
  • Simple cellulitis/abscess (less extensive fascial involvement)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation for debridement; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation.

5. Diabetic foot osteomyelitis, MRI

Concerning

Bone infection in the diabetic foot, with MRI as the most sensitive and specific modality, particularly important given the overlap with Charcot changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Marrow edema with enhancement, often adjacent to a soft-tissue ulcer or sinus tract; a sinus tract extending directly to bone is a strong indicator of underlying osteomyelitis.

Differential
  • Diabetic foot osteomyelitis
  • Charcot neuroarthropathy (key differential, particularly in the acute phase, though MRI is more specific than plain film for this distinction)
Next Step

Podiatric/infectious disease referral; correlate with the presence of a sinus tract and clinical context.

6. Pyomyositis, MRI

Concerning

Bacterial infection of skeletal muscle, forming an intramuscular abscess, more common in tropical climates but increasingly recognized elsewhere.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse muscle edema in the early phase, progressing to a rim-enhancing, centrally necrotic intramuscular abscess.

Differential
  • Pyomyositis (Staphylococcus aureus most common cause)
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage for a discrete abscess plus antibiotics.

Muscle & Tendon Injury, General

8 findings

1. Muscle strain, grading

Variable

Muscle-tendon unit injury from excessive stretch or forceful contraction, graded by severity.

Imaging Characteristics

Grade 1: mild edema without fiber disruption. Grade 2: partial fiber disruption with edema and hemorrhage. Grade 3: complete tear with retraction and a fluid-filled gap.

Differential
  • Grade 1 strain (mild)
  • Grade 2 strain (partial tear)
  • Grade 3 strain (complete tear)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; management and return-to-play timeline depend on grade.

2. Hamstring injury, MRI

Variable

Injury to the hamstring muscle-tendon complex, common in sprinting/kicking athletes, with the proximal myotendinous junction most frequently involved.

Imaging Characteristics

Edema and/or fiber discontinuity at the myotendinous junction or, in more severe injuries, complete tendon avulsion from the ischial tuberosity.

Differential
  • Myotendinous strain (most common)
  • Proximal hamstring tendon avulsion (complete, may require surgical repair)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; complete proximal avulsions with significant retraction often warrant surgical repair.

3. Gastrocnemius tear ('tennis leg')

Variable

Tear of the medial gastrocnemius muscle, classically at the myotendinous junction, common in middle-aged recreational athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid/hemorrhage at the myotendinous junction of the medial gastrocnemius, often with fluid tracking between the gastrocnemius and soleus.

Differential
  • Medial gastrocnemius tear ('tennis leg')
  • Deep vein thrombosis (important differential given similar presentation with calf pain/swelling)
Next Step

Conservative management (rest, compression); exclude DVT given overlapping clinical presentation.

4. Muscle contusion, MRI

Variable

Direct traumatic muscle injury from a blunt impact, without fiber disruption at the myotendinous junction.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined intramuscular edema and hemorrhage at the site of direct impact, without the myotendinous junction predilection of a strain injury.

Differential
  • Muscle contusion
Next Step

Conservative management; monitor for myositis ossificans as a potential late complication, particularly with repeated trauma to the same area.

5. Compartment syndrome, MRI findings

Concerning

Elevated pressure within a fascial compartment, primarily a clinical diagnosis, with MRI showing supportive but non-diagnostic findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse muscle edema within an affected compartment, though MRI is not the primary diagnostic tool for this time-sensitive clinical diagnosis.

Differential
  • Compartment syndrome (surgical emergency, diagnosed by clinical exam and compartment pressure measurement)
Next Step

Do not delay fasciotomy for imaging; compartment syndrome remains a clinical diagnosis based on exam and pressure measurement.

6. Rhabdomyolysis, MRI findings

Concerning

Muscle breakdown with release of intracellular contents, from trauma, exertion, toxins, or other causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse muscle edema and swelling, often bilateral and symmetric if from a systemic cause (exertional, toxic), with variable enhancement.

Differential
  • Rhabdomyolysis (correlate with elevated CK and clinical context)
Next Step

Correlate with CK levels and renal function; aggressive IV fluid resuscitation and treatment of the underlying cause.

7. Myositis, inflammatory (dermatomyositis/polymyositis)

Concerning

Inflammatory muscle disease causing symmetric proximal muscle weakness.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, often proximal muscle edema on STIR/fat-saturated T2 sequences, useful for both diagnosis and identifying an optimal biopsy site.

Differential
  • Dermatomyositis/polymyositis
  • Other inflammatory myopathies
Next Step

Rheumatology/neurology referral; muscle biopsy for definitive diagnosis, often guided by the MRI findings.

8. Tendon xanthoma

Variable

Lipid deposition within a tendon, classically the Achilles tendon, associated with familial hypercholesterolemia.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform thickening of the tendon (most often Achilles) with signal characteristics reflecting lipid deposition.

Differential
  • Tendon xanthoma (correlate with lipid panel and family history of hypercholesterolemia)
Next Step

Endocrine/lipid referral for hypercholesterolemia workup and management.

Nerve Entrapment & Neuropathy

6 findings

1. Carpal tunnel syndrome, MRI (median nerve)

Variable

Median nerve compression at the wrist, the most common entrapment neuropathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Median nerve flattening at the level of the hamate with proximal swelling/increased signal, sometimes with an identifiable underlying cause.

Differential
  • Idiopathic carpal tunnel syndrome
  • Secondary carpal tunnel syndrome (mass, tenosynovitis)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam and electrodiagnostic studies; hand surgery referral for refractory or secondary cases.

2. Cubital tunnel syndrome (ulnar nerve)

Variable

Ulnar nerve compression at the elbow, the second most common upper extremity entrapment neuropathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Ulnar nerve swelling and increased signal at or proximal to the cubital tunnel, sometimes with an identifiable compressive structure (thickened retinaculum, anconeus epitrochlearis anomalous muscle, ganglion cyst).

Differential
  • Cubital tunnel syndrome (idiopathic or secondary to a compressive lesion)
Next Step

Neurology/orthopedic referral; electrodiagnostic studies for confirmation.

3. Tarsal tunnel syndrome

Variable

Posterior tibial nerve compression at the ankle, less common than carpal tunnel syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Posterior tibial nerve swelling/increased signal within the tarsal tunnel, sometimes with an identifiable compressive lesion (ganglion cyst, varicosities, accessory muscle).

Differential
  • Tarsal tunnel syndrome (idiopathic or secondary to a compressive lesion)
Next Step

Neurology/orthopedic/podiatric referral; electrodiagnostic studies for confirmation.

4. Peroneal (fibular) neuropathy at the fibular head

Concerning

Common peroneal nerve compression at the fibular neck, a common site of lower extremity entrapment, causing foot drop.

Imaging Characteristics

Nerve swelling/increased signal at the fibular neck, sometimes with denervation edema/atrophy in the anterior and lateral compartment muscles (tibialis anterior, peroneus longus/brevis).

Differential
  • Peroneal neuropathy at the fibular head (compression, traction, or mass-related)
Next Step

Neurology/orthopedic referral; electrodiagnostic studies and identification of any compressive cause.

5. Denervation muscle changes, MRI

Concerning

Muscle signal changes from loss of nerve innervation, evolving from acute edema to chronic fatty atrophy.

Imaging Characteristics

Acute/subacute: diffuse muscle edema (STIR/T2 hyperintensity) without volume loss. Chronic: muscle volume loss with fatty replacement (T1-hyperintense).

Differential
  • Denervation change from a specific identifiable nerve injury/entrapment
  • Correlate the muscle distribution with the specific nerve/root involved
Next Step

Neurology referral; the pattern of muscle involvement helps localize the level of nerve or nerve root injury.

6. Peripheral nerve traumatic injury, MRI

Concerning

Traumatic nerve injury ranging from neurapraxia to complete transection, with MRI (MR neurography) assessing continuity and the degree of injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Nerve swelling, increased signal, and, in complete injuries, discontinuity with a neuroma-in-continuity or true gap; MR neurography provides detailed nerve-specific imaging.

Differential
  • Neurapraxia (mildest, best prognosis)
  • Axonotmesis (partial)
  • Neurotmesis (complete transection, worst prognosis, may require surgical repair)
Next Step

Neurology/hand or peripheral nerve surgery referral; serial exams and electrodiagnostic studies to monitor recovery.

Arthropathy - Inflammatory & Crystal

8 findings

1. Rheumatoid arthritis, MRI findings

Concerning

Chronic inflammatory arthropathy, with MRI detecting synovitis, erosions, and marrow edema earlier than plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Synovial thickening/enhancement (synovitis), marrow edema (a predictor of subsequent erosion), and marginal bone erosions, classically at the MCP/PIP joints of the hands and MTP joints of the feet.

Differential
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (MRI detects disease activity earlier than plain film)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; MRI findings can guide early disease-modifying treatment decisions.

2. Gout, MRI findings

Concerning

Crystal deposition arthropathy from monosodium urate, with MRI showing tophi and erosive changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Tophi appear as soft-tissue masses with variable, often heterogeneous T1/T2 signal and variable enhancement, with adjacent well-defined ('punched-out') bone erosions.

Differential
  • Gouty tophus and associated erosive arthropathy
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with serum uric acid and joint aspiration during flares.

3. Calcium pyrophosphate deposition disease (CPPD), MRI

Variable

Crystal deposition arthropathy from calcium pyrophosphate crystals, causing chondrocalcinosis and, during flares, acute pseudogout.

Imaging Characteristics

Low-signal deposits within cartilage/menisci (chondrocalcinosis) on all sequences, sometimes with associated joint effusion during acute flares.

Differential
  • CPPD (chondrocalcinosis with or without acute pseudogout flare)
Next Step

Correlate with joint aspiration for definitive crystal identification during acute flares.

4. Psoriatic arthritis, MRI findings

Concerning

Inflammatory arthropathy associated with psoriasis, with MRI showing characteristic enthesitis and dactylitis in addition to synovitis/erosion.

Imaging Characteristics

Enthesitis (inflammation at tendon/ligament insertions), 'sausage digit' dactylitis (diffuse soft-tissue edema of an entire digit), synovitis, and bone erosion with adjacent new bone formation.

Differential
  • Psoriatic arthritis (enthesitis and dactylitis are relatively distinguishing features from rheumatoid arthritis)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with skin/nail findings.

5. Septic arthritis vs. inflammatory arthropathy, MRI distinction

Concerning

Distinguishing infectious from inflammatory (non-infectious) arthritis, a clinically important but sometimes imaging-overlapping distinction.

Imaging Characteristics

Both can show joint effusion and synovitis; features favoring infection include adjacent osteomyelitis, a drainable abscess, and single-joint (monoarticular) involvement with rapid onset.

Differential
  • Septic arthritis (surgical emergency)
  • Inflammatory/reactive arthropathy flare
Next Step

Joint aspiration remains essential for distinguishing infection from inflammatory arthropathy; do not rely on imaging alone.

6. Hemophilic arthropathy, MRI

Concerning

Chronic joint damage from recurrent hemarthrosis in patients with hemophilia, most often affecting the knees, elbows, and ankles.

Imaging Characteristics

Synovial hemosiderin deposition (low signal on gradient-echo/susceptibility sequences), synovial hypertrophy, and progressive cartilage/bone destruction with recurrent bleeding episodes.

Differential
  • Hemophilic arthropathy (correlate with known bleeding disorder history)
Next Step

Hematology referral; optimize factor replacement therapy to reduce further hemarthrosis episodes.

7. Pigmented villonodular synovitis (tenosynovial giant cell tumor)

Variable

Benign but locally aggressive proliferative synovial process, most often monoarticular, classically affecting the knee.

Imaging Characteristics

Synovial proliferation with characteristic diffuse low signal on gradient-echo/susceptibility sequences from hemosiderin deposition, often with joint effusion and bone erosion in advanced cases.

Differential
  • Pigmented villonodular synovitis / diffuse-type tenosynovial giant cell tumor
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for synovectomy; recurrence is possible, and newer targeted therapies (CSF1R inhibitors) are available for refractory/diffuse disease.

8. Synovial osteochondromatosis

Variable

Benign metaplastic process producing multiple cartilaginous/osteocartilaginous bodies within the synovium.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple, often uniform-sized intra-articular bodies, which may be cartilaginous (non-calcified, best seen on MRI) or calcified/ossified (also visible on plain film/CT).

Differential
  • Synovial osteochondromatosis (benign, can rarely undergo malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma)
Next Step

Orthopedic referral for symptomatic cases (mechanical symptoms) given the loose body burden.

Miscellaneous

5 findings

1. Bone marrow reconversion, MRI

Benign

Reversion of fatty (yellow) marrow to red (hematopoietic) marrow, from increased hematopoietic demand.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or patchy T1-hypointense marrow signal (less fatty than expected) in a distribution following the normal pattern of marrow reconversion (axial skeleton first, then appendicular).

Differential
  • Chronic anemia (hemolytic, chronic blood loss)
  • Smoking-related marrow reconversion
  • High-altitude living/endurance athletes
Next Step

Correlate with CBC and clinical context; usually a benign, reactive process not requiring specific treatment.

2. Red marrow, normal pediatric/young adult pattern

Benign

Normal distribution of hematopoietically active red marrow in children and young adults, which can be mistaken for pathologic marrow infiltration if the normal pattern is not recognized.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely present red marrow (lower T1 signal than fatty marrow, but with signal characteristics remaining higher than muscle) within the axial skeleton and metaphyses of long bones, following the expected age-based conversion pattern.

Differential
  • Normal age-appropriate red marrow distribution

No action needed; important to recognize as normal to avoid misdiagnosing infiltrative marrow disease.

3. Bone island (enostosis), MRI

Benign

Benign focus of compact bone within cancellous bone, common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, low-signal focus on all sequences (correlating with dense bone on CT/plain film), without surrounding edema.

Differential
  • Bone island (benign, common incidental finding)
  • Osteoblastic metastasis (larger, may show surrounding edema, and grows on serial imaging - key differential)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a small, stable, classic bone island.

4. Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome

Variable

Compression of the popliteal artery by an anomalous relationship with the gastrocnemius or popliteus muscle, causing claudication in young athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Medial deviation of the popliteal artery relative to an anomalous muscle origin, with dynamic imaging (plantarflexion/dorsiflexion) demonstrating positional compression.

Differential
  • Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome (consider in young athletes with exertional calf pain/claudication)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for consideration of surgical decompression.

5. Accessory muscle, extremity

Anomalous accessory muscle, a normal variant that can occasionally cause mass effect or nerve compression.

Additional muscle belly in an atypical location, most commonly described as the accessory soleus (posteromedial ankle) or peroneus quartus (lateral ankle).

Differential
  • Accessory soleus muscle
  • Peroneus quartus muscle
  • Other accessory muscle variants
Next Step

No action needed if asymptomatic; correlate with symptoms if causing mass effect or associated nerve entrapment.

Urgent orthopedic/sports medicine referral given the time-sensitive surgical repair window for complete tears.

4. Olecranon bursitis, ultrasound

Variable

Fluid distension and/or thickening of the olecranon bursa, from trauma, overuse, gout, or infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to complex fluid collection overlying the olecranon, with synovial thickening and internal debris/septations suggesting a more inflammatory or infected process.

Differential
  • Traumatic/mechanical bursitis (simple fluid)
  • Septic bursitis (complex, hyperemic)
  • Gouty bursitis (may show tophaceous material)
Next Step

Aspiration for fluid analysis if infection or crystal arthropathy is suspected; conservative management for simple bursitis.

5. Ulnar nerve subluxation, elbow ultrasound

Variable

Abnormal movement of the ulnar nerve out of the cubital tunnel with elbow flexion, well demonstrated dynamically by ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Ulnar nerve displaced anterior to the medial epicondyle on dynamic imaging during elbow flexion, rather than remaining within the cubital tunnel.

Differential
  • Ulnar nerve subluxation/dislocation (dynamic assessment is a particular strength of ultrasound for this diagnosis)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical symptoms of ulnar neuropathy; orthopedic/neurology referral if symptomatic.

Wrist & Hand

8 findings

1. De Quervain tenosynovitis, ultrasound

Variable

Inflammation of the first dorsal compartment tendons (abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis), a classic ultrasound diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon sheath thickening and fluid distension surrounding the first dorsal compartment tendons at the radial styloid, sometimes with a septum dividing the compartment (relevant to injection technique).

Differential
  • De Quervain tenosynovitis
Next Step

Conservative management (splinting, NSAIDs); ultrasound-guided injection for refractory cases.

2. Trigger finger, ultrasound

Variable

Stenosing tenosynovitis of a flexor tendon at the A1 pulley.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening of the flexor tendon and/or A1 pulley with surrounding tenosynovial fluid/thickening, sometimes with a visible nodule catching at the pulley on dynamic imaging.

Differential
  • Trigger finger (stenosing flexor tenosynovitis)
Next Step

Conservative management; ultrasound-guided A1 pulley injection or release for refractory cases.

3. Carpal tunnel syndrome, ultrasound (median nerve)

Variable

Median nerve compression at the wrist, assessable by ultrasound via cross-sectional area measurement.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased median nerve cross-sectional area at the wrist (commonly >10-12 mm², threshold varies by reference) with flattening distally and swelling proximal to the tunnel.

Differential
  • Idiopathic carpal tunnel syndrome
  • Secondary carpal tunnel syndrome (mass, tenosynovitis - directly visualized on ultrasound)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam and electrodiagnostic studies; ultrasound-guided injection or surgical referral for refractory cases.

4. Ganglion cyst, wrist/hand ultrasound

Benign

Common benign cystic lesion arising from a joint or tendon sheath, a classic and highly accurate ultrasound diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic cystic lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement, often with a visible stalk connecting to the adjacent joint or tendon sheath; avascular on Doppler.

Differential
  • Ganglion cyst (benign, extremely common)
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic; ultrasound-guided aspiration or hand surgery referral for excision if symptomatic.

5. Flexor tendon injury, ultrasound

Concerning

Tear or laceration of a flexor tendon.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the flexor tendon with a gap, sometimes with retraction visible on dynamic imaging with finger motion.

Differential
  • Complete flexor tendon laceration/rupture
  • Partial tendon injury
Next Step

Urgent hand surgery referral given the time-sensitive nature of tendon repair.

6. TFCC tear, ultrasound (limited role)

Variable

Tear of the triangular fibrocartilage complex; ultrasound has a more limited role than MRI/MR arthrography for this deep structure.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregularity or discontinuity of the TFCC may be seen, though ultrasound is generally considered less sensitive than MRI for this diagnosis given the structure's depth and complexity.

Differential
  • TFCC tear (MRI/MR arthrography generally preferred for definitive characterization)
Next Step

MRI or MR arthrography for more definitive TFCC assessment if ultrasound findings are equivocal.

7. Rheumatoid synovitis, wrist ultrasound

Concerning

Inflammatory synovial proliferation from rheumatoid arthritis, well detected by ultrasound including Doppler assessment of disease activity.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic synovial thickening (synovitis) with increased power Doppler signal correlating with disease activity, sometimes with adjacent bone erosions.

Differential
  • Rheumatoid arthritis synovitis (Doppler activity helps guide treatment response assessment)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; ultrasound can help monitor treatment response over time.

8. Foreign body, hand ultrasound

Variable

Retained foreign material within the hand soft tissues, a common and effective ultrasound application, particularly for radiolucent objects.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperechoic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing (reverberation artifact for smooth objects like glass/metal) or a comet-tail artifact, often with surrounding hypoechoic inflammatory reaction if retained for some time.

Differential
  • Retained foreign body (wood, glass, metal, plastic - ultrasound detects both radiopaque and radiolucent materials)
Next Step

Surgical/wound care referral for removal if symptomatic or accessible; ultrasound can help localize the object for guided removal.

Hip & Groin

6 findings

1. Trochanteric bursitis, ultrasound

Variable

Fluid distension and/or thickening of the greater trochanteric bursa, a common cause of lateral hip pain, often associated with gluteal tendinopathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to hypoechoic fluid collection overlying the greater trochanter, often with adjacent gluteus medius/minimus tendinopathy.

Differential
  • Greater trochanteric bursitis
  • Gluteal tendinopathy/tear (frequently coexists, sometimes termed greater trochanteric pain syndrome)
Next Step

Conservative management; ultrasound-guided injection for refractory cases.

2. Gluteus medius/minimus tendinopathy or tear, ultrasound

Concerning

Tendinopathy or tear of the gluteus medius/minimus at the greater trochanter, an important cause of chronic lateral hip pain.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening with hypoechoic change (tendinopathy) or a discrete hypoechoic/anechoic defect (tear) at the greater trochanteric insertion.

Differential
  • Gluteal tendinopathy
  • Partial or full-thickness gluteal tendon tear
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; conservative management vs. surgical repair based on tear size and symptoms.

3. Iliopsoas bursitis / snapping hip, ultrasound

Variable

Fluid distension of the iliopsoas bursa, or dynamic snapping of the iliopsoas tendon over the femoral head/iliopectineal eminence.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic fluid collection deep to the iliopsoas tendon (bursitis); dynamic imaging with hip flexion/extension can demonstrate the tendon snapping over a bony prominence (internal snapping hip).

Differential
  • Iliopsoas bursitis
  • Internal snapping hip syndrome (dynamic tendon snapping)
Next Step

Conservative management; ultrasound-guided bursa injection for symptomatic relief.

4. Hip joint effusion, ultrasound (pediatric)

Concerning

Fluid distension of the hip joint capsule, an important pediatric application given the ability to detect effusion without radiation and guide aspiration.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or hypoechoic fluid distending the anterior hip joint capsule, with capsular distension measured and compared to the contralateral side.

Differential
  • Transient synovitis (most common cause in children, typically simple/anechoic fluid)
  • Septic arthritis (correlate with clinical/laboratory findings; ultrasound cannot reliably distinguish infected from non-infected fluid by appearance alone)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical/laboratory prediction rules (Kocher criteria); ultrasound-guided aspiration if infection cannot be excluded clinically.

5. Athletic pubalgia, ultrasound (limited role)

Variable

Chronic groin pain from injury to the pubic symphysis-related musculotendinous structures; ultrasound has a more limited role than MRI for this deep, complex region.

Imaging Characteristics

Dynamic ultrasound may show a posterior inguinal wall bulge or weakness, though MRI is generally preferred for comprehensive assessment of this complex anatomic region.

Differential
  • Athletic pubalgia/sports hernia complex (MRI generally preferred for definitive characterization)
Next Step

MRI for more definitive assessment; sports medicine referral.

6. Meralgia paresthetica (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve)

Variable

Compression of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve near the anterior superior iliac spine, causing anterolateral thigh numbness/pain.

Imaging Characteristics

Nerve swelling/enlargement at or near its exit point medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, sometimes with an identifiable compressive cause (tight clothing, obesity, direct compression).

Differential
  • Meralgia paresthetica (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve entrapment)
Next Step

Conservative management (avoid compressive clothing, weight loss if relevant); ultrasound-guided nerve block for symptomatic relief or diagnostic confirmation.

Knee

7 findings

1. Baker (popliteal) cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Fluid distension of the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa, a classic and highly accurate ultrasound diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic (or complex if ruptured/hemorrhagic) cystic collection in the posteromedial knee, with a characteristic neck between the semimembranosus and medial gastrocnemius tendons.

Differential
  • Baker cyst (commonly associated with an underlying meniscal tear or osteoarthritis)
  • Ruptured Baker cyst (fluid tracking into the calf, can mimic DVT clinically)
Next Step

Treat the underlying intra-articular pathology; exclude DVT if clinical presentation suggests a ruptured cyst.

2. Knee joint effusion, ultrasound

Variable

Fluid distension of the knee joint, well assessed via the suprapatellar recess.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or hypoechoic fluid distending the suprapatellar recess, with synovial thickening/hyperemia suggesting a more inflammatory or infectious process.

Differential
  • Traumatic effusion
  • Osteoarthritis-related effusion
  • Inflammatory arthropathy
  • Septic arthritis (correlate with clinical infection signs)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; ultrasound-guided aspiration if infection is a concern.

3. Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee), ultrasound

Variable

Degenerative change of the patellar tendon at its proximal (patellar) insertion, common in jumping athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening with hypoechoic, heterogeneous echotexture at the proximal patellar tendon, sometimes with increased Doppler flow (neovascularity) correlating with symptom activity.

Differential
  • Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee)
Next Step

Conservative management (eccentric strengthening); ultrasound-guided treatments (dry needling, PRP) for refractory cases.

4. Quadriceps tendon tear, ultrasound

Concerning

Tear of the quadriceps tendon, well assessed by ultrasound given its superficial location.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the quadriceps tendon just proximal to the patella, with a hypoechoic gap and variable retraction if complete.

Differential
  • Complete quadriceps tendon tear
  • Partial tear/tendinopathy
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic referral for complete tears given the need for prompt surgical repair.

5. Medial/lateral collateral ligament injury, ultrasound

Variable

Injury to the MCL or LCL, assessable by ultrasound though MRI provides more comprehensive associated injury assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

Ligament thickening and hypoechoic change (sprain) or discontinuity (tear), with dynamic stress testing able to assess for gapping/instability.

Differential
  • MCL or LCL sprain/tear
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; MRI for comprehensive assessment of associated intra-articular injury.

6. Meniscal cyst, ultrasound

Variable

Cystic lesion associated with a horizontal meniscal tear, from extrusion of joint fluid through the tear.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often multiloculated anechoic to hypoechoic cystic lesion at the joint line, corresponding to the location of an underlying meniscal tear.

Differential
  • Meniscal cyst (associated with an underlying horizontal meniscal tear)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral; MRI to characterize the associated meniscal tear.

7. Prepatellar bursitis

Variable

Fluid distension and/or thickening of the prepatellar bursa, 'housemaid's knee,' from repetitive kneeling or direct trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to complex fluid collection overlying the patella, superficial to the patella itself (distinguishing it from a joint effusion), with internal debris/septations suggesting infection.

Differential
  • Traumatic/mechanical bursitis (simple fluid)
  • Septic bursitis (complex, hyperemic, warrants aspiration)
Next Step

Aspiration for fluid analysis if infection is suspected; conservative management for simple bursitis.

Ankle & Foot

9 findings

1. Achilles tendinopathy, ultrasound

Variable

Degenerative change of the Achilles tendon, a classic and highly accurate ultrasound diagnosis given superficial location.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening with hypoechoic, heterogeneous echotexture, at the insertion (insertional) or mid-substance (non-insertional), with neovascularity on Doppler correlating with symptom activity.

Differential
  • Insertional Achilles tendinopathy
  • Non-insertional (mid-substance) tendinopathy
Next Step

Conservative management (eccentric strengthening); ultrasound-guided treatments for refractory cases.

2. Achilles tendon tear, ultrasound

Concerning

Tear of the Achilles tendon, well assessed by ultrasound including dynamic testing.

Imaging Characteristics

Discontinuity of the tendon with a hypoechoic/anechoic gap, most often in the hypovascular zone 2-6 cm proximal to the calcaneal insertion; dynamic plantarflexion testing (Thompson test equivalent) can assess for residual continuity.

Differential
  • Complete Achilles tear
  • Partial Achilles tear
Next Step

Urgent orthopedic/sports medicine referral for complete tears given time-sensitive management decisions.

3. Plantar fasciitis, ultrasound

Variable

Degenerative/inflammatory change of the plantar fascia at its calcaneal origin, a common and accurate ultrasound diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickening (>4 mm) and hypoechoic change of the plantar fascia at its calcaneal origin, with loss of the normal fibrillar pattern.

Differential
  • Plantar fasciitis
  • Plantar fascia tear (more focal discontinuity, less common)
Next Step

Conservative management (stretching, orthotics); ultrasound-guided injection for refractory cases.

4. Morton neuroma, ultrasound

Variable

Perineural fibrosis of an interdigital nerve, most common at the third webspace, a classic and accurate ultrasound diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, hypoechoic, non-compressible mass-like lesion within the interdigital space, most often between the third and fourth metatarsal heads, reproducing symptoms with probe pressure (sonographic Mulder sign).

Differential
  • Morton neuroma (perineural fibrosis)
  • Intermetatarsal bursitis (can coexist or mimic)
Next Step

Conservative management (padding, ultrasound-guided injection); surgical excision for refractory cases.

5. Peroneal tendon pathology, ultrasound

Concerning

Tear or instability of the peroneal tendons, well assessed dynamically by ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Longitudinal split tear shows a hypoechoic cleft within the tendon (classically peroneus brevis); subluxation/dislocation is demonstrated dynamically with the tendon(s) displacing anteriorly out of the retromalleolar groove during ankle eversion/dorsiflexion.

Differential
  • Peroneal tendon split tear
  • Peroneal tendon subluxation/dislocation (dynamic assessment is a particular strength of ultrasound)
Next Step

Orthopedic/sports medicine referral.

6. Ankle ligament injury (ATFL/CFL), dynamic ultrasound

Variable

Injury to the lateral ankle ligament complex, assessable with dynamic stress ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Ligament thickening and hypoechoic change (sprain) or discontinuity (tear), with dynamic anterior drawer stress testing able to assess for instability.

Differential
  • ATFL sprain/tear (most common)
  • Combined ATFL/CFL injury
Next Step

Conservative management for isolated injury; orthopedic referral for chronic instability.

7. Retrocalcaneal bursitis

Variable

Fluid distension of the retrocalcaneal bursa, anterior to the Achilles tendon insertion, often associated with insertional Achilles tendinopathy or a Haglund deformity.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to hypoechoic fluid collection anterior to the distal Achilles tendon, at its calcaneal insertion.

Differential
  • Retrocalcaneal bursitis (often associated with insertional Achilles tendinopathy/Haglund deformity)
Next Step

Conservative management; ultrasound-guided injection for refractory symptoms.

8. Ganglion cyst, foot/ankle ultrasound

Benign

Common benign cystic lesion arising from a joint or tendon sheath in the foot/ankle.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic cystic lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement, often with a visible stalk connecting to an adjacent joint or tendon sheath.

Differential
  • Ganglion cyst (benign)
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic; ultrasound-guided aspiration or surgical referral if symptomatic.

9. Posterior tibial tendon pathology, ultrasound

Concerning

Degeneration or tear of the posterior tibial tendon, the leading cause of acquired adult flatfoot deformity.

Imaging Characteristics

Tendon thickening with hypoechoic change (tendinopathy) or a discrete hypoechoic/anechoic defect (tear), posterior to the medial malleolus.

Differential
  • Posterior tibial tendon tendinopathy/tear
Next Step

Orthopedic/podiatric referral; management depends on the stage of dysfunction.

Soft Tissue Masses & Miscellaneous

8 findings

1. Lipoma, ultrasound characterization

Benign

Common benign fat-containing soft-tissue tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, homogeneous or mildly heterogeneous, typically isoechoic to slightly hyperechoic mass relative to surrounding fat, with a thin capsule and minimal internal vascularity on Doppler.

Differential
  • Simple lipoma (benign)
  • Atypical lipomatous tumor/well-differentiated liposarcoma (thicker septa, more heterogeneous - key differential for atypical features)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous, superficial lipoma; MRI for further characterization of atypical or deep lesions.

2. Soft tissue abscess, ultrasound

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the soft tissues, with ultrasound distinguishing a drainable abscess from simple cellulitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete, complex, hypoechoic-to-anechoic fluid collection, often with internal debris and increased through-transmission, distinguishing it from the diffuse, non-drainable pattern of cellulitis.

Differential
  • Drainable abscess
  • Simple cellulitis (no discrete collection)
Next Step

Incision and drainage for a discrete abscess; antibiotics alone for cellulitis without a drainable collection.

3. Hematoma, soft tissue ultrasound

Variable

Blood collection within the soft tissues, from trauma, anticoagulation, or post-procedural bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Variable echogenicity depending on age (acute hematomas can be relatively echogenic, becoming more anechoic/complex as they liquefy over days to weeks), without internal vascularity.

Differential
  • Traumatic or spontaneous (anticoagulation-related) hematoma
Next Step

Correlate with anticoagulation status and size; drainage for large or symptomatic hematomas.

4. Foreign body, soft tissue ultrasound

Variable

Retained foreign material within the soft tissues, a highly effective ultrasound application, particularly for radiolucent objects like wood.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperechoic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing or reverberation/comet-tail artifact, often with a surrounding hypoechoic halo of inflammatory reaction if retained for some time.

Differential
  • Retained foreign body (wood, glass, metal, plastic)
Next Step

Surgical/wound care referral for removal if symptomatic; ultrasound can guide localization for removal.

5. Peripheral nerve sheath tumor, ultrasound

Variable

Benign or malignant tumor arising from a peripheral nerve.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform, hypoechoic mass in continuity with a nerve, with the nerve entering and exiting the mass being a key identifying feature.

Differential
  • Schwannoma (benign, eccentric to the nerve)
  • Neurofibroma (nerve passes through the tumor)
  • Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor (larger, more heterogeneous, irregular margins)
Next Step

MRI for further characterization; surgical referral for symptomatic or concerning lesions.

6. Vascular malformation, soft tissue ultrasound

Variable

Congenital vascular malformation (venous, lymphatic, arteriovenous, or mixed), characterized by ultrasound including Doppler flow assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

Venous malformation: compressible, low-flow, often with visible phleboliths. Lymphatic malformation: multiloculated cystic spaces without significant flow. Arteriovenous malformation: high-flow with arterialized venous waveforms on Doppler.

Differential
  • Venous malformation
  • Lymphatic malformation
  • Arteriovenous malformation
Next Step

Interventional radiology referral for symptomatic lesions (sclerotherapy or embolization depending on type).

7. Muscle tear, ultrasound grading

Variable

Muscle-tendon unit injury, graded by severity, well assessed dynamically by ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Grade 1: mild hyperechoic edema/fiber blurring without disruption. Grade 2: partial hypoechoic fiber disruption with hematoma. Grade 3: complete fiber discontinuity with a fluid-filled gap and muscle retraction.

Differential
  • Grade 1 strain (mild)
  • Grade 2 strain (partial tear)
  • Grade 3 strain (complete tear)
Next Step

Sports medicine referral; management and return-to-play timeline depend on grade.

8. Myositis ossificans, ultrasound (early)

Variable

Heterotopic bone formation within muscle following trauma, with ultrasound able to detect early peripheral mineralization before it is apparent on plain film.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined hypoechoic mass in the early phase, developing a peripheral echogenic rim with posterior shadowing (early mineralization) over 2-4 weeks, often visible on ultrasound before plain film.

Differential
  • Myositis ossificans (early peripheral mineralization on ultrasound can precede plain film findings)
  • Soft tissue sarcoma (early lesions can mimic before the characteristic peripheral maturation pattern develops)
Next Step

Serial imaging to confirm the characteristic peripheral maturation pattern if imaged early/atypically.

Pediatric-Specific

5 findings

1. Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH), ultrasound screening

Concerning

Screening ultrasound for hip dysplasia in infants, the preferred initial imaging modality before femoral head ossification (typically under 4-6 months of age).

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of the alpha angle (acetabular bony coverage) and beta angle (cartilaginous coverage), along with dynamic stress testing (Barlow/Ortolani maneuvers under ultrasound) for stability.

Differential
  • Normal hip (alpha angle ≥60 degrees)
  • Hip dysplasia (reduced alpha angle)
  • Hip subluxation/dislocation (dynamic instability on stress testing)
Next Step

Pediatric orthopedic referral for confirmed dysplasia; Pavlik harness or other treatment based on severity and age.

2. Transient synovitis, pediatric hip ultrasound

Variable

Self-limited, reactive hip joint effusion in children, often following a viral illness, the most common cause of pediatric hip pain/limp.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to mildly complex fluid distending the anterior hip joint capsule, without the more concerning features (marked complexity, adjacent bone changes) suggestive of infection.

Differential
  • Transient synovitis (self-limited, most common cause)
  • Septic arthritis (key differential requiring clinical/laboratory correlation, as ultrasound appearance alone cannot reliably distinguish)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical prediction rules (Kocher criteria: fever, non-weight-bearing, ESR, WBC) to help distinguish from septic arthritis; aspiration if infection cannot be excluded.

3. Osgood-Schlatter disease, ultrasound

Benign

Traction apophysitis of the tibial tubercle, assessable by ultrasound including patellar tendon changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Fragmentation and irregularity of the tibial tubercle apophysis with overlying soft-tissue swelling and patellar tendon thickening/hypoechogenicity near its insertion.

Differential
  • Osgood-Schlatter disease (self-limited, activity-related)
Next Step

Activity modification and symptomatic management; generally self-limited with skeletal maturity.

4. Nursemaid's elbow, ultrasound (limited role)

Benign

Radial head subluxation in young children, primarily a clinical diagnosis with a limited but emerging ultrasound role.

Imaging Characteristics

Some studies describe a hypoechoic or altered appearance of the annular ligament at the radiocapitellar joint, though this remains primarily a clinical diagnosis and treatment (reduction) without imaging confirmation.

Differential
  • Radial head subluxation (nursemaid's elbow, clinical diagnosis)
Next Step

Closed reduction based on clinical history and exam; imaging is generally unnecessary for classic presentations.

5. Pediatric fracture, ultrasound (point-of-care)

Variable

Point-of-care ultrasound detection of pediatric long bone fractures, an emerging application to reduce radiation exposure for select fractures (e.g., forearm, clavicle).

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical step-off or discontinuity, with an overlying subperiosteal hematoma often visible, particularly useful for superficial long bones like the clavicle, forearm, and lower leg.

Differential
  • Long bone fracture (ultrasound sensitivity varies by location and fracture type)
Next Step

Correlate with plain film when needed for definitive characterization and to guide orthopedic management, particularly for angulated or displaced fractures.

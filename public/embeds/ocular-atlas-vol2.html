Hyphema

The grade you assign on day one matters less than what you do about rebleed risk over the next five days — that window, not the initial exam, is where most of the bad outcomes happen.

Blunt trauma compresses the globe anteroposteriorly while expanding it equatorially, shearing the small vessels of the iris root, ciliary body face, and anterior chamber angle. Blood layers in the anterior chamber under gravity. The original clot begins to lyse/retract around day 2–5, which is precisely when the highest risk of secondary (re-bleed) hemorrhage occurs — and a rebleed carries meaningfully worse prognosis than the original bleed, both because more blood is now present and because the fragile healing vessels are disrupted again.

Sickle cell hemoglobinopathy (disease OR trait) is the single most important host factor that changes management: sickled red blood cells clog the trabecular meshwork more readily than normal cells, so even modest IOP elevations that would be tolerated in another patient can cause optic nerve ischemia and central retinal artery occlusion at pressures that seem unremarkable on paper.

Risk Factors
  • Blunt trauma: sports (ball sports, racquet sports), altercations/assault, airbag deployment, BB/pellet gun injuries
  • Sickle cell disease or sickle cell TRAIT (not just disease) — a critical and frequently under-recognized risk factor, particularly in patients of African descent, though trait status can occur in any ancestry and should not be assumed absent based on appearance
  • Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication use, or an underlying bleeding diathesis (hemophilia, von Willebrand disease)
  • Intraocular surgery (postoperative hyphema) or neovascularization from chronic ischemic retinal disease (spontaneous/non-traumatic hyphema)
Fundoscopic Findings

Often difficult or impossible to visualize acutely if the hyphema is large, since blood in the anterior chamber blocks the view to the posterior segment. Once enough clearing has occurred (or via B-scan ultrasound if needed sooner), specifically look for other traumatic findings that commonly co-occur: commotio retinae, retinal tears/detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, and choroidal rupture — a hyphema is a marker of significant blunt force and should prompt a search for these associated injuries once view allows.

History
  • Precise mechanism and force of injury; time since injury.
  • Ask specifically about sickle cell disease or trait, and anticoagulant/antiplatelet medication use or known bleeding disorders — this changes the entire management plan.
  • Ancestry/ethnicity should prompt consideration of sickle cell screening but should never be used to assume trait status is absent; screen based on clinical suspicion and shared decision-making, not appearance alone.
Exam
  • Visual acuity, pupil exam (check for an associated traumatic mydriasis/iridodialysis), and grade the hyphema by estimated percentage of anterior chamber volume filled with layered blood (Grade I: <1/3, Grade II: 1/3–1/2, Grade III: >1/2, Grade IV: total/'eight-ball').
  • Tonometry — document the exact IOP.
  • Full slit lamp exam for associated findings: corneal blood staining (blood pigment infiltrating the cornea, generally only with prolonged total hyphema), lens subluxation, iridodialysis.
  • Defer gonioscopy and dilated fundus exam until after the acute high-rebleed-risk window (roughly the first 4–5 days) unless urgently needed, since the additional manipulation carries theoretical rebleed risk.
Imaging/Labs
  • Sickle cell prep/hemoglobin electrophoresis in patients where sickle hemoglobinopathy is a reasonable consideration — many ophthalmologists recommend this be considered broadly given how much it changes management, rather than restricted narrowly by presumed ancestry.
  • CT orbit if the mechanism or exam raises concern for an associated orbital fracture or open globe.
1
Shield and restrict activity

Rigid eye shield (not a pressure patch), strict limitation of physical activity, head of bed elevated approximately 30 degrees to allow blood to layer inferiorly and away from the visual axis, and no reading (accommodation has been thought to stress already-injured vessels).

⏰ Immediately
2
Control pain and nausea without platelet-affecting agents

Use acetaminophen-based analgesia; specifically AVOID aspirin and NSAIDs, which impair platelet function and can worsen bleeding/rebleed risk. Treat nausea proactively, since vomiting raises IOP and can precipitate rebleeding.

⏰ Immediately
3
Cycloplege for comfort

A cycloplegic (e.g., cyclopentolate or homatropine) reduces ciliary spasm and photophobia-related discomfort.

⏰ Same visit
4
Manage IOP if elevated, avoiding sickle-cell-unsafe agents

Topical beta-blockers and alpha-agonists are generally safe. AVOID carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide, dorzolamide) in sickle cell disease or trait, since the resulting increase in aqueous humor acidity promotes further red blood cell sickling. Avoid hyperosmotics as first-line given limited evidence of benefit and volume-shift risk; reserve for refractory elevated IOP per ophthalmology guidance.

⏰ As needed, ongoing
5
Do NOT routinely use antifibrinolytics

Systemic or topical aminocaproic acid/tranexamic acid was historically used to reduce rebleed risk, but trial evidence has not shown a reduction in rebleeding rates or a faster time to resolution, and current guidance no longer routinely recommends them even in higher-risk patients (sickle cell, bleeding disorders). Reserve for selected high-risk cases per ophthalmology judgment rather than using by default.

⏰ N/A — a default-avoid, not a default-give
6
Risk-stratify for admission vs. outpatient management

Consider admission/close monitoring for: Grade III–IV hyphema, known sickle cell disease/trait, bleeding disorders, uncontrolled IOP, poor follow-up reliability or unsafe social situation, or inability to comply with activity restriction (notably young children). Otherwise, reliable patients with Grade I–II hyphema and normal IOP can often be managed as outpatients with daily ophthalmology follow-up through the highest-risk window.

⏰ At disposition decision
7
Daily follow-up through the high-risk window

Whether inpatient or outpatient, visual acuity, IOP, and hyphema size should be reassessed daily for at least the first 4–5 days, since this is the window in which secondary hemorrhage most often occurs.

⏰ Days 1–5 post-injury
8
Escalate to surgical evacuation if criteria are met

Indications include: IOP ≥50 mmHg for more than 5 days (or ≥25 mmHg for more than 24 hours specifically in sickle cell disease/trait, given the lower ischemic threshold), early corneal blood staining, persistent large/total hyphema without adequate clearing, or continued active bleeding.

⏰ As criteria are met, typically after the initial 4-day trial of medical management
🔧 Procedure: Anterior Chamber Washout (performed by ophthalmology)
  1. Reserved for eyes meeting surgical criteria: uncontrolled IOP despite maximal medical therapy, early corneal blood staining, or a persistent large/total hyphema.
  2. Performed in the OR; the anterior chamber is irrigated to remove clot/blood, sometimes with additional maneuvers depending on findings (e.g., addressing an underlying cause of ongoing bleeding).
Contraindications:
  • This is an ophthalmology-performed procedure; the ED's role is recognizing the indication and arranging timely surgical evaluation, not performing the washout.

Complications to Anticipate
  • Secondary (re-bleed) hemorrhage, occurring in a meaningful minority of cases (reported around 30% in some series) and generally worsening prognosis
  • Elevated IOP / secondary glaucoma, especially in sickle hemoglobinopathy where even modest pressure elevations can cause optic nerve or central retinal artery ischemia
  • Corneal blood staining (uncommon, but more likely with prolonged total/Grade IV hyphema)
  • Angle recession glaucoma presenting months to years later, a distinct long-term entity from the acute IOP elevation
Clinical Pearls
  • Ask about sickle cell trait, not just sickle cell disease — trait status alone has been shown to meaningfully increase the risk of secondary hemorrhage and pressure-related complications.
  • Antifibrinolytics have fallen out of routine use after trial evidence failed to show reduced rebleeding or faster resolution — don't reach for aminocaproic acid/tranexamic acid by default even in a 'high-risk-looking' hyphema.
  • Uncontrolled IOP thresholds differ by patient: ≥50 mmHg for 5+ days in general, but only ≥25 mmHg for 24+ hours is considered uncontrolled in sickle cell disease/trait — document which threshold applies to a given patient.
Evidence Notes
  • A 2001 comprehensive review and subsequent controlled trials of antifibrinolytic therapy for traumatic hyphema failed to demonstrate reduced rebleeding rates or faster time to resolution, leading current guidance (StatPearls, EyeWiki) to no longer routinely recommend these agents even in higher-risk patients.
  • A pediatric cohort study found secondary hemorrhage in 14% of African-American children with traumatic hyphema versus 0% in white children in that series, but when sickle-cell-trait-positive children were excluded from the African-American group, the remaining sickle-trait-negative children (both groups combined) had no secondary hemorrhages — supporting sickle trait, not race per se, as the operative risk factor.

Blowout / Orbital Fractures

The pediatric 'white-eyed' variant is the one that gets missed: a nearly normal-looking eye with a surgical emergency underneath, because the bone is springy enough in children to trap muscle without leaving the bruising that would otherwise tip you off.

Blunt force to the globe or orbital rim transmits pressure to the thinnest bony walls of the orbit (floor, over the maxillary sinus, and medial wall, over the ethmoid sinuses), causing them to fracture outward ('blow out'). Orbital soft tissue — fat, and sometimes the inferior or medial rectus muscle itself — can herniate into the adjacent sinus and become entrapped in the fracture line.

In children and adolescents, the orbital bone is more elastic and prone to a 'trapdoor' or 'greenstick' pattern: the bone flexes open, tissue herniates through, and the bone springs back to its original position, trapping the herniated tissue in a linear fracture that may be subtle or even invisible on some imaging cuts. Because there's often minimal associated soft tissue injury, external bruising/swelling can be minimal or absent — the 'white-eyed blowout fracture' (WEBOF) — which is exactly what makes it dangerous: the eye looks reassuring while a muscle is ischemic underneath.

Risk Factors
  • Blunt facial trauma: sports (ball-to-orbit impact from baseballs, tennis balls, hockey pucks — objects roughly the diameter of the orbital rim transmit force efficiently into the orbit), altercations/assault, falls, motor vehicle collisions
  • Pediatric and adolescent age specifically for the white-eyed/trapdoor variant, due to greater bone elasticity
  • Male sex predominance in most trauma series
Fundoscopic Findings

Not typically abnormal from the fracture itself; perform a dilated fundus exam to evaluate for a co-occurring globe injury (the same blunt force can cause commotio retinae, hyphema, or retinal detachment) rather than expecting fracture-specific fundus findings. Extraocular movement restriction and pain, not fundus findings, are the key exam findings driving fracture-related management decisions.

History
  • Mechanism and exact object/force involved (helps gauge likelihood of fracture vs. simple contusion).
  • In pediatric patients: specifically ask about and examine for nausea, vomiting, bradycardia-associated symptoms, and pain with eye movement — these can be misattributed to head injury/increased ICP when they actually reflect oculocardiac reflex from muscle entrapment.
Exam
  • Visual acuity, pupil exam, IOP (to help exclude a coexisting orbital compartment syndrome).
  • Extraocular movements: specifically test upgaze (inferior rectus entrapment, orbital floor fracture) and lateral gaze (medial rectus entrapment, medial wall fracture); note whether restriction is due to mechanical entrapment (sudden hard stop) versus simple pain-limited movement.
  • Infraorbital nerve distribution sensation (cheek, upper lip, upper teeth) — hypoesthesia suggests infraorbital canal involvement.
  • Enophthalmos or globe malposition (may not be apparent acutely due to swelling; more reliable on delayed exam).
  • Palpate for step-off deformity, and check for orbital/subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus) — instruct the patient NOT to blow their nose, since this can force air/sinus contents into the orbit and worsen emphysema or theoretically propagate infection.
  • In children, explicitly look for the white-eyed picture: minimal bruising/swelling despite significant motility restriction and nausea/vomiting — this combination should raise, not lower, your index of suspicion.
Imaging/Labs
  • CT orbit/facial bones, thin cuts, with coronal reconstructions — the study of choice to identify fracture location, size, and whether orbital contents (fat and/or muscle) are herniated or entrapped.
  • Forced duction testing (typically performed by ophthalmology) can confirm mechanical entrapment when the exam is ambiguous, though a positive clinical/motility exam plus imaging is often sufficient to proceed.
1
Assess for and manage any coexisting sight-threatening injury first

Orbital fractures can coexist with globe rupture, traumatic optic neuropathy, or orbital compartment syndrome — rule these out (or manage them emergently) before focusing on the fracture itself.

⏰ Immediately
2
Recognize the pediatric white-eyed pattern specifically

Nausea, vomiting, bradycardia, pain/restriction with vertical gaze, and a deceptively unremarkable external exam in a child or adolescent should prompt urgent orbital CT and ophthalmology consultation — do not let a benign-appearing external exam lower suspicion, and do not misattribute these symptoms to head injury/increased ICP without an eye-specific exam.

⏰ On recognition
3
Obtain CT and determine entrapment status

CT with coronal cuts identifies fracture size/location and whether orbital fat and/or extraocular muscle is herniated/entrapped versus simply prolapsed without true entrapment.

⏰ Promptly on presentation
4
Emergent surgical referral for entrapment/oculocardiac reflex

True muscle entrapment (especially with a positive oculocardiac reflex, persistent diplopia, or a positive forced duction test) is a surgical emergency in children — published series recommend surgical release within 24–48 hours, with some data suggesting even better outcomes when performed within 24 hours. The younger the child, the more urgent the timeline, given faster onset of ischemic muscle damage and fibrosis.

⏰ Within 24–48 hours if entrapment confirmed (sooner is better, especially in younger children)
5
Conservative management if no entrapment

For fractures without entrapment and without significant enophthalmos, diplopia, or large fracture size: ice packs, head of bed elevation, oral antibiotics (sinus communication risk), avoidance of nose blowing/Valsalva, and short-course oral steroids in some protocols to reduce edema. Ophthalmology/oculoplastics follow-up within days, not emergently.

⏰ Ongoing, days to weeks
6
Identify other delayed surgical indications

Even without acute entrapment, surgery may be indicated for: enophthalmos greater than 2mm, large fracture (>50% of orbital floor), persistent symptomatic diplopia, or no improvement after several weeks of conservative management.

⏰ Assessed over the following 1–2 weeks

  • Persistent diplopia/strabismus from muscle ischemia, fibrosis, or scarring if entrapment is not released promptly
  • Enophthalmos (posterior globe displacement) from bony volume expansion, often more apparent once acute swelling resolves
  • Infraorbital nerve hypoesthesia (may be transient or persistent)
  • Orbital cellulitis (uncommon, related to sinus communication)
Clinical Pearls
  • A 'white-eyed' appearance in a child with eye pain, nausea, and restricted upgaze is not reassuring — it's the classic presentation of the highest-urgency variant of this injury, and it is frequently misdiagnosed as a head injury or gastroenteritis before someone finally examines the eye movements directly.
  • Instruct every orbital fracture patient not to blow their nose — this simple instruction prevents iatrogenic worsening of orbital emphysema.
  • The timing literature increasingly favors within-24-hours repair for confirmed pediatric entrapment where feasible, rather than the traditionally quoted 24–48 hour window, given better reported outcomes with earlier release.
Evidence Notes
  • Multiple case series and reviews (Yew et al. 2015; multiple pediatric case reports 2019–2022) document white-eyed blowout fractures being repeatedly misdiagnosed as head injury/increased ICP due to nausea, vomiting, and lethargy overlapping with both presentations — a dedicated ocular motility exam is what distinguishes them.
  • Comparative surgical timing data (Gerbino et al.) found better outcomes with repair under 24 hours from injury compared to the more commonly cited 48-hour window, particularly in pediatric trapdoor fractures.

Corneal / Intraocular Foreign Body

The rust ring you're tempted to chase all the way clean at 2am can usually wait until tomorrow — the tissue softens overnight, making complete removal easier and lower-risk for the ophthalmologist the next day than for you tonight.

A superficial corneal foreign body (metal, wood, glass, insect parts, vegetable matter) lodges in the corneal epithelium or anterior stroma, most often from grinding, hammering, or wind-blown debris. Metallic foreign bodies, particularly iron/steel, begin oxidizing within hours, depositing a brownish-orange rust ring in the surrounding stroma that itself becomes a source of ongoing irritation and delayed healing even after the primary object is removed.

The critical branch point is distinguishing a superficial/corneal foreign body from a penetrating injury with an intraocular foreign body (IOFB) — the historical mechanism (high-velocity metal-on-metal, such as hammering a metal object or grinding) should raise suspicion for IOFB even when the entry wound is subtle, since a small, self-sealing corneal entry can easily be overlooked on routine exam.

Risk Factors
  • Occupational exposure without eye protection: grinding, hammering metal-on-metal, woodworking, lawn care/weed-trimming, welding (arc/UV keratitis risk as well)
  • Recreational/DIY activities involving metal-on-metal contact or power tools
  • Wind/debris exposure (motorcycling, cycling, farming) for organic/particulate foreign bodies
  • Contact lens wear as a compounding factor for infection risk if a foreign body or resulting abrasion is present
Fundoscopic Findings

Not relevant for a truly superficial corneal foreign body with an intact globe. If any history suggests a possible penetrating mechanism (metal-on-metal, explosion, high-velocity projectile), fundus exam should be deferred in favor of urgent CT orbit and ophthalmology evaluation to exclude an intraocular foreign body and open globe, rather than attempting a dilated exam that could miss a subtle posterior entry site.

History
  • Specific mechanism: what was being done (grinding, hammering, weed-trimming, etc.), what material, and whether any high-velocity component was involved (metal-on-metal strikes classically generate small, fast metallic fragments capable of penetrating the globe with a deceptively small/subtle entry wound).
  • Use of eye protection at the time of injury.
  • Timing (rust ring formation is time-dependent, generally becoming visible after several hours if the object is metallic and left in place).
Exam
  • Visual acuity, external inspection, and slit lamp exam under magnification to localize the foreign body and assess depth.
  • Fluorescein staining to delineate any associated epithelial defect once foreign body location is known (remove any visible foreign body before staining if it would obscure the view).
  • Explicitly screen for signs suggesting a penetrating injury/IOFB before proceeding with routine removal: hyphema, irregular or peaked pupil, positive Seidel sign, shallow or asymmetric anterior chamber, decreased vision out of proportion to what a superficial foreign body would cause.
  • Double-evert the upper lid to check for a sub-tarsal foreign body if vertical linear corneal abrasions ('ice-rink sign') are seen, since these are classically caused by a foreign body trapped under the upper lid dragging across the cornea with each blink.
Imaging/Labs
  • CT orbit (thin cuts) if any suspicion of a penetrating mechanism or IOFB — do not rely on exam alone to exclude this given how subtle entry wounds can be.
  • Avoid MRI if a metallic IOFB is possible (magnetic field risk) until ferromagnetic status is clarified.
1
Rule out penetrating injury/IOFB before proceeding with removal

If mechanism or exam findings raise any suspicion for a penetrating injury (see exam red flags above), stop and manage as an open globe: shield, no further manipulation, CT orbit, emergent ophthalmology consultation.

⏰ Before any FB removal attempt
2
Instill topical anesthetic

Facilitates comfortable examination and removal; not to be dispensed for home use.

⏰ Before removal
3
Remove superficial foreign body

Use a sterile needle, spud, or cotton-tip applicator under slit lamp magnification for superficial/loosely adherent material; very superficial debris can sometimes simply be irrigated or wiped away. Approach tangentially, anchoring your hand against the patient's cheek/face to minimize risk if the patient moves.

⏰ Same visit
4
Decide on rust ring management

A rust ring does NOT need to be fully removed in the ED if the patient can be seen by ophthalmology within about 24 hours — the surrounding tissue softens overnight, making removal easier and lower-risk the next day. If ophthalmology follow-up isn't readily available, gentle partial removal with a low-speed rotary burr (e.g., Alger brush) under magnification is reasonable, but avoid aggressive removal that risks deeper stromal scarring.

⏰ Same visit or deferred ~24h to ophthalmology
5
Post-removal antibiotic prophylaxis

Broad-spectrum topical antibiotic (fluoroquinolone preferred) at least QID until re-epithelialization; ensure Pseudomonas coverage specifically for contact lens wearers or organic/vegetative foreign bodies.

⏰ Starting same visit, continue until healed
6
Cycloplegic and comfort measures as needed

Short-acting cycloplegic (cyclopentolate/homatropine) for significant photophobia/ciliary spasm; avoid pressure patching, particularly with contact-lens-related or organic foreign bodies given increased infection risk with patching.

⏰ Same visit
7
Arrange follow-up

Reevaluate within the first week (sooner, ~24h, if rust ring was left in place or if the defect is large/deep) to confirm re-epithelialization and screen for infection.

⏰ 24h–1 week depending on findings
🔧 Procedure: Corneal Foreign Body and Rust Ring Removal
  1. Confirm no signs of penetrating injury before proceeding (see exam red flags).
  2. Instill topical anesthetic; position the patient at the slit lamp with head stabilized.
  3. Using a 25–27 gauge needle or commercial spud, approach the foreign body tangentially (parallel to the corneal surface, not perpendicular) with your hand braced against the patient's cheek/face for stability.
  4. Gently lift or flick the foreign body free; avoid excessive digging, which risks deeper stromal injury.
  5. If a rust ring remains and same/next-day ophthalmology follow-up is available, it is reasonable to leave it and let it soften overnight rather than aggressively pursuing complete removal acutely.
  6. If removal is pursued, a low-speed rotary burr (Alger brush) can be used gently under magnification; stop once the majority of rust is cleared rather than chasing every last fleck, which increases scarring risk.
  7. Reassess the epithelial defect size/depth and perform a final Seidel test if the defect is deep, to exclude an unrecognized full-thickness injury.
Contraindications:
  • Suspected penetrating injury/IOFB — do not attempt removal; manage as open globe instead.
  • Foreign body appears to extend deep to Bowman's layer or centrally through the visual axis — refer to ophthalmology rather than risking perforation or central scarring.

Clinical Pearls
  • A rust ring does not need to be fully chased out in the middle of the night — leaving a same/next-day ophthalmology follow-up plan is entirely reasonable and often produces a better cosmetic/scarring outcome than an aggressive ED removal attempt.

  • A history of hammering metal-on-metal deserves a specifically higher index of suspicion for IOFB regardless of how benign the visible corneal surface looks, since these fragments are small, fast, and can leave a nearly imperceptible entry wound.

Corneal Abrasion

The patching habit many of us learned in training has been walked back by the evidence — patches don't speed healing or reduce pain, and they may raise infection risk, particularly in exactly the populations (contact lens wearers, organic material) where it matters most.

A defect in the corneal epithelium from direct trauma (fingernail, tree branch, paper edge, contact lens insertion/removal, foreign body) exposes the underlying basement membrane and superficial stroma, producing significant pain disproportionate to the injury's small size due to the cornea's dense sensory innervation. Uncomplicated epithelial defects covering less than about 60% of the corneal surface typically heal within 1–4 days; larger or deeper defects can take up to a week.

Risk Factors
  • Contact lens wear (both the mechanical trauma of insertion/removal and, more importantly, the substantially elevated risk of Pseudomonas superinfection if an abrasion occurs while lenses are worn)
  • Foreign body exposure (see Corneal Foreign Body entry) — abrasion frequently co-occurs
  • Occupational/recreational trauma: fingernail scratches (common in caregivers of infants/young children), tree branches, paper cuts, sports
  • Recurrent corneal erosion syndrome as a distinct but related entity in patients with a history of prior abrasion, particularly at the same location, presenting with pain on waking
Fundoscopic Findings

Not relevant to an isolated corneal abrasion with an intact globe; fundus exam is only pursued if the mechanism raises concern for a co-occurring globe injury (e.g., a foreign-body-associated abrasion where penetrating injury hasn't been excluded).

History
  • Mechanism specifically, since it changes management: contact lens-associated (Pseudomonas risk), organic/vegetative material (fungal risk), fingernail/blunt (lowest infection risk), or foreign-body-associated (see that entry).
  • Contact lens wear status and lens type/hygiene practices if relevant.
Exam
  • Visual acuity before and after fluorescein instillation (documents baseline and confirms improvement with treatment at follow-up).
  • Fluorescein staining under cobalt blue light: a corneal abrasion stains as a well-demarcated area of uptake; a dendritic branching pattern instead suggests HSV keratitis, which changes management entirely (steroids are contraindicated).
  • Double-evert the upper lid, especially with vertical linear staining patterns, to exclude a retained sub-tarsal foreign body.
  • Assess pupil, anterior chamber, and IOP to exclude a coexisting traumatic iritis or more significant injury.
Imaging/Labs
  • None required for an isolated, uncomplicated abrasion; this is a clinical, fluorescein-based diagnosis.
1
Confirm the diagnosis and exclude mimics/complications first

Rule out a dendritic pattern (HSV keratitis), a retained foreign body (evert the lid), and any sign of infection (infiltrate, significant anterior chamber reaction) before treating as a simple abrasion.

⏰ At initial exam
2
Do not pressure patch

Current evidence does not support pressure patching for corneal abrasions — it does not speed healing or reduce pain, and may increase infection risk, particularly for contact-lens-related or organic-material abrasions. Avoid patching as a default; it is rarely, if ever, indicated.

⏰ At time of treatment decision
3
Start prophylactic topical antibiotics

Broad-spectrum topical antibiotic (fluoroquinolone preferred, e.g., ofloxacin or moxifloxacin) QID until re-epithelialized. For contact lens wearers or any organic/vegetative mechanism, ensure Pseudomonas coverage specifically and advise discontinuing contact lens wear until fully healed and cleared by follow-up.

⏰ Same visit, continue until healed
4
Cycloplegic for larger/more symptomatic abrasions

A short-acting cycloplegic (cyclopentolate) can reduce ciliary spasm-related pain for larger defects; not necessary for small, minimally symptomatic abrasions.

⏰ Same visit if indicated
5
Pain control without dispensing topical anesthetic

Oral analgesia (acetaminophen, NSAIDs if no contraindication) for comfort. Do NOT dispense topical anesthetic drops for home/repeated use — repeated use delays healing and can mask a worsening infection, and has been associated with severe complications including corneal melting with chronic misuse.

⏰ Same visit and discharge instructions
6
Consider a bandage contact lens for select larger defects

⏰ Selected cases only

Traumatic Iritis

The management overlaps heavily with infectious/inflammatory anterior uveitis, but the trigger is mechanical, not immune or infectious — which means the real job is confirming there isn't a bigger injury (hyphema, angle recession, lens subluxation) hiding underneath the same blunt mechanism.

Blunt force to the globe causes direct mechanical irritation and inflammation of the iris and ciliary body, producing the same anterior chamber cell-and-flare picture seen in infectious/autoimmune anterior uveitis, but from a mechanical rather than immune trigger. Ciliary muscle spasm drives the deep, aching pain and photophobia; ciliary body trauma can also transiently affect aqueous production, causing IOP to be either lower (ciliary shutdown) or higher (trabecular meshwork debris/angle injury) than baseline depending on the specific structures affected.

Risk Factors
  • Blunt ocular trauma of essentially any mechanism sufficient to cause a contusion injury (sports, assault, projectile, motor vehicle)
  • Frequently co-occurs with hyphema, angle recession, traumatic mydriasis/miosis, and lens subluxation from the same blunt force — its presence should prompt a careful search for these associated injuries rather than being treated as an isolated diagnosis
Fundoscopic Findings

Not typically abnormal from the iritis itself; as with hyphema, the presence of traumatic iritis should prompt a dilated fundus exam once view allows to specifically exclude commotio retinae, retinal tears/detachment, and vitreous hemorrhage from the same blunt mechanism.

History
  • Mechanism and timing of blunt trauma; associated symptoms of deep aching pain, photophobia, and blurred vision.
  • Screen for symptoms suggesting a co-occurring more serious injury: any history of a 'shower of floaters,' a curtain/shadow in vision (detachment), or visibly red/pooled blood in the anterior chamber (hyphema).
Exam
  • Visual acuity, pupil exam (traumatic mydriasis/miosis or an irregular pupil from sphincter tears is common and distinct from the pupillary findings of infectious uveitis).
  • Slit lamp exam for cell and flare in the anterior chamber, perilimbal flush, and consensual photophobia (pain in the traumatized eye when light is shone in the opposite eye).
  • IOP measurement — can be low (ciliary body shutdown) or elevated (angle/trabecular meshwork injury or debris); document which pattern is present.
  • Careful search for hyphema (even trace/microhyphema), lens subluxation (iridodonesis, asymmetric anterior chamber depth), and gonioscopy for angle recession once safe to perform (typically deferred if hyphema is also present, per that entry's timing).
Imaging/Labs
  • None required specifically for iritis; imaging is directed at excluding associated injuries (orbital fracture, globe rupture) suggested by the mechanism or exam.
1
Confirm the diagnosis and exclude associated injury

Full slit lamp exam to document cell/flare, rule out hyphema, assess pupil and lens position, and check IOP before treating as isolated iritis.

⏰ At initial exam
2
Cycloplege for pain relief and to prevent synechiae

A cycloplegic (cyclopentolate or homatropine, sometimes a longer-acting agent like atropine for more severe cases per ophthalmology preference) relieves ciliary spasm pain and reduces the risk of the iris adhering to the lens (posterior synechiae) during the inflammatory period.

⏰ Same visit, continued through the inflammatory course
3
Topical corticosteroid to control inflammation

Prednisolone acetate 1%, dosed frequently initially (e.g., every 1–4 hours depending on severity) and tapered over 1–2 weeks as inflammation resolves. As with any topical steroid use in the eye, confirm there is no dendritic/HSV keratitis or other infectious process present first.

⏰ Starting same visit, tapered over 1–2 weeks
4
Monitor and treat IOP as needed

If IOP is elevated (trabecular meshwork injury/debris), manage with topical beta-blockers/alpha-agonists as needed; if IOP is low from ciliary shutdown, this generally resolves as inflammation improves and doesn't require specific treatment beyond treating the underlying iritis.

⏰ Ongoing, reassessed at follow-up
5
Arrange ophthalmology follow-up

Close follow-up (days, not weeks) to confirm the inflammation is responding to therapy, appropriately taper steroids, and perform gonioscopy for angle recession once any coexisting hyphema has cleared the high-rebleed-risk window.

Lid Lacerations (Including Canalicular Injury)

The lacerations that look the most 'fixable' at bedside are sometimes exactly the ones that shouldn't be closed there — any hint of lid margin, canalicular, or levator involvement changes this from a laceration repair into an oculoplastics case.

Eyelid lacerations are best thought of in three tiers of increasing complexity: (1) skin-only lacerations without margin or deep structure involvement, generally suitable for standard ED/primary repair; (2) lacerations involving the lid margin, levator/superior rectus complex, orbital septum (with visible fat prolapse signaling septum penetration), or canthal tendons, which need oculoplastics/ophthalmology repair; and (3) lacerations involving the lacrimal drainage system (canaliculi), concentrated at the medial canthus because that region lacks the tarsal plate's structural support and is where the eyelid's attachment to the medial canthal tendon and lacrimal bone can avulse under shearing forces.

Dog bites are a classically overrepresented mechanism for canalicular lacerations specifically, because the blunt shearing/traction forces of a bite concentrate at exactly this structurally vulnerable medial attachment point. Canalicular lacerations are present in a meaningful minority of all eyelid lacerations from ocular trauma broadly.

Risk Factors
  • Dog bites (classic and disproportionate cause of canalicular/medial canthal injury specifically)
  • Sharp trauma (knife, glass) for direct lid margin/canalicular lacerations
  • Blunt trauma with shearing/traction mechanism (falls, assault) for indirect canalicular avulsion injuries
  • Any periorbital laceration close to the medial canthus, regardless of how trivial it appears, given the disproportionate rate of occult canalicular involvement in this location
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable to the laceration itself; a dilated or at least careful posterior exam should still be pursued once globe injury has been excluded, since the same mechanism (particularly blunt/shearing trauma) can cause a co-occurring globe or orbital injury.

History
  • Mechanism (sharp vs. blunt/shearing, dog bite specifically), time since injury, tetanus status, and last oral intake if surgical repair may be needed.
Exam
  • Resist the urge to focus immediately on the obvious laceration — perform a complete ophthalmic exam first, avoiding any pressure on the underlying globe until an open globe injury has been definitively excluded.
  • Assess for lid margin involvement: notching, step-off, or misalignment of the gray line/meibomian gland orifices.
  • Assess for levator/superior rectus involvement: new ptosis or restricted upgaze.
  • Look specifically for visible orbital fat in the wound, which signals septum penetration and an orbital-communicating injury.
  • Any laceration near the medial canthus, however trivial-appearing, should raise suspicion for canalicular involvement: look for laxity, rounding, or pouting of the punctum, and perform lacrimal probing and irrigation to confirm patency and identify the location of any canalicular transection.
  • Assess canthal tendon integrity: sudden-onset ectropion, rounding, or displacement of the canthal angle suggests canthal tendon avulsion.
Imaging/Labs
  • CT orbit if there's any suspicion of a co-occurring orbital fracture, retained foreign body, or globe injury given the mechanism.
  • Consider toxicology/alcohol screening in select patients if general anesthesia for repair is anticipated.

⏰ At initial assessment

⏰ Early in the encounter

⏰ Before beginning repair

Canalicular lacerations are optimally repaired within about 24–48 hours to restore normal lacrimal drainage physiology and minimize chronic epiphora risk, though evidence suggests acceptable outcomes can still be achieved with repair delayed up to about a week in appropriately selected, confined injuries. Repair involves identifying both cut ends of the canaliculus, canalicular reapproximation over a stent (mono- or bicanalicular), and anatomic restoration of lid margin position.

  • Visible orbital fat in a lid wound means the orbital septum has been violated — this converts a 'simple' laceration into one needing oculoplastics involvement, since it signals a deeper injury than the skin appearance suggests.

Commotio Retinae (Berlin's Edema)

The best thing you can do for this diagnosis is correctly recognize that it needs no specific treatment of its own — and then make sure you've actually excluded the other, less benign injuries that share the same blunt mechanism and can masquerade alongside it.

Blunt trauma displaces and compresses the globe, generating a shockwave that transmits posteriorly and mechanically disrupts the photoreceptor outer segments and retinal pigment epithelium, most classically at a site distant from the direct impact (contrecoup) though it can also occur at the impact site (coup). Despite the traditional name 'edema,' modern imaging (OCT) has shown the retinal whitening is due to disruption/disorganization of the photoreceptor outer segments and RPE rather than true extracellular fluid accumulation.

The condition is common, present in an estimated 30% (with some series reporting up to 90%) of eyes with significant blunt trauma, and is usually self-limited, resolving over roughly 3–4 weeks. Prognosis correlates with location and severity: extramacular involvement carries an excellent prognosis (roughly 95% achieving 20/30 or better), while macular involvement is more variable (roughly 74% achieving 20/30 or better, with the remainder experiencing some persistent visual deficit).

Risk Factors
  • High-impact sports, particularly ball sports (the object's size relative to the orbit matters — a ball roughly the diameter of the orbital rim transmits force efficiently into the globe)
  • Assault/violence
  • Motor vehicle collisions and other blunt facial/orbital trauma
  • Young male predominance in most reported series, reflecting the epidemiology of blunt ocular trauma generally
Fundoscopic Findings

The defining finding: an area of retinal whitening/opacification, classically described as a grayish-white discoloration, at the site of trauma (coup) or a distant site such as the macula (contrecoup). Retinal blood vessels remain visible through the whitened area, distinguishing it from more severe ischemic whitening. When the macula is involved (Berlin's edema proper), a contrasting cherry-red-appearing fovea can be seen against the surrounding whitened retina, similar in appearance to (but mechanistically distinct from) the cherry-red spot of CRAO. Look specifically for associated findings from the same blunt mechanism: choroidal rupture (a crescentic yellow-white subretinal streak, usually concentric to the disc), retinal hemorrhage, or signs of a retinal tear/detachment.

History
  • Mechanism and timing of blunt trauma; note associated symptoms (blurred vision, central scotoma if macula involved).
Exam
  • Visual acuity (can range from normal to significantly reduced depending on whether/how much the macula is involved).
  • Dilated fundus exam is essential, both to make the diagnosis (retinal whitening with preserved visible vessels) and, critically, to exclude the more visually threatening injuries that share the same blunt mechanism: retinal tear/detachment, choroidal rupture, vitreous hemorrhage, and traumatic optic neuropathy.
  • Full trauma-directed exam for coexisting hyphema, traumatic iritis, angle recession, and lens subluxation given the shared blunt mechanism.
Imaging/Labs
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT), when available (typically via ophthalmology), can characterize the extent of photoreceptor/RPE disruption and has been used to help prognosticate visual outcome based on how many outer retinal layers are affected — more extensive disruption correlates with worse visual recovery.
  • B-scan ultrasound if a dense vitreous hemorrhage precludes direct fundus visualization, to exclude a coexisting retinal detachment.

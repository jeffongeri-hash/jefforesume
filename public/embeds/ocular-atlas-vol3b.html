Preseptal vs. Orbital Cellulitis

History and exam alone run around 82% accurate for this distinction; CT gets you to roughly 91% — which is exactly why a clinical exam alone shouldn't be the final word when the stakes include permanent vision loss.

The orbital septum, a thin fibrous membrane extending from the orbital rim periosteum to the eyelid tarsal plates, is the anatomic boundary that separates two very different diseases. Preseptal (periorbital) cellulitis is infection confined to the eyelid and soft tissue anterior to the septum, most often from traumatic skin superinfection, or spread from meibomian gland/lacrimal sac infection, or (less commonly) hematogenous seeding. Orbital cellulitis is infection posterior to the septum involving the orbital fat, muscles, and contents, arising in the overwhelming majority of cases from contiguous spread of bacterial sinusitis — classically ethmoid sinusitis breaching the paper-thin lamina papyracea (the shared wall between the ethmoid sinuses and the medial orbit).

Risk Factors
  • Preseptal: skin trauma/superinfection, insect bites, chalazion/hordeolum, dacryocystitis, or minor facial/eyelid wounds
  • Orbital: acute bacterial sinusitis (especially ethmoid), more common in children and adolescents; dental infection and orbital trauma/surgery are less common but real alternative sources
  • Both: recent upper respiratory infection, diabetes, and immunosuppression as general risk amplifiers
Fundoscopic Findings

Usually normal in preseptal disease. In orbital cellulitis, particularly with more advanced Chandler stages (subperiosteal or orbital abscess, or with optic nerve compromise), fundus exam may show disc edema, and a relative afferent pupillary defect or decreased color vision can signal optic nerve compromise requiring urgent surgical intervention — these are late, serious findings and should not be the threshold used to first suspect orbital involvement.

History
  • Recent sinusitis, dental infection, trauma, or skin wound; symptom duration and rate of progression.
  • In children specifically, ask about vaccination status (relevant to likely causative organisms) and systemic symptoms (fever, lethargy, irritability).
Exam
  • The critical distinguishing exam findings for ORBITAL involvement: proptosis, pain with eye movement, restricted/painful extraocular movements, diplopia, and decreased visual acuity or an RAPD. Preseptal disease shows eyelid erythema/edema/tenderness WITHOUT these findings.
  • Fever is present more often in orbital disease (reported around 82% of orbital cases vs. roughly half of preseptal cases in comparative series), and CRP tends to run substantially higher in orbital disease, though neither is a perfect discriminator alone.
  • If the lids are swollen shut, they must still be gently opened (manually if needed) for visual inspection and acuity/motility testing — swollen-shut lids occur with both conditions and are not themselves discriminating.
Imaging/Labs
  • CT orbit and sinuses with IV contrast is the study of choice when orbital involvement is suspected or exam is equivocal — comparative studies show meaningfully higher diagnostic accuracy for CT (roughly 91%) versus history and physical alone (roughly 82%), and CT additionally identifies subperiosteal/orbital abscess and sinus source.
  • MRI is reserved for cases where CT is non-diagnostic but suspicion remains high, or for suspected intracranial extension/complications.
  • CBC, blood cultures (especially if systemically unwell or in young children), and consider nasal MRSA PCR given rising utility in guiding empiric coverage in some pediatric series.
1
First exclude orbital compartment syndrome

Before focusing on the cellulitis distinction itself, rapidly assess for signs of a separate, even more urgent process (tense proptosis, markedly elevated IOP, severe visual compromise) that would need immediate lateral canthotomy rather than antibiotics alone.

⏰ Immediately, first assessment
2
Perform a focused eye exam to separate preseptal from orbital disease

Visual acuity, pupil exam (RAPD), extraocular movements (restriction, pain, diplopia), and proptosis assessment. Any positive finding shifts management from oral outpatient therapy to a CT-imaged, IV-antibiotic, ophthalmology/ENT-involved pathway.

⏰ At initial exam
3
Obtain CT with IV contrast for any suspected orbital involvement or diagnostic uncertainty

Also indicated in young children, toxic-appearing patients, prominent forehead swelling, markedly elevated WBC/CRP, or where the eyelid cannot be adequately examined (e.g., swollen shut and unable to be opened even manually).

⏰ Promptly once orbital involvement is suspected or exam is inconclusive
4
Treat confirmed/strongly suspected preseptal cellulitis with oral antibiotics

Most mild-to-moderate preseptal cases in otherwise healthy patients respond well to oral antibiotics covering typical skin/sinus pathogens (Staph aureus including consideration for MRSA depending on local rates, Strep pneumoniae, other Strep species) with close outpatient follow-up.

⏰ Same visit, outpatient course
5
Treat confirmed orbital cellulitis with IV antibiotics and admission

Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics covering Staph (including MRSA where locally prevalent), Strep, and anaerobes, combined with admission for monitoring, since orbital cellulitis can progress rapidly and has genuine vision- and life-threatening potential (progression to subperiosteal/orbital abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, or intracranial extension).

⏰ Immediately on diagnosis
6
Involve ophthalmology and ENT/otolaryngology promptly for orbital disease

Ophthalmology for ongoing visual/motility monitoring, ENT for the underlying sinus source and potential surgical drainage; both should be involved early rather than only if the patient fails to improve.

⏰ Same day as orbital cellulitis diagnosis
7
Reassess and escalate for abscess or non-response

Subperiosteal or orbital abscess on imaging, or clinical non-response/worsening (increasing proptosis, worsening motility restriction, decreasing vision) after 24–48 hours of appropriate IV antibiotics, should prompt surgical drainage (typically ENT endoscopic sinus surgery ± orbital drainage).

⏰ Reassessed at 24–48h; sooner if acutely worsening

Complications to Anticipate
  • Subperiosteal or intraorbital abscess
  • Vision loss from optic nerve compression/compartment-syndrome-like effects in severe orbital disease
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis and intracranial extension (meningitis, brain abscess) in severe or delayed-treatment cases — rare but life-threatening
Clinical Pearls
  • Diplopia and true painful/restricted extraocular movement essentially never occur with isolated preseptal disease — their presence should be treated as orbital cellulitis until proven otherwise.
  • A normal visual acuity does NOT rule out orbital cellulitis; vision is often preserved until relatively late in the disease course, so don't let a reassuring visual acuity override other concerning exam findings.
  • History and physical exam alone are meaningfully less accurate than CT for this distinction (roughly 82% vs. 91% in comparative data) — maintain a low threshold for imaging when there's any diagnostic uncertainty, especially in children.
Evidence Notes
  • A retrospective study of 43 patients with orbital complications of sinusitis found predictive accuracy of 82% for history and physical examination alone versus 91% for CT in diagnosing orbital cellulitis, supporting liberal use of CT when the distinction is unclear.
  • The Chandler classification (Group I: preseptal; II: orbital cellulitis without abscess; III: subperiosteal abscess; IV: orbital abscess; V: cavernous sinus thrombosis) remains the most widely used staging system and correlates with both management approach and complication risk.

Subconjunctival Hemorrhage

The dramatic appearance is precisely what makes the reassurance conversation the actual clinical skill here — most patients need to hear, clearly, why something that looks this alarming needs nothing more than time.

Rupture of a small, fragile conjunctival or episcleral vessel allows blood to collect in the potential space between the conjunctiva and the underlying sclera. Because this space has no anatomic barriers to spreading, even a small vessel rupture can produce a dramatic, sharply demarcated field of blood. Common precipitants include Valsalva-type events (coughing, sneezing, straining, vomiting, heavy lifting), minor trauma, contact lens wear, and anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, though a substantial proportion occur with no identifiable precipitant at all.

Risk Factors
  • Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication use
  • Hypertension (recurrent or unusually severe subconjunctival hemorrhage should prompt a blood pressure check)
  • Valsalva-generating activities: coughing, sneezing, straining, vomiting, heavy lifting, strenuous exercise
  • Minor trauma, including vigorous eye rubbing, and contact lens wear/removal
  • Bleeding disorders (relevant particularly for recurrent or bilateral presentations without another clear precipitant)
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; subconjunctival hemorrhage is confined to the space between conjunctiva and sclera and has no associated fundus findings. A dilated exam is not indicated as part of routine workup for this diagnosis alone.

History
  • Any preceding Valsalva event, trauma, anticoagulant/antiplatelet use, and whether this is a first or recurrent episode.
  • Explicitly ask about pain, vision change, and photophobia — their absence is the key reassurance point; their presence should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.
Exam
  • Sharply demarcated, flat, uniformly red patch of blood beneath the conjunctiva; confirm fully normal visual acuity, a normal, reactive pupil, and a normal cornea.
  • Assess whether the posterior extent of the hemorrhage can be fully visualized — a hemorrhage extending posteriorly without a visible border, especially after trauma, should raise concern for an occult scleral rupture underneath and prompt a more careful globe assessment.
  • Check blood pressure, particularly for recurrent or unusually extensive hemorrhage without a clear precipitant.
Imaging/Labs
  • Not routinely needed. Consider INR for patients on warfarin, and consider a bleeding disorder workup (CBC with platelets, coagulation studies) for recurrent, bilateral, or unusually severe presentations without an identifiable precipitant.
1
Confirm the reassurance triad

Painless, no vision change, and a normal pupil/cornea exam — this triad, once confirmed, is sufficient to diagnose simple subconjunctival hemorrhage and avoid unnecessary workup.

⏰ At initial exam
2
Explicitly exclude occult globe injury if trauma is the precipitant

A subconjunctival hemorrhage following trauma, especially if extending 360 degrees or posteriorly without a visible border, warrants a more careful exam for an underlying scleral rupture before being labeled as simple/benign.

⏰ When trauma is the precipitant
3
Reassure and educate on expected course

Explain that this is a benign, self-limited finding that will resolve over 1–2 weeks (may change color as it resolves, similar to a skin bruise, from bright red to yellow-green), with no effect on vision and no specific treatment needed.

⏰ At diagnosis
4
Check blood pressure and review anticoagulation status

Particularly relevant for recurrent, bilateral, or unusually extensive hemorrhage without a clear Valsalva/trauma precipitant; uncontrolled hypertension identified this way warrants its own follow-up.

⏰ Same visit
5
Consider bleeding disorder workup for atypical presentations

Recurrent episodes, bilateral simultaneous hemorrhage, or any hemorrhage disproportionate to a trivial precipitant, particularly without hypertension or anticoagulant use to explain it, warrants basic bleeding disorder screening.

⏰ At follow-up if pattern is atypical/recurrent

Clinical Pearls
  • The dramatic visual appearance relative to the benign underlying process is the actual counseling challenge — patients are often disproportionately alarmed, and clear reassurance (with an explanation of why it looks this way) is the main value added at this visit.
  • Document the absence of pain, vision change, and globe tenderness explicitly in the chart — this is exactly the language that protects against a missed traumatic globe injury being second-guessed later.
  • Recurrent or bilateral subconjunctival hemorrhage without an explaining precipitant deserves a blood pressure check and consideration of an anticoagulation/bleeding-disorder workup, not just reassurance again.
Evidence Notes
  • Clinical consensus across emergency medicine and ophthalmology references consistently frames subconjunctival hemorrhage as a benign, self-resolving finding (typically 1-2 weeks) requiring no specific treatment beyond addressing any underlying precipitant (blood pressure, anticoagulation review) in recurrent or atypical cases.

Pterygium / Pinguecula

The surgical technique choice matters enormously here — conjunctival autograft brings recurrence rates down to the single digits, while bare sclera excision alone (still sometimes used for speed) carries meaningfully higher recurrence.

Both are UV-related degenerative conjunctival conditions on a shared spectrum. A pinguecula is a yellow-white, raised conjunctival lesion, typically nasal, that does NOT cross the limbus onto the cornea. A pterygium is a fibrovascular growth that does cross the limbus and extends onto the corneal surface, capable of inducing astigmatism, encroaching the visual axis, and restricting ocular motility if large enough. Chronic UV exposure damages the limbal stem cell barrier, allowing conjunctival tissue to encroach onto the cornea over years — a process sometimes described as conjunctivalization of the cornea.

Risk Factors
  • Chronic UV light exposure (sunlight or other sources) — the dominant identified risk factor for both conditions
  • Living in warm, dry, sunny climates and occupations/lifestyles with substantial outdoor sun exposure
  • Male sex (both conditions reported more common in men in most series)
  • Contact lens wear and increasing age
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; both conditions are confined to the conjunctival/corneal surface and do not produce fundus findings.

History
  • Duration, rate of growth, associated irritation/foreign body sensation, and any visual symptoms (blurring from induced astigmatism, or awareness of the lesion encroaching centrally).
  • Sun exposure history and use of protective eyewear.
Exam
  • Pinguecula: raised, yellow-white lesion in the interpalpebral bulbar conjunctiva, not crossing the limbus.
  • Pterygium: wing-shaped fibrovascular growth crossing the limbus onto the cornea, typically nasal; assess size/extent (how close to encroaching the visual axis), presence of induced astigmatism (visual acuity, keratometry/topography where available), and any restriction of ocular motility with larger lesions.
  • Assess for inflammation (injected, engorged vessels within the lesion) versus a quiet, non-inflamed appearance.
Imaging/Labs
  • Not routinely required; corneal topography can help document and follow induced astigmatism in a growing or surgically-considered pterygium.
1
Start with conservative management for asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic lesions

Artificial tears and lubricating ointment for irritation; sun protection counseling (broad-brimmed hat, UV-blocking sunglasses) to reduce further UV-driven progression — relevant for both pinguecula and pterygium.

⏰ First-line for essentially all presentations
2
Treat inflamed/irritated lesions with a short course of topical anti-inflammatory therapy

A brief course of a mild topical corticosteroid or topical NSAID can settle an acutely inflamed pinguecula or pterygium; this is symptomatic therapy and does not shrink or eliminate the underlying lesion.

⏰ As needed for symptomatic flares
3
Identify surgical indications for pterygium

Surgery is indicated for: persistent irritation resistant to medical therapy, encroachment on or threat to the visual axis, induced astigmatism causing blurred vision, progressive growth, or restriction of ocular motility. Surgery is not undertaken casually for small, quiet, asymptomatic lesions given recurrence risk and other surgical considerations.

⏰ When indications are met, elective timing
4
Choose conjunctival autograft as the preferred surgical technique

Excision with conjunctival autograft is the current gold standard given its low recurrence rate (roughly 5-10% in typical series, with some modified techniques reporting recurrence as low as 1-2.5%), clearly outperforming bare sclera excision alone. Amniotic membrane graft is a reasonable alternative where autograft tissue or technique isn't feasible, though with somewhat higher recurrence than autograft.

⏰ At time of elective surgery
5
Consider adjunctive mitomycin C for higher-recurrence-risk cases

Intraoperative mitomycin C can further reduce recurrence, particularly for recurrent pterygia, but carries its own risk profile (scleromalacia, chronic keratopathy, toxic keratoconjunctivitis with higher concentrations/exposure) and should be used judiciously rather than routinely.

⏰ Selected cases, surgeon's judgment
6
Counsel on recurrence risk and ongoing sun protection post-surgery

Even with optimal technique, some recurrence risk remains; ongoing UV protection reduces this risk and is worth continued reinforcement after surgery.

⏰ Post-operative counseling, ongoing

Complications to Anticipate
  • Induced astigmatism and blurred vision from progressive pterygium growth
  • Restriction of ocular motility with large, longstanding pterygia
  • Post-surgical recurrence, notably higher with bare sclera technique alone compared to conjunctival autograft
  • Rare surgical complications: graft-related giant papillary conjunctivitis, granuloma formation, and (with older radiotherapy-based techniques, now largely abandoned) scleromalacia
Clinical Pearls
  • Surgical technique choice meaningfully changes the recurrence conversation with patients — conjunctival autograft (roughly 5-10% recurrence, potentially lower with modified techniques) is a fundamentally different proposition than older bare sclera excision alone (historically 20%+ recurrence in various series).
  • Sun protection counseling is genuinely disease-modifying here, not just generic advice — chronic UV exposure is the dominant identified driver of both conditions and their progression.
  • Pterygium surgery isn't undertaken casually for small, asymptomatic lesions given real recurrence risk and surgical considerations — reserve it for lesions meeting clear functional or symptomatic indications.
Evidence Notes
  • Comparative surgical series consistently show conjunctival autograft recurrence rates in the 5-10% range (with some modified techniques, such as the extensive Tenon's resection 'P.E.R.F.E.C.T.' technique, reporting recurrence as low as roughly 1 in 1000 in one series), versus substantially higher recurrence with bare sclera excision alone.

Dry Eye Disease

Only two drug classes have ever cleared FDA approval for this diagnosis despite years of trials — a sobering reminder that the treatment ladder here is built more on stepwise clinical practice than a deep bench of positive Phase 3 trials.

A multifactorial disease of the tear film and ocular surface, classically divided into aqueous-deficient (inadequate tear production, as in Sjögren syndrome) and evaporative (excessive tear evaporation, most often from meibomian gland dysfunction) subtypes per the TFOS DEWS II framework, though many patients have a mixed picture. Tear film instability and hyperosmolarity drive a self-perpetuating cycle of ocular surface inflammation and further tear film compromise, which is why anti-inflammatory therapy (not just lubrication) is central to treating moderate-to-severe disease.

Risk Factors
  • Female sex and older age (both strongly associated with higher prevalence)
  • Sjögren syndrome and other autoimmune/connective tissue diseases (a directed cause of significant aqueous-deficient dry eye)
  • Meibomian gland dysfunction/blepharitis (the dominant driver of evaporative dry eye)
  • Contact lens wear, prior refractive surgery (e.g., LASIK), and certain systemic medications (antihistamines, some antidepressants, isotretinoin) as contributing factors
  • Prolonged screen time/reduced blink rate as an environmental contributor
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; dry eye disease is confined to the ocular surface and tear film and does not produce fundus findings.

History
  • Symptom pattern (burning, foreign body sensation, fluctuating vision, worse with prolonged reading/screen use), and directed review of systems for Sjögren syndrome (dry mouth, joint pain, parotid swelling) given how significantly this changes both prognosis and systemic workup considerations.
  • Contact lens wear, prior refractive surgery, and relevant systemic medications.
Exam
  • Tear film breakup time, corneal/conjunctival staining pattern (fluorescein and/or lissamine green), tear meniscus height, and meibomian gland assessment (expression quality, gland dropout if meibography available) to help classify aqueous-deficient versus evaporative predominance.
  • Standardized symptom questionnaires (e.g., OSDI) are commonly used in specialty settings to quantify symptom burden and track treatment response.
Imaging/Labs
  • Tear osmolarity testing, where available, supports the diagnosis and can help track severity/response to treatment.
  • For suspected Sjögren syndrome (directed by history): consider ANA, SSA/SSB antibodies, and rheumatology referral; a formal Sjögren diagnosis has systemic management implications well beyond the eye.
1
Start with artificial tears and environmental/behavioral modification

Preservative-free artificial tears (particularly for frequent use, more than 4x/day, to avoid preservative toxicity), environmental humidification, reduced screen time or scheduled blink breaks, and addressing any contributing systemic medications where feasible.

⏰ First-line for essentially all severity levels
2
Treat underlying meibomian gland dysfunction concurrently if evaporative component present

Warm compresses, lid hygiene, and the MGD-specific treatment ladder (see Blepharitis entry) since evaporative dry eye and MGD/blepharitis are frequently the same underlying process viewed from different angles.

⏰ Concurrent with dry eye treatment
3
Add punctal plugs for inadequate response to lubrication alone

A simple in-office procedure that blocks tear drainage, improving ocular surface tear retention; a reasonable next step particularly for aqueous-deficient disease once inflammation is being addressed, since plugging drainage in the setting of unaddressed surface inflammation can be less effective.

⏰ Second-line, in-office procedure
4
Add topical anti-inflammatory therapy for moderate-severe or inflammation-driven disease

Topical cyclosporine A (multiple approved formulations/concentrations) or lifitegrast are the two FDA-approved prescription options in the US, both targeting the underlying inflammatory component rather than just lubricating; onset of benefit is typically gradual over weeks to months, and this should be set as an expectation with patients.

⏰ For moderate-severe disease or inadequate response to tears/plugs; expect weeks-to-months for full effect
5
Consider short-course topical corticosteroids for symptom flares

A brief course of a mild topical steroid can be used as a bridge therapy for acute symptom flares or to jump-start improvement while awaiting the slower onset of cyclosporine/lifitegrast, but is not intended for long-term use given steroid-related risks (IOP elevation, cataract) with chronic use.

⏰ Short-term bridge therapy only
6
Escalate to autologous serum tears or other advanced therapy for refractory severe disease

Reserved for severe, refractory cases (often in the context of significant Sjögren-associated or other severe aqueous-deficient disease) under ophthalmology direction.

⏰ Refractory, severe disease only
7
Refer for rheumatology evaluation if Sjögren syndrome is suspected

A directed history/exam suggestive of Sjögren syndrome warrants rheumatology referral, since systemic disease management (and monitoring for associated lymphoma risk in Sjögren specifically) extends well beyond ocular symptom control.

⏰ When systemic disease is suspected
🔧 Procedure: Punctal Plug Insertion
  1. Confirm inadequate response to artificial tears/lubrication alone before proceeding.
  2. Select appropriate plug size/type (temporary dissolvable or longer-term silicone) based on clinical goals.
  3. Insert into the inferior (and/or superior) punctum using the manufacturer-specific inserter, typically performed in-office without anesthesia or with brief topical anesthetic.
  4. Reassess symptom improvement and tear film parameters at follow-up.
Contraindications:
  • Active ocular surface inflammation should generally be at least partially addressed first, since blocking drainage in the setting of significant unaddressed inflammation can be less effective and is a reasonable rationale for sequencing anti-inflammatory therapy alongside or before plug placement in more inflamed eyes.

  • Corneal complications from severe, chronic disease: punctate epithelial erosions, filamentary keratitis, and rarely corneal ulceration/scarring in severe, poorly controlled cases

  • Set expectations explicitly that cyclosporine/lifitegrast take weeks to months for full benefit — patients who expect rapid relief similar to artificial tears are often prematurely disappointed and discontinue an appropriate therapy before it has had a chance to work.

Evidence Notes
  • Per TFOS DEWS II, only two therapeutic agents (cyclosporine A and lifitegrast) have achieved FDA approval specifically for dry eye disease despite numerous clinical trials in the space, reflecting how difficult this disease has been to target pharmacologically at the regulatory level.

Chalazion / Hordeolum

A single steroid injection resolves chalazia about as well as surgical incision and curettage, with less pain and inconvenience for the patient — a genuinely underused first-line option relative to how often surgery gets reached for instead.

A hordeolum is an acute bacterial infection (typically staphylococcal), classified as external (infection of an eyelash follicle/associated gland of Zeis, presenting at the lid margin) or internal (infection of a meibomian gland, often pointing on the conjunctival/tarsal side). A chalazion, by contrast, is a chronic, NON-infectious lipogranulomatous inflammatory response: obstruction of a meibomian (or Zeis) gland causes stagnant lipid secretion to leak into the surrounding tarsal tissue, triggering a foreign-body granulomatous reaction (epithelioid histiocytes, giant cells) around the lipid material — no bacteria are present in a mature chalazion, which is the fundamental reason antibiotics don't address it. An internal hordeolum that doesn't fully resolve can evolve into a chalazion over time.

Risk Factors
  • Chronic blepharitis/meibomian gland dysfunction (the dominant risk factor for both, given the shared underlying gland obstruction pathway)
  • Rosacea
  • Prior chalazion/hordeolum episodes
  • Adults aged 30-50 with a female predominance in most chalazion series specifically
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; both conditions are confined to the eyelid/meibomian gland structures and do not involve or require fundus assessment.

History
  • Onset, whether the lesion is painful (favors hordeolum) or a painless, gradually enlarging nodule (favors chalazion), and any history of chronic blepharitis/rosacea.
  • For any lesion near the inner canthus specifically, consider and exclude dacryocystitis (tenderness/swelling centered under the medial canthus near the nose) and canaliculitis (tenderness medial to the punctum) as alternative diagnoses based on location.
Exam
  • Hordeolum: acute, tender, red, localized swelling at the lid margin (external) or pointing toward the conjunctival surface (internal).
  • Chalazion: firm, non-tender, smooth nodule, typically in the mid-tarsus, with freely mobile overlying skin; may show a visible or palpable nodule on the conjunctival (inner eyelid) surface.
  • Chronic chalazia unresponsive to treatment, or ANY recurrent lesion in the same location in a patient over 40, should raise suspicion for sebaceous gland carcinoma and prompt biopsy rather than repeated conservative treatment attempts.
Imaging/Labs
  • Not routinely needed; biopsy is reserved for chronic, treatment-refractory, or recurrent lesions raising concern for malignancy (particularly sebaceous gland carcinoma in older patients).
1
Start both with warm compresses

Warm compresses for 5-10 minutes, 2-3 times daily, are first-line for both hordeolum and early/small chalazia, promoting drainage and gland unclogging; hordeola and external chalazion-precursor lesions frequently resolve with this alone within about a week.

⏰ First-line, ongoing until resolved
2
Add oral antibiotics for hordeolum with associated cellulitis or systemic signs

Most simple hordeola resolve with warm compresses alone; oral antibiotics are reserved for hordeola with surrounding preseptal cellulitis or systemic symptoms, since the underlying process (bacterial infection) does respond to antimicrobial therapy unlike chalazion.

⏰ If cellulitis/systemic signs present
3
Do NOT expect antibiotics to resolve a chalazion

Since mature chalazia are non-infectious lipogranulomatous lesions, antibiotics alone don't address the underlying process; topical antibiotic-steroid combinations may have a role if associated blepharitis is present, but the antibiotic component isn't treating the chalazion itself.

⏰ N/A — a default-avoid as monotherapy for chalazion
4
Offer intralesional steroid injection as an effective, less invasive first-line option for chalazion

A single intralesional triamcinolone acetonide injection (commonly 0.2 mL of 10 mg/mL) achieves resolution rates comparable to incision and curettage (roughly 84% vs. 87% at 3 weeks in one randomized trial) with less patient-reported pain and inconvenience; this is a reasonable first-line procedural option, particularly for lesions near the lacrimal drainage system where an incision could risk complications.

⏰ Reasonable first-line procedural option once conservative measures have been given a trial (generally several weeks)
5
Reserve incision and curettage for larger, persistent, or steroid-injection-refractory chalazia

I&C remains highly effective and is preferred when a tissue diagnosis is needed (e.g., diagnostic uncertainty) or when steroid injection isn't preferred/available; performed via a posterior (conjunctival-side) approach to avoid a visible anterior scar.

⏰ For lesions persisting beyond 4-6 weeks despite conservative measures, or when steroid injection fails/isn't appropriate
6
Counsel on steroid injection risks if that route is chosen

Potential complications include permanent skin depigmentation (particularly notable in darker skin tones), local tissue atrophy, elevated IOP, and rarely corneal/globe injury if injected improperly; using a soluble aqueous preparation (e.g., dexamethasone) rather than a crystalline suspension can reduce some of these risks.

⏰ Pre-procedure counseling
7
Biopsy any atypical, recurrent, or treatment-refractory lesion, especially in patients over 40

Recurrent chalazion at the same site in an older adult is a red flag for sebaceous gland carcinoma, a masquerading malignancy that can mimic recurrent benign chalazion; don't keep treating conservatively indefinitely without eventually pursuing tissue diagnosis if this pattern emerges.

⏰ When recurrence/atypical features are identified
🔧 Procedure: Incision and Curettage (I&C) of Chalazion
  1. Local anesthetic is injected into the area surrounding the lesion.
  2. The eyelid is everted using a chalazion clamp for stabilization and hemostasis.
  3. An incision is made through the conjunctiva (posterior approach, avoiding a visible anterior/skin scar).
  4. The lipogranulomatous contents are curetted out; the tarsus may be minimally resected if needed.
  5. The clamp is released and gentle pressure applied; sutures are typically not needed given the posterior conjunctival incision location.
Contraindications:
  • Active acute infection/cellulitis at the site (address infection first).
  • Consider biopsy submission of curetted tissue if there's any concern for malignancy (recurrent lesion, atypical appearance, older patient) rather than assuming benign disease.

Contact Lens Complications

The single unifying management step across nearly every complication on this list is the same: stop wearing the lens — the harder skill is recognizing which specific complication you're looking at so the rest of the plan follows correctly.

Contact lenses substitute for the anterior corneal surface and can produce complications through several distinct mechanisms: hypoxic changes to the cornea from reduced oxygen transmission, mechanical trauma from lens edge or foreign material trapped beneath the lens, hypersensitivity/immune reactions to lens deposits or solution components, chemical toxicity from solution preservatives, and infectious risk from contamination (worse with poor hygiene, overnight wear, or water exposure). Giant papillary conjunctivitis (GPC), a major and often underappreciated complication, is believed to be an immunologic reaction to protein/lipid deposits accumulating on the lens surface acting as an antigen, producing large papillae on the upper tarsal conjunctiva — it can develop as early as three weeks into wear or only after months to years of previously uneventful lens use.

Risk Factors
  • Extended or overnight lens wear (substantially increases hypoxic and infectious risk across essentially every complication category)
  • Poor lens hygiene: infrequent replacement, reusing old disinfecting solution, water exposure (showering/swimming in lenses)
  • Heavy lens deposits (protein/lipid), a major driver of GPC specifically
  • Extended wear duration and cumulative years of contact lens use generally
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable to isolated contact-lens-related conjunctival/corneal complications; fundus exam is only relevant if a complication progresses to a more severe process (e.g., microbial keratitis with risk of endophthalmitis), which is addressed under those specific entries elsewhere in this atlas.

History
  • Lens type, wear schedule (daily disposable vs. extended/overnight wear), replacement frequency, and hygiene practices (solution type, water exposure).
  • Time course of symptom onset relative to lens wear changes (new lens type/solution, extended wear beyond the prescribed schedule).
Exam
  • Everting the upper lid to inspect the tarsal conjunctiva is essential for diagnosing GPC (large papillae) and distinguishing it from other causes of contact-lens-related irritation.
  • Slit lamp exam of the cornea for punctate staining, infiltrates (sterile vs. suspected infectious, see Corneal Foreign Body/Microbial Keratitis entries for full differentiation), neovascularization (a sign of chronic hypoxia), and epithelial changes (edema, microcysts).
  • Assess whether findings are consistent with a benign, self-limited process (mild GPC, simple hypoxic changes) versus a vision-threatening infectious process requiring the more urgent workup described elsewhere in this atlas (contact-lens-associated microbial keratitis, particularly Pseudomonas).
Imaging/Labs
  • Corneal culture/scraping if an infectious infiltrate is suspected (see Corneal/Intraocular Foreign Body and Microbial Keratitis entries) — this is a different diagnostic pathway from the more benign complications covered primarily in this entry.
1
Discontinue contact lens wear as the universal first step

Regardless of the specific complication identified, stopping lens wear is the foundational management step while the underlying process is diagnosed and treated; resuming lens wear before full resolution risks recurrence or worsening.

⏰ Immediately, at diagnosis
2
Exclude microbial keratitis first for any painful, infiltrate-associated presentation

Any corneal infiltrate in a contact lens wearer should be evaluated with the same urgency and Pseudomonas-coverage mindset described in the Corneal Foreign Body and Microbial Keratitis entries before being assumed to be a more benign sterile/inflammatory process.

⏰ At initial exam, before assuming a benign etiology
3
Treat giant papillary conjunctivitis with lens discontinuation and topical anti-allergy therapy

Discontinue lens wear; topical mast cell stabilizers/antihistamines and, for more significant cases, a short course of topical steroid can hasten symptom resolution. Once resolved, address the underlying driver (switch to more frequent lens replacement, daily disposables, or improved cleaning regimen, and mechanically remove/reduce deposits) before considering lens wear resumption.

⏰ Discontinuation immediate; anti-inflammatory therapy as needed; lens modifications before any resumption
4
Treat contact-lens-associated infiltrative keratitis (sterile) with lens discontinuation ± topical antibiotic

Sterile infiltrates from an immune response to bacterial products on the lens/lid margin (rather than true infection) are managed with lens discontinuation and close follow-up; a topical antibiotic is often still used empirically given the difficulty definitively excluding a low-grade infectious component at initial presentation.

⏰ At diagnosis, with close follow-up to confirm resolution rather than progression
5
Address hypoxic corneal changes (edema, neovascularization, microcysts) by modifying lens parameters

These findings signal inadequate oxygen transmission; management involves switching to a higher oxygen-permeability lens material, reducing wear time, or discontinuing extended/overnight wear, rather than any pharmacologic treatment.

⏰ Ongoing lens-fitting modification, ophthalmology/optometry-directed
6
Reinforce hygiene and replacement schedule counseling at every visit

Proper hand hygiene before lens handling, fresh disinfecting solution (never topped off or reused), avoiding water exposure while wearing lenses, and adherence to the prescribed replacement schedule (daily disposables where appropriate) meaningfully reduce recurrence across nearly every complication category.

⏰ Ongoing patient education

  • GPC can develop after years of previously uneventful, successful lens wear — a long history of good tolerance doesn't rule it out as a new complication.
  • The reflexive first step for nearly any contact-lens-related complication is the same (stop wearing the lens), but getting the rest of the management right depends on correctly identifying which specific complication is actually present.

Allergic Conjunctivitis

Vernal and atopic keratoconjunctivitis are not just 'severe allergic conjunctivitis' — they're sight-threatening variants that need topical cyclosporine/tacrolimus and close monitoring for keratoconus, a different treatment conversation entirely from seasonal itching.

Seasonal and perennial allergic conjunctivitis (SAC/PAC), together representing the vast majority (roughly 95%) of ocular allergy cases, result from an immediate type 1 IgE-mediated hypersensitivity response to airborne allergens (pollens, dust mites, pet dander), producing itching, redness, chemosis, and watery discharge. Vernal keratoconjunctivitis (VKC, primarily affecting children/young adults, often seasonal) and atopic keratoconjunctivitis (AKC, a more chronic, often perennial disease associated with atopic dermatitis) are distinct, more severe variants involving a more complex, chronic immune response with giant cobblestone papillae, and are capable of causing genuine corneal complications and vision threat, unlike typical SAC/PAC.

Risk Factors
  • Personal or family history of atopy (allergic rhinitis, asthma, eczema) for all forms, and particularly strong for AKC given its direct association with atopic dermatitis
  • Seasonal environmental allergen exposure (pollen) for SAC; year-round exposure (dust mites, pet dander, mold) for PAC
  • Male predominance and younger age (childhood/adolescence) for vernal keratoconjunctivitis specifically
  • Chronic eye rubbing, itself both a symptom and a risk factor for progressive corneal changes (keratoconus) in VKC/AKC
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; allergic conjunctivitis in all its forms is confined to the conjunctival and (in VKC/AKC) corneal surface, and does not produce fundus findings.

History
  • Timing/pattern (seasonal vs. perennial), personal/family atopy history, and specific identification of triggers where possible.
  • For suspected VKC/AKC: age (VKC more common in children/young adults), chronicity, and associated atopic dermatitis history (AKC).
Exam
  • SAC/PAC: conjunctival injection, chemosis, watery discharge, and mild papillary reaction; itching is the dominant and most specific symptom.
  • VKC: giant, cobblestone papillae of the upper tarsal conjunctiva, and Horner-Trantas dots (limbal, gelatinous, whitish nodules) in more severe cases; assess the cornea specifically for shield ulcers (a sight-threatening VKC complication) and any signs of keratoconus (irregular astigmatism, corneal thinning) given the chronic eye-rubbing association.
  • AKC: chronic lid changes (thickened, macerated skin, Dennie-Morgan lines), conjunctival scarring/symblepharon in severe chronic disease, and similarly assess for keratoconus and corneal complications.
Imaging/Labs
  • Generally a clinical diagnosis; corneal topography can help monitor for keratoconus progression in VKC/AKC patients given the elevated risk from chronic eye rubbing.
1
Start with a dual-action topical antihistamine/mast cell stabilizer for SAC/PAC

Agents like olopatadine, ketotifen, epinastine, or azelastine provide both rapid symptom relief (within about 30 minutes) and mast cell stabilization to reduce future episodes; olopatadine has shown modest superiority over ketotifen for itch reduction in comparative trials. This is first-line for typical seasonal/perennial allergic conjunctivitis.

⏰ First-line, ongoing through the allergen exposure period
2
Add cool compresses and allergen avoidance counseling

Simple adjuncts: cool compresses for symptomatic relief, and counseling on reducing allergen exposure (air filtration, avoiding triggers, hand-washing after outdoor exposure during pollen season).

⏰ Concurrent with drop therapy
3
Consider a short course of topical corticosteroid for significant acute flares

Reserved for more severe or breakthrough symptoms not adequately controlled by antihistamine/mast cell stabilizer therapy alone; not intended for chronic/long-term use given steroid-related risks with prolonged exposure.

⏰ Short-term, for acute flares only
4
Recognize VKC/AKC as distinct, more aggressive diseases requiring escalated therapy

Giant papillae, Horner-Trantas dots, or any corneal involvement (shield ulcer, keratoconus signs) shifts management toward topical cyclosporine 0.1% (FDA-approved specifically for VKC) or 0.05%, or topical tacrolimus as steroid-sparing options, given the chronic nature of these conditions and the risks of prolonged steroid dependence.

⏰ Once VKC/AKC is recognized, distinct from typical SAC/PAC management
5
Monitor for keratoconus and counsel against eye rubbing in VKC/AKC

Chronic eye rubbing is both a symptom-relief behavior and a driver of progressive corneal ectasia in these patients; adequate allergy control paired with explicit counseling against rubbing is part of preventing keratoconus progression.

⏰ Ongoing counseling and monitoring
6
Consider allergist referral for disease refractory to topical therapy alone

Systemic allergy management (oral antihistamines used judiciously, given some can paradoxically worsen ocular dryness/irritation, or allergen immunotherapy) may benefit patients whose disease isn't adequately controlled with topical therapy alone.

⏰ For refractory or systemically-driven disease

Clinical Pearls
  • Itching is the single most specific symptom for allergic conjunctivitis generally — its absence should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.
  • VKC and AKC are not simply 'severe' allergic conjunctivitis on the same treatment ladder as SAC/PAC — they are distinct diseases with real vision-threatening potential that warrant steroid-sparing immunomodulatory therapy and dedicated corneal monitoring.
  • Ask specifically about eye rubbing in any VKC/AKC patient and counsel against it explicitly — it's a modifiable behavior directly implicated in keratoconus progression in these populations.
Evidence Notes

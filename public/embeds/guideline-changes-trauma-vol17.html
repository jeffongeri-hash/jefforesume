What Changed · 2025

ABCDE gained a letter — and it comes first

ATLS 11th Edition is a full revamp of trauma-care fundamentals. The single most visible change: the primary survey sequence itself. Exsanguinating hemorrhage control now precedes airway — ABCDE becomes xABCDE — formalizing what battlefield and civilian trauma data have argued for years.

02 · Deep Dive · Headline Change

Why hemorrhage control jumped ahead of airway

xABCDE New sequence

Civilian and military data converge on the same finding: uncontrolled hemorrhage is a leading cause of preventable trauma death, and the time spent on airway management before addressing an actively exsanguinating wound has real cost. ATLS 11 formalizes this by adding "x" — control of exsanguinating external hemorrhage — as the explicit first step, ahead of airway.

In practice, this means tourniquets, wound packing, and pelvic binders are treated as immediate, high-yield actions taken in the first seconds of the primary survey — not deferred until "C" is reached. This isn't a demotion of airway management; it's a recognition that a patient who exsanguinates during airway setup doesn't survive to benefit from a secured airway.

03 · Deep Dive

Damage control resuscitation, formalized

Permissive hypotension, minimal crystalloid, early transfusion Reinforced as standard

Large-volume crystalloid resuscitation — the traditional "6 to 10 units of crystalloid before blood" approach — is explicitly not recommended. It exacerbates coagulopathy and is linked to tissue edema, acidosis, reperfusion injury, and multiorgan failure. ATLS 11 reinforces limiting crystalloid to roughly 1 liter before transitioning to blood products.

PrincipleWhat it means at the bedside
Permissive hypotensionTolerate MAP 50–60 mmHg until hemorrhage is controlled, absent suspected brain injury
Minimize crystalloidTreat crystalloid as a bridging fluid only, capped around 1 L, not a resuscitation endpoint
Early transfusionActivate massive transfusion protocols early rather than after a crystalloid trial fails
Balanced component therapy1:1:1 (RBC:plasma:platelets) or low-titer O-negative whole blood when available
⚠ Permissive hypotension has a carve-out

Permissive hypotension is not appropriate when traumatic brain injury is suspected — cerebral perfusion pressure needs remain a competing priority in that population. Don't apply the strategy uniformly across all trauma patients.

04 · Deep Dive

TXA dosing, including the TBI-specific bump

1g bolus + 1g infusion
Standard TXA dosing for major hemorrhage, within 3 hours of injury
2g bolus
Supported specifically in traumatic brain injury

The 3-hour window is a hard consideration, not a soft target — TXA given beyond that window loses benefit and may cause harm, consistent with the CRASH-2 evidence base this recommendation continues to rest on.

05 · Deep Dive

Airway and spine motion restriction updates

Video laryngoscopy is now the preferred primary tool for intubation in trauma, reflecting accumulated evidence on first-pass success rates — direct laryngoscopy remains a reasonable backup, not the default starting point. Airway management continues to prioritize rapid sequence intubation, now explicitly sequenced after initial hemorrhage-focused resuscitation rather than as an automatic early step.

Spine motion restriction principles were also revised — modifications reflect a move away from blanket rigid immobilization toward more selective, evidence-based restriction, consistent with the broader trend (also seen in this atlas's Neurology and MSK volumes) of narrowing routine interventions to patients who actually benefit from them.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Control exsanguinating hemorrhage — tourniquet, packing, pelvic binder — before, not after, airway management. Think xABCDE, not ABCDE.
  • Cap crystalloid around 1 liter; don't chase normal vitals with more fluid before blood products are available.
  • Activate massive transfusion protocols early rather than waiting for a crystalloid trial to fail.
  • TXA within 3 hours of major hemorrhage — 1g bolus + 1g infusion, or a 2g bolus if TBI is suspected.
  • Reach for video laryngoscopy first for trauma intubation, not as a rescue device.
  • Skip permissive hypotension in patients with suspected TBI — cerebral perfusion takes priority there.

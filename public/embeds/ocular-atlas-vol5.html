Slit Lamp Examination Technique

The slit lamp is a binocular microscope combined with an adjustable light source that produces a thin, bright beam ('slit') of light, allowing optical sectioning of the transparent ocular structures (cornea, anterior chamber, lens) layer by layer — a capability no other bedside exam tool provides. Mastery of a few standard beam configurations covers the large majority of clinically relevant anterior segment findings.

Equipment
  • Slit lamp biomicroscope
  • Fluorescein strips (for corneal/tear film assessment)
  • Cobalt blue filter (built into most slit lamps)
  • Handheld lenses (90D, 78D) for posterior segment viewing when needed
Technique Steps
1
Position and stabilize the patient

Chin on the chinrest, forehead firmly against the headband, with the lateral canthus aligned to the marked line on the headrest post — this alignment matters more than it seems, since a poorly positioned patient makes fine focus nearly impossible.

2
Start with diffuse, wide-beam, low-magnification illumination

Get oriented to the overall external anatomy (lids, lashes, conjunctiva) before narrowing the beam; this wide overview prevents missing a gross finding while focused too early on fine detail.

3
Narrow to a thin optical section for the cornea and anterior chamber

A narrow (roughly 1mm or less) beam angled at 30-60 degrees from the viewing axis creates an optical cross-section, allowing you to localize a corneal opacity or foreign body to a specific depth (epithelium, stroma, endothelium) rather than just seeing that 'something is there.'

4
Use direct, indirect, retroillumination, and specular reflection as needed

Direct illumination for most surface detail; indirect (light aimed adjacent to, not directly at, the area of interest) for subtle edema or infiltrates; retroillumination (light reflected off the iris or fundus, viewed through the area of interest) is excellent for detecting subtle corneal or lens opacities backlit against the red reflex; specular reflection for endothelial detail.

5
Assess anterior chamber depth and cell/flare with a short, angled beam in a dark room

A short, thin beam angled through the anterior chamber in a darkened room lets you count cells (inflammatory cells appear as small bright specks moving in the aqueous, best seen against the dark pupil background) and note flare (a hazy, smoke-like light-scatter from increased aqueous protein) — this is the technique underlying anterior chamber grading in iritis/uveitis.

6
Instill fluorescein and use the cobalt blue filter for corneal epithelial assessment

Fluorescein pools in areas of epithelial defect and fluoresces green under cobalt blue light; use a wide beam for a general survey, then narrow to characterize specific staining patterns (dendrite, geographic ulcer, punctate staining).

7
Evert the upper lid when indicated

For any suspected retained foreign body, vertical linear corneal staining pattern, or unexplained persistent irritation, double-eversion of the upper lid over a cotton-tip applicator exposes the tarsal conjunctiva and superior fornix, a step that is easy to skip but frequently diagnostic.

Pearls
  • Retroillumination is underused relative to how much diagnostic information it provides — subtle findings invisible on direct illumination (early cataract, faint corneal scarring, fine keratic precipitates) often show up clearly backlit against the red reflex.
  • A dark room genuinely matters for anterior chamber cell/flare assessment; ambient light washes out the subtle light-scatter signal you're trying to detect.
  • Lid eversion is one of the highest-yield, most commonly skipped steps in the entire exam — make it a reflex for foreign body sensation, vertical staining patterns, or unexplained irritation.
Common Pitfalls
  • Rushing straight to high magnification/narrow beam without a wide-angle overview first can cause a gross finding (foreign body, obvious infiltrate) to be missed.
  • Poor patient positioning (chin/forehead not fully engaged) is a common source of exam frustration that's easily fixed by re-positioning rather than fighting the focus.

Tonometry Methods

Multiple tonometry methods exist, and they are not interchangeable — each has a different relationship to corneal thickness/biomechanics, meaning the 'same' IOP can read differently depending on which device measured it. Knowing which method you're using, and its typical bias relative to the Goldmann gold standard, matters when interpreting a borderline number.

Equipment
  • Goldmann applanation tonometer (mounted on slit lamp) — the gold standard
  • Tono-Pen (portable, handheld)
  • iCare rebound tonometer (portable, no topical anesthetic required)
  • Schiøtz tonometer (older indentation method, rarely used now)
  • Topical anesthetic and fluorescein (for Goldmann/Tono-Pen)
Technique Steps
1
Goldmann applanation tonometry (gold standard)

Instill topical anesthetic with fluorescein; align the tonometer prism on the slit lamp, applanate a fixed 3.06mm diameter area of cornea, and adjust the dial until the two fluorescein semicircles (mires) just touch at their inner edges when viewed through the prism under cobalt blue light. The dial reading (multiplied by 10) gives IOP in mmHg. Requires a cooperative, seated patient at the slit lamp.

2
Tono-Pen (portable applanation)

After topical anesthetic, gently touch the covered tip to the central cornea multiple times in quick succession; the device statistically analyzes the pulse waveforms and displays an averaged IOP with a confidence indicator. Useful for bedridden, supine, or poorly cooperative patients where slit lamp positioning isn't feasible.

3
iCare rebound tonometry

A lightweight, disposable probe is briefly and gently launched at the cornea and its deceleration on rebound is measured; this method notably does NOT require topical anesthetic, making it well-tolerated and particularly useful in children or needle-phobic patients.

4
Digital/finger palpation (last-resort estimate)

With the patient looking down and eyes closed, gently palpate the globe through the upper lid with both index fingers, alternating pressure, to grossly estimate whether IOP feels normal, soft, or rock-hard; this is a crude estimate reserved for situations where no device is available (e.g., suspected orbital compartment syndrome at bedside before/without a tonometer) and should be replaced with instrument-based measurement as soon as feasible.

Pearls
  • Both Tono-Pen and iCare tend to read HIGHER than Goldmann on average, with good but imperfect correlation (r roughly 0.7-0.8 in comparative studies) — a borderline elevated reading on either portable device is worth confirming with Goldmann when feasible, rather than acting on the portable reading alone.
  • Central corneal thickness (CCT) meaningfully affects all applanation-based methods (thicker corneas tend to overestimate true IOP, thinner corneas underestimate) but affects rebound tonometry (iCare) more than Goldmann in some comparative studies — this matters specifically in post-refractive-surgery or keratoconus patients with atypical corneal thickness/biomechanics.
  • iCare's lack of required topical anesthetic makes it a genuinely useful first-pass option in children or anxious patients, but confirm with Goldmann when a definitive number will change management (e.g., confirming resolution of acute angle closure).
Common Pitfalls
  • Treating a portable-device (Tono-Pen/iCare) reading as numerically interchangeable with a prior Goldmann reading on the same patient can create a false impression of IOP change when the difference is really just method-related bias.
  • Digital palpation is a gross estimate only and should never be the sole basis for a definitive clinical decision when an actual tonometer is available.

Fluorescein Staining & Seidel Test

Fluorescein dye pools in areas where the corneal epithelium is disrupted and fluoresces bright green under cobalt blue light, making it the single most useful bedside dye in ophthalmology for characterizing everything from simple abrasions to a full-thickness globe injury. The Seidel test is a specific application of this same dye to detect an active aqueous leak from a full-thickness corneal or scleral wound.

Equipment
  • Fluorescein-impregnated paper strips (preferred over fluorescein liquid in multi-use bottles, which carries a small but real risk of Pseudomonas contamination)
  • Cobalt blue light source (slit lamp filter, or a blue penlight/Wood's lamp as a lower-fidelity alternative)
  • Topical anesthetic (improves comfort and tolerance for the exam, doesn't affect staining pattern)
Technique Steps
1
Instill fluorescein

Moisten the strip tip with a drop of saline or topical anesthetic, then touch it briefly to the inferior palpebral conjunctiva or tear lake; ask the patient to blink several times to distribute the dye.

2
Examine under cobalt blue light for staining pattern

A well-demarcated area of green uptake indicates an epithelial defect (simple abrasion); a branching pattern with terminal bulbs indicates a dendrite (HSV keratitis, not a 'more severe abrasion'); diffuse fine punctate staining suggests a different process (dry eye, UV keratitis, toxic/medicamentosa reaction) than a single discrete defect.

3
Perform the Seidel test if full-thickness injury is suspected

Apply a more concentrated fluorescein strip directly to (or very close to, without pressure, if an open globe is suspected) the area of concern and observe under cobalt blue light: a POSITIVE Seidel test shows a dark rivulet of aqueous streaming through and diluting the surrounding concentrated (darker-appearing) fluorescein, visible as a lighter-green, flowing stream against the more concentrated orange-appearing dye pooled around it. This confirms an actively leaking full-thickness wound.

4
Document the pattern precisely

Note the size, shape, depth impression, and location of any staining, and explicitly document a positive or negative Seidel result when tested — this level of detail matters both clinically (tracking healing at follow-up) and medicolegally.

Pearls
  • A positive Seidel sign confirms an active full-thickness leak, but a NEGATIVE Seidel test does not fully exclude a self-sealing open globe injury — small wounds can seal themselves and stop leaking despite being a true full-thickness injury; clinical suspicion based on mechanism and other exam findings should still drive management even with a negative Seidel result.
  • A dendritic staining pattern is a different diagnosis, not a more severe version of a simple abrasion — recognizing this distinction at the slit lamp is what prevents inappropriately starting a topical steroid on an active HSV epithelial infection.
  • Fluorescein strips are preferred over multi-use liquid fluorescein bottles specifically because the liquid form has a documented history of Pseudomonas contamination outbreaks in clinical settings.
Common Pitfalls
  • Pressing firmly with the fluorescein strip directly against a suspected open globe to perform a 'standard' Seidel test risks extruding intraocular contents — approach gently, from a distance where possible, when open globe is a real concern.
  • Interpreting diffuse punctate staining as a single 'bad abrasion' rather than recognizing the pattern as suggestive of a different underlying process (dry eye, toxic drop reaction, UV keratitis) can lead to the wrong treatment plan.

Cycloplegics & Mydriatics Comparison

Cycloplegic agents paralyze the ciliary muscle (relieving accommodative spasm/pain) and secondarily dilate the pupil via unopposed sympathetic tone; pure mydriatics (phenylephrine) dilate the pupil directly via alpha-1 agonism without any cycloplegic/accommodation-paralyzing effect. Selection depends entirely on the clinical goal: comfort/synechiae-prevention in an inflamed eye calls for a cycloplegic with an appropriate duration; a diagnostic dilated fundus exam calls for the shortest-acting agent that achieves adequate dilation.

Pearls
  • Match duration to the clinical problem: don't send a patient home on 1-2 week atropine for a simple corneal abrasion that needed only brief comfort cycloplegia, and don't under-treat significant traumatic iritis with a short-acting agent that wears off before the inflammation does.
  • Phenylephrine is the one agent on this list that does NOT provide cycloplegia at all — it's purely a pupil dilator, which matters if the clinical goal is pain relief from ciliary spasm rather than just a dilated view.
  • Warn patients receiving any cycloplegic about expected blurred near vision and light sensitivity for the drug's duration, and advise against driving until they've assessed their own visual tolerance.

Topical Antibiotic Selection Framework

Topical antibiotic choice for ocular surface/corneal disease should be driven by three questions in sequence: (1) is this contact-lens-associated (raises Pseudomonas concern), (2) is this a simple surface process (conjunctivitis, uncomplicated abrasion) or a vision-threatening corneal infiltrate, and (3) does severity/location warrant standard commercial drops or compounded fortified antibiotics.

1
Simple bacterial conjunctivitis, non-contact-lens-wearer

Trimethoprim-polymyxin B or erythromycin ointment are reasonable first-line choices; most cases are self-limited regardless of antibiotic selected, and antibiotics primarily shorten the course and reduce transmission risk rather than being strictly necessary for resolution.

2
Simple corneal abrasion or conjunctivitis, contact-lens-associated

Switch to a fluoroquinolone (moxifloxacin 0.5% or ofloxacin 0.3%) specifically for reliable Pseudomonas coverage; this population has a meaningfully different risk profile than non-lens-wearers and should not receive the same default first-line agent.

3
Small, peripheral, low-risk corneal ulcer/infiltrate

Fourth-generation fluoroquinolone monotherapy (moxifloxacin or gatifloxacin), dosed hourly initially, has randomized trial evidence showing equivalence to fortified combination therapy for this severity tier — full detail and dosing schedule in the Bacterial Keratitis entry (Vol 3a).

4
Large, central, or severe corneal ulcer/infiltrate

Escalate to fortified antibiotics: fortified cefazolin or vancomycin (gram-positive) alternating with fortified tobramycin (gram-negative), compounded specially and dosed hourly round-the-clock initially. Obtain corneal culture before starting whenever feasible at this severity tier. Full detail in the Bacterial Keratitis entry (Vol 3a).

5
Any suspicion of a non-bacterial process before starting therapy

Fluorescein exam for a dendrite (HSV, changes to antiviral therapy, no steroids) and consider fungal risk factors (vegetative trauma, prior steroid use — changes to antifungal therapy, different culture media) before committing to an antibacterial-only approach; full detail in the HSV Keratitis and Fungal Keratitis entries (Vol 3a).

Pearls
  • The single highest-yield branch point in this entire framework is contact lens status — it should change your default antibiotic choice even for an apparently simple, low-severity presentation.
  • Culturing thresholds are size/location-based, not universal: small peripheral low-risk ulcers can reasonably proceed to empiric fluoroquinolone monotherapy without a culture; large, central, or severe ulcers should be cultured first whenever feasible.
Evidence Notes
  • RCTs (Constantinou et al. 2007; Sharma et al. 2013) established fourth-generation fluoroquinolone monotherapy as statistically equivalent to fortified combination therapy for non-severe bacterial keratitis, supporting current practice pattern guidance reserving fortified antibiotics for large/central/severe disease.

Topical Ocular Steroid Cautions

Topical corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory tools used throughout this atlas (uveitis, scleritis, stromal HSV keratitis, post-surgical inflammation), but they carry three specific, well-characterized risks that should be checked before every prescription: infectious mimic exacerbation, IOP elevation, and cataract formation with prolonged use.

RiskTimelineKey Point
Infectious mimic exacerbationCan occur within days of startingSteroids on an unrecognized HSV dendrite, fungal infiltrate, or bacterial ulcer can rapidly worsen the underlying infection — ALWAYS perform a fluorescein exam (and consider culture for a significant infiltrate) before the first drop, every time, regardless of how confident the diagnosis seems
IOP elevation (steroid response)Typically develops over the first few weeks of topical use, though can rarely occur within days in a highly sensitive 'early responder'Roughly one-third of the general population are 'steroid responders' who develop clinically meaningful IOP elevation with topical steroid exposure; check IOP at follow-up for any patient on more than a brief (days) steroid course, and more frequently for known responders or glaucoma-risk patients
Cataract formation (posterior subcapsular)Develops with prolonged/chronic use over months to years, not short coursesRelevant mainly for patients on long-term topical steroid therapy for chronic inflammatory disease (chronic uveitis, scleritis); not a practical concern for a typical days-to-weeks acute treatment course
1
Before the first drop: confirm no infectious mimic

Fluorescein exam to exclude a dendrite; consider the possibility of a fungal or atypical bacterial process based on history (vegetative trauma, prior steroid use, contact lens wear) before starting any ocular steroid.

2
Choose the lowest effective potency and frequency for the clinical problem

Mild disease (e.g., episcleritis) can often be managed with a lower-potency agent (fluorometholone, loteprednol) rather than defaulting to a more potent option (prednisolone acetate) reserved for more significant inflammation (uveitis, scleritis, stromal keratitis).

3
Check IOP at follow-up for any course beyond a few days

This is the single most common missed safety step in ocular steroid prescribing — a follow-up visit that doesn't include a pressure check on a patient using topical steroids is an incomplete visit.

4
Taper rather than stop abruptly for any course beyond brief/mild use

Sudden discontinuation after a significant course risks rebound inflammation; taper schedules should be individualized to the underlying disease and response, generally guided by ophthalmology for anything beyond a brief, mild course.

Pearls
  • The fluorescein-exam-before-steroids rule has no exceptions worth making — this single habit prevents one of the most common iatrogenic complications in ocular pharmacology.
  • "Steroid responder" status is common (roughly a third of the population) and not predictable from a patient's general health or steroid history in other body systems — check IOP, don't assume.
  • Cataract risk is a long-term-use concern, not a reason to avoid an appropriately indicated short acute course for a condition that genuinely needs steroid therapy.
Evidence Notes
  • Landmark work by Armaly and Becker established that individuals vary widely in IOP responsiveness to topical glucocorticoids, with roughly one-third of the general population classified as clinically significant steroid responders, underlying current recommendations for routine IOP monitoring during topical steroid courses.

Topical Anesthetic "Bridge" Therapy Controversy

The traditional, near-universal teaching (still reflected in major EM textbooks) is an absolute prohibition on dispensing any topical anesthetic for home/outpatient use in corneal abrasion management, based on historical case reports of severe corneal complications with chronic, unsupervised, heavy misuse. A meaningfully different body of evidence has accumulated specifically around short-term (roughly 24-48 hour), dilute, monitored use, and this atlas presents both positions rather than picking one as settled.

1
The traditional position (textbook standard, currently still the more conservative and widely-taught default)

Never dispense topical anesthetic for home use under any circumstances; rely on oral analgesia, cycloplegia, and antibiotic prophylaxis instead. This remains the position of major emergency medicine reference texts (Rosen's, Tintinalli's) and is the safer default in a busy practice without a structured short-course protocol.

2
The evolving evidence-based position (growing RCT and cohort support for short, monitored courses)

Multiple randomized controlled trials (dilute proparacaine, tetracaine) and at least one large retrospective cohort study have found short-term (up to 24 hours, dosed roughly every 30 minutes as needed) topical anesthetic use for simple, uncomplicated corneal abrasions provides significantly better pain control than placebo, with no evidence of delayed healing or serious complications in these specific study populations. A 2020 double-blind RCT found a large, clinically significant reduction in pain scores at 24-48 hour follow-up with short-course tetracaine versus placebo, and reduced opioid/hydrocodone use in the treatment group.

3
What both positions agree on

Chronic, unsupervised, heavy long-term misuse of topical anesthetics IS dangerous and has been linked to severe complications including corneal melt — this is not in dispute. The controversy is specifically about brief, dose-limited, monitored courses (roughly 24 hours) for simple, uncomplicated abrasions, not about unrestricted long-term use, which remains universally discouraged.

4
A reasonable practical approach given the divided evidence

If choosing to use a short-course protocol, this should be a deliberate, informed practice decision (not an ad hoc individual prescribing choice), limited strictly to simple, uncomplicated abrasions (excluding contact-lens-associated, organic-material, or infiltrate-associated cases), for a clearly bounded duration (typically no more than 24 hours), with explicit patient instructions and a firm follow-up plan given the diagnostic uncertainty that still exists in this evolving evidence space.

Pearls
  • This atlas deliberately presents this as an unresolved tension rather than a settled answer — the RCT evidence for short-course safety is real, but major reference texts haven't yet fully caught up to it, and that gap is itself clinically relevant to know about.
  • The historical case reports underlying the traditional prohibition involved chronic, heavy, unsupervised misuse, not the short, bounded courses studied in the more recent trials — conflating the two overstates the risk of the specific practice being newly studied.
  • Regardless of which position a given practice adopts, the point of clear consensus (unsupervised long-term use is dangerous) should never be relaxed.
Evidence Notes
  • Shipman et al. 2020 (Annals of Emergency Medicine): double-blind RCT of 111 patients found significantly lower pain scores with 24-hour tetracaine versus placebo (NRS 1 vs. 8 at follow-up) with no attributable complications noted.
  • A 2017 large retrospective cohort study found no evidence that 24-hour topical tetracaine use for simple corneal abrasions was unsafe, though confidence intervals were wide and some increased risk signal was observed specifically for abrasions from non-simple/complicated mechanisms, reinforcing that any short-course approach should be restricted to simple, uncomplicated cases.
  • A 2015 systematic review of six total studies (2 ED-based RCTs, 4 post-PRK-surgery studies) found short-course dilute topical anesthetic effective without adverse effects or delayed healing across all six, while also concluding that available data remain limited enough that safety for routine outpatient use is not yet fully established beyond these specific study conditions.

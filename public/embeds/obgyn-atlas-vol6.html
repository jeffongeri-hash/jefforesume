Gynecologic Emergencies

The non-pregnant gynecologic presentations that show up on an ER shift: the ones where minutes matter, the ones where the diagnosis is easy to miss, and the ones where a same-looking abscess is either a five-minute office procedure or a septic-shock admission.

01

Ovarian Torsion

The gynecologic emergency where imaging reassurance is the trap — a normal-looking Doppler study does not rule this out, and knowing that cold is the single highest-yield fact in this module.

The pitfall that costs ovaries

Normal or present Doppler flow does not exclude torsion. The ovary has a dual blood supply (ovarian artery + uterine artery branch), so partial or intermittent torsion can preserve detectable arterial and/or venous flow — one study found this in up to 60% of confirmed torsion cases. ACOG states plainly: there are no clinical or imaging criteria sufficient to confirm or exclude the diagnosis preoperatively. This is a clinical diagnosis supported by imaging, not an imaging diagnosis — a reassuring ultrasound in a patient with a compelling clinical picture should not delay gynecology consultation.

Presentation & Risk Factors

  • Classic picture: sudden-onset, severe unilateral pelvic pain, often with nausea/vomiting; pain may be intermittent if the adnexa is partially or intermittently torsing and detorsing spontaneously.
  • An adnexal mass is present in 86–95% of cases (mature cystic teratoma is the single most common lead point in reproductive-age and pediatric patients) — but torsion of a normal, non-cystic ovary does occur, more often in pediatric/adolescent patients (absence of a mass in up to 46% of that age group vs. much less common in adults).
  • Ovarian cancer is a rare cause of the underlying mass in adolescents/young adults specifically — this shouldn't be the driving fear in a young patient, though it becomes more relevant with increasing age and a complex-appearing mass.

Imaging — What to Look For

  • Ovarian enlargement is the most consistent finding — a torsed ovary is essentially always enlarged, often ≥2.5× the volume of the contralateral ovary, from venous/lymphatic congestion.
  • The "whirlpool sign" (twisted vascular pedicle on color Doppler) is specific when seen but frequently absent.
  • Doppler flow findings should inform, not solely determine, the decision to proceed to surgery — correlate with the clinical picture every time.
  • MRI or CT can help in equivocal ultrasound cases, particularly when an alternative diagnosis (e.g., endometrioma) is suspected, but should not delay surgical consultation when clinical suspicion is high.

Management

1
Gynecology consultation for diagnostic laparoscopy when suspicion is high — don't wait for a "confirmatory" imaging study that doesn't exist.
2
Detorsion with adnexal preservation is standard, regardless of how ischemic/discolored the ovary appears intraoperatively — ovaries that look frankly necrotic at the time of surgery frequently recover meaningful function after untwisting. Oophorectomy is reserved for an ovary so necrotic it is falling apart, not for one that merely looks dusky or dark.
3
Cystectomy does not need to happen at the same time as detorsion — it can be deferred to a second-look procedure if the tissue needs time to demarcate.
4
Oophoropexy (fixing the ovary to prevent recurrence) is not routinely recommended — recurrence is uncommon and the supporting data for prophylactic fixation is limited.

This is a fertility-preservation emergency as much as a pain emergency — the operative approach described above exists specifically because timely, conservative surgical management preserves ovarian function that a "wait and see" approach or reflexive oophorectomy would sacrifice.

02

Ruptured Ovarian Cyst

Most cases are a same-day discharge; a minority are a hemorrhage emergency — the hemodynamic exam, not the ultrasound appearance alone, is what separates them.

Presentation

Sudden-onset unilateral pelvic pain, classically mid-cycle (ruptured functional/corpus luteum cyst around ovulation) or with intercourse/exertion as a precipitant. Pain may radiate to the shoulder if hemoperitoneum irritates the diaphragm — a useful, often-missed clue pointing toward intraperitoneal blood rather than a simple ruptured cyst without significant bleeding.

Management

  • Hemodynamically stable, small free fluid: supportive management — analgesia, observation, outpatient follow-up. This is the majority of presentations.
  • Hemodynamically stable but larger hemoperitoneum: serial hemoglobin checks and a period of ED/observation-unit monitoring before committing to discharge, since ongoing slow bleeding can declare itself over hours.
  • Hemodynamically unstable: this is a hemorrhage resuscitation — IV access, fluids/blood products, and emergent gynecology consultation for likely surgical management (laparoscopy for hemostasis, cystectomy, or oophorectomy if bleeding cannot otherwise be controlled).
  • Anticoagulated patients and those with an underlying bleeding disorder warrant a lower threshold for observation even with reassuring initial vitals, given reduced physiologic buffer against ongoing hemorrhage.
Don't forget the differential

A positive pregnancy test changes everything here — ruptured ectopic pregnancy (Vol. 1, Module 6) is the diagnosis that must be excluded before settling on a ruptured functional cyst as the explanation for hemoperitoneum in a reproductive-age patient.

03

PID & Tubo-Ovarian Abscess

Per the 2021 CDC STI Treatment Guidelines — the diagnostic bar is deliberately low, because the cost of under-treating is higher than the cost of over-treating.

Diagnosis — The Minimum Criteria Are Intentionally Low

CDC's explicit standard

Empiric treatment is recommended for any sexually active woman with pelvic or lower abdominal pain, no other identifiable cause, and one or more of: cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, or adnexal tenderness. Fever, elevated WBC, mucopurulent discharge, and confirmed gonorrhea/chlamydia all support the diagnosis but are not required — up to a third of confirmed PID cases have negative gonorrhea/chlamydia testing, since Gardnerella, H. influenzae, and other vaginal-flora organisms increasingly account for a meaningful share of cases.

More specific (but not required for empiric treatment) confirmatory findings: endometrial biopsy showing endometritis, TVUS/MRI showing thickened fluid-filled tubes or a tubo-ovarian complex, or laparoscopic findings consistent with PID.

Treatment Regimens

  • All regimens must cover N. gonorrhoeae and C. trachomatis — negative endocervical screening for these does not rule out upper-tract infection, so empiric coverage is universal regardless of test results.
  • Outpatient: ceftriaxone (IM) plus doxycycline 100mg PO BID for 14 days, plus metronidazole 500mg PO BID for 14 days.
  • Parenteral (preferred regimen): a cephalosporin (e.g., cefotetan or cefoxitin) plus doxycycline; alternative regimen is clindamycin plus gentamicin. Transition to oral therapy (doxycycline + metronidazole) usually possible within 24–48 hours of clinical improvement, to complete a total 14-day course.
  • Because doxycycline IV is painful and has equivalent bioavailability to oral, give it orally when possible even in an otherwise-hospitalized patient.
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess specifically: favor a regimen with reliable anaerobic coverage (clindamycin-containing, or ampicillin-sulbactam plus doxycycline) — metronidazole alone alongside a cephalosporin/doxycycline backbone is also effective and commonly used. Minimum >24 hours of inpatient observation is recommended for TOA specifically.

Fitz-Hugh-Curtis Syndrome

Perihepatitis complicating PID in an estimated 8–10% of cases — presents with right-upper-quadrant or referred right-shoulder pain and can mimic cholecystitis or other RUQ pathology. Elevated transaminases support the diagnosis; treatment is the same PID regimen, not a separate hepatic workup once recognized.

TOA rupture is a surgical emergency

A ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess causing diffuse peritonitis and septic shock is a distinct, immediately surgical emergency — this is the scenario where antibiotics alone are no longer sufficient and emergent gynecology/surgical involvement for source control cannot wait on an antibiotic trial.

04

Bartholin Gland Abscess

Usually a straightforward office/ED procedure — with one age-based decision point that changes the entire conversation.

The Age Rule

Age ≥40 changes the management

Bartholin gland involution normally occurs by around age 30 — a new Bartholin mass in a patient 40 or older (or any age with an irregular, nodular, or recurrent mass) should prompt biopsy or excision to rule out Bartholin gland carcinoma, a rare but real malignancy. This is the single fact most likely to change management in this module: simple drainage alone is not adequate management in this age group without tissue diagnosis.

Management by Setting

  • Word catheter placement is the standard ED/office-based approach for an uncomplicated abscess: small stab incision, insert the balloon catheter, inflate with 2–4mL saline, leave in place 2–4 weeks to allow a fistulous tract (permanent drainage channel) to epithelialize. Fast (minutes), well-tolerated under local anesthesia.
  • Simple incision and drainage alone (without a Word catheter or packing) is the quickest option but carries a recurrence rate approaching 40%, since the incision tends to close before a permanent tract forms.
  • Marsupialization (suturing the cyst wall edges to the skin to create a permanently open pouch) is a more definitive outpatient-surgical-suite procedure, generally reserved for recurrent cases or performed by gynecology rather than in the ED — some sources reserve it specifically for cysts rather than acute abscesses, given a theoretically higher hematoma/infection risk when performed on actively infected tissue, though comparative outcome data on this point is mixed.
  • Antibiotics are not routinely necessary for an uncomplicated abscess after adequate drainage — reserve them for evidence of surrounding cellulitis, systemic signs of infection, or a concurrent STI.
Don't miss the outlier

Bartholin abscess is usually a benign, localized process — but case reports document progression to septic shock requiring ICU-level sepsis management, disproportionately in older patients. Treat systemic signs (fever, tachycardia out of proportion to a localized abscess, or any hemodynamic change) as a reason to broaden the workup rather than assuming the abscess alone explains the whole picture.

Acute Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

Per ACOG Committee Opinion 557 — the acute-management playbook for bleeding heavy enough to need an emergency, not a clinic-visit, response.

Step 1 — Hemodynamic Assessment Comes Before Etiology

Assess for tachycardia, orthostasis, or hypotension before working through the cause. A pregnancy test is mandatory in every reproductive-age patient regardless of stated contraceptive use or reported last menstrual period — this changes the entire differential and management pathway (Vol. 1, Module 6 if pregnant).

Classify by PALM-COEIN — But Treat the Acuity First

The PALM-COEIN system (Polyp, Adenomyosis, Leiomyoma, Malignancy/hyperplasia — structural; Coagulopathy, Ovulatory dysfunction, Endometrial, Iatrogenic, Not otherwise classified — nonstructural) organizes the differential, but acute hemodynamically significant bleeding gets stabilized first and classified after — don't let etiology workup delay hemostatic treatment in an unstable patient.

Toxic Shock Syndrome

Menstrual TSS is rarer than it was in the 1980s tampon-era peak, but it hasn't disappeared — and the case definition is a genuinely useful bedside checklist.

CDC Case Definition — Clinical Criteria

  • Fever ≥102°F (38.9°C)
  • Diffuse macular erythroderma ("sunburn-like" rash)
  • Desquamation, particularly palms and soles, 1–2 weeks after illness onset (a finding that confirms the diagnosis retrospectively, not something to wait for acutely)
  • Hypotension — systolic BP ≤90mmHg in adults
  • Multi-system involvement — three or more of: GI (vomiting/diarrhea), muscular (severe myalgia or elevated CK), mucous membrane hyperemia (vaginal, oropharyngeal, or conjunctival), renal (elevated BUN/Cr or pyuria without infection), hepatic (elevated transaminases/bilirubin), hematologic (platelets ≤100,000), CNS (disorientation without focal signs, in the absence of fever/hypotension explaining it alone)

Mechanism & Risk Factors

Caused by TSST-1 (or related superantigen) produced by Staphylococcus aureus, most classically associated with prolonged high-absorbency tampon use, but also seen with other prolonged vaginal foreign bodies (contraceptive sponges, diaphragms left in place too long) and, importantly, with non-menstrual sources (surgical wounds, skin/soft tissue infections, postpartum). A retained foreign body should be actively sought and removed as part of initial management in any suspected case.

Management

1
Remove the source — retained tampon or other foreign body, immediately.
2
Aggressive fluid resuscitation — capillary leak from the superantigen-driven cytokine cascade causes large fluid shifts; patients often need substantial volume.
3
Antistaphylococcal antibiotics with anti-toxin activity — a protein-synthesis inhibitor (clindamycin) is specifically included to shut down further toxin production, typically combined with a beta-lactam or vancomycin depending on MRSA risk.
4
ICU-level supportive care — this progresses to multi-organ dysfunction and can be rapidly fatal without aggressive early management.
07

Vulvovaginal Emergencies

The presentations where a delayed diagnosis, not just a missed one, is what causes the bad outcome.

Necrotizing Vulvar Infection (Fournier's Gangrene Spectrum)

Pain out of proportion to exam findings is the key red flag

Necrotizing soft-tissue infection of the vulva/perineum is rare but carries the same lethality as Fournier's gangrene in men, and shares the same core red flag: pain disproportionate to the visible skin findings, rapidly progressive erythema/edema, crepitus (not always present), skin discoloration or bullae, and systemic toxicity out of proportion to what looks like "just cellulitis" on inspection. Risk factors mirror Fournier's generally: diabetes, immunosuppression, obesity, recent surgery or trauma to the area.

Management is surgical, not medical — emergent surgical debridement is the definitive treatment, and broad-spectrum antibiotics (covering gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms) are an adjunct, not a substitute, for source control. Imaging should never delay surgical consultation when clinical suspicion is high; this is a "call surgery now, image in parallel if it doesn't delay the call" presentation.

Retained Vaginal Foreign Body

Most commonly a forgotten tampon — presents with malodorous discharge, sometimes with vaginal bleeding or irritation from local mucosal irritation, and is a specific entity to actively search for in any TSS evaluation (Module 6) as well as its own straightforward diagnosis. Speculum exam and removal is usually sufficient; a genuinely foul, purulent discharge in this setting still warrants recognizing this as a potential TSS-precipitating scenario.

Genital Trauma / Straddle Injuries

  • Straddle injuries (most common mechanism in pediatric/adolescent genital trauma) typically cause superficial, anterior, asymmetric injury from compression against a hard surface — a pattern that's usually reassuring for accidental mechanism.
  • Injury patterns raising concern for abuse/assault instead: posterior fourchette/hymenal involvement, injuries inconsistent with the stated mechanism, or any penetrating injury — these warrant a structured, trauma-informed evaluation pathway rather than assuming an accidental straddle mechanism.
  • Significant vulvar hematoma can require surgical evacuation if expanding or causing urinary retention from mass effect; most smaller hematomas are managed conservatively with ice and analgesia.
  • A calm, trauma-informed examination approach matters clinically as well as ethically — a rushed or poorly explained exam in an already frightened patient (particularly a child or assault survivor) can itself become a secondary harm.

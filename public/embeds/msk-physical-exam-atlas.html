MSK Physical Exam Atlas

Animated range of motion for every extremity and the spine, MRC strength grading with the lever-arm principle, 23 special tests with real diagnostic performance data, palpation landmark maps, red-flag significance, and defensible documentation — including the patient with a pre-existing deficit.

Range of motion norms below follow the AAOS reference standard. Each diagram animates the sweep from 0° (neutral) through the end of normal range — the dashed line is the 0° reference, the ghosted limb is the end position. Norms are a guide: actual values vary with age, sex, and body habitus, and THE PATIENT’S CONTRALATERAL SIDE IS ALWAYS THE BETTER CONTROL.

Shoulder

Always compare to the contralateral side. Scapulothoracic substitution (shoulder hiking) falsely inflates apparent glenohumeral motion — stabilize the scapula to isolate true GH ROM.

Flexion 0–180°

Arm forward and overhead. Loss is early in adhesive capsulitis and rotator cuff pathology.

Extension 0–60°

Arm posteriorly. Often overlooked; limited after anterior capsule tightening.

Abduction 0–180°

A painful arc between 60–120° suggests subacromial impingement; pain at 170–180° suggests AC joint pathology.

External rotation 0–90°

The FIRST motion lost in adhesive capsulitis — a highly useful discriminator from rotator cuff disease.

Internal rotation 0–70°

Often documented functionally by the highest spinal level the thumb reaches behind the back (e.g., 'IR to L3').

Elbow & Forearm

A fixed flexion contracture (loss of terminal extension) is the earliest and most functionally important elbow deficit — document the extension lag, not just the flexion arc.

Flexion 0–150°

Functional arc for most ADLs is roughly 30–130°.

Extension 0° (full)

0° = fully straight. Up to ~10° hyperextension can be normal (cubitus recurvatus), especially in women. Document a contracture as e.g. '−15° extension lag'.

Supination 0–80°

Test with the elbow at 90° tucked at the side — otherwise shoulder rotation substitutes.

Pronation 0–80°

Loss suggests radial head, DRUJ, or interosseous membrane pathology.

Wrist & Hand

For digits, Total Active Motion (TAM) = (MCP + PIP + DIP flexion) − (any extension lag) — the standard hand-surgery summary metric.

Wrist flexion 0–80°

Phalen's position. Sustained flexion reproducing paresthesias suggests carpal tunnel syndrome.

Wrist extension 0–70°

Functional wrist arc is ~10° flexion to 35° extension for most ADLs.

Radial deviation 0–20°

The smaller of the two deviations — blocked by the radial styloid.

Ulnar deviation 0–30°

Pain here with an ulnar-sided click suggests TFCC pathology.

MCP flexion 0–90°

Immobilize MCPs in ~70–90° flexion (intrinsic-plus/safe position) to prevent collateral ligament contracture.

PIP flexion 0–100°

PIP stiffness is the most functionally devastating finger contracture — immobilize PIPs in EXTENSION, never flexion.

Hip

Lumbar spine motion readily substitutes for hip motion. Flex the contralateral hip (Thomas test position) to flatten lordosis before measuring — otherwise you will overestimate hip extension.

Flexion 0–120°

120° with knee flexed; ~90° with knee extended (hamstring-limited).

Extension 0–20°

A flexion contracture (Thomas test positive) is common in hip OA and often missed.

Abduction 0–40°

Stabilize the pelvis (hand on contralateral ASIS) — pelvic tilt otherwise fakes abduction.

Adduction 0–20°

Cross the contralateral limb out of the way first.

Internal rotation 0–45°

LOSS OF INTERNAL ROTATION IS THE EARLIEST SIGN OF HIP OA — and painful, restricted IR in a febrile patient is a red flag for septic hip.

External rotation 0–45°

A hip held in flexion/abduction/external rotation at rest is the classic septic hip posture (maximizes capsular volume).

Knee

Always measure extension first and document any lag separately from the flexion arc — an extension lag is far more disabling than lost terminal flexion.

Flexion 0–135°

Needs ~110° to rise from a chair, ~120° for stairs. A tense effusion mechanically blocks terminal flexion.

Extension 0° (to −10°)

Up to ~10° hyperextension (genu recurvatum) can be normal. Distinguish an EXTENSION LAG (active loss, intact passive — extensor mechanism problem) from a FLEXION CONTRACTURE (passive loss too — a mechanical block).

Ankle & Foot

Measure dorsiflexion with the knee both extended and flexed — if DF improves with the knee flexed, the restriction is gastrocnemius (Silfverskiold test); if unchanged, it is soleus or the joint itself.

Dorsiflexion 0–20°

Needs ≥10° for normal gait. Equinus contracture drives midfoot breakdown and forefoot ulceration in diabetics.

Plantarflexion 0–50°

Weakness of resisted PF (or inability to do a single-leg heel raise) suggests Achilles rupture — confirm with Thompson test.

Subtalar inversion 0–35°

The inversion mechanism of the classic lateral ankle sprain (ATFL first).

Subtalar eversion 0–15°

Much smaller than inversion — which is exactly why eversion (deltoid) injuries are rarer but more serious.

Cervical Spine

In trauma, do NOT test cervical ROM until the spine is cleared (NEXUS/Canadian C-spine). Active ROM testing is a clearance step, not a screening step.

Flexion 0–45°

Chin-to-chest. Roughly 50% of cervical flexion/extension occurs at the occiput–C1–C2 complex.

Extension 0–45°

Extension + rotation narrows the neuroforamen (Spurling position) and can reproduce radicular pain.

Lateral flexion 0–45°

Ear toward shoulder without shoulder hiking.

Rotation 0–60°

~50% of cervical rotation happens at C1–C2 alone. <45° rotation bilaterally is a Canadian C-spine rule imaging trigger.

Thoracolumbar Spine

Visual estimation of lumbar ROM is notoriously unreliable. The modified Schober test is the reproducible bedside measure: mark 10 cm above and 5 cm below the PSIS line; normal flexion increases that 15 cm span by ≥5 cm.

Flexion 0–80°

Much of apparent 'lumbar flexion' is actually hip flexion — use Schober to separate them.

Extension 0–25°

Pain WORSE with extension and relieved by flexion/sitting suggests spinal stenosis or facet arthropathy.

Lateral flexion 0–35°

Asymmetry is more informative than the absolute number.

Rotation 0–45°

Stabilize the pelvis (seated testing) — most apparent trunk rotation otherwise comes from the hips.

The MRC 0–5 scale is the universal language of motor exam — but it is only reproducible if you standardize position, stabilization, and where you place your hand. That last variable is the one most often fumbled, and it is exactly what your attending was demonstrating.

The Lever Arm Principle — Why Hand Placement Changes the Grade

This is the single most under-taught source of inter-rater disagreement in manual muscle testing. Torque = force × distance from the joint axis. The further DISTALLY you apply resistance, the longer your lever arm, the more torque you generate for the same effort — and the weaker the patient appears.

Concrete example (knee extension, quadriceps): if you push down at the DISTAL FEMUR just above the patella, your lever arm is short and you have little mechanical advantage — almost any patient will hold. Move your hand to the ANKLE and your lever arm roughly triples; the same quadriceps now 'breaks.' Same muscle, same patient, two different grades — purely from hand placement.

Your attending pressing at the distal femur versus at the ankle is demonstrating exactly this: it is a lever-arm demonstration, not two different tests. The lesson is that a grade is meaningless unless the hand position is standardized and reproducible.

The convention: STANDARD MMT APPLIES RESISTANCE DISTALLY on the segment being moved, just proximal to the next distal joint (for knee extension, that is just above the ankle). Applying resistance more proximally is a legitimate modification — but it inflates the grade, so it must be documented.

Recognized exceptions to distal resistance: (1) pain or a healing wound at the distal site; (2) fracture, hardware, or skin breakdown you must avoid loading; (3) certain muscles tested more functionally with a longer lever (e.g., the rhomboids are resisted at the arm rather than the scapula, because the longer lever better reflects functional demand). If you deviate, say so in the note.

Break Test vs. Make Test

BREAK TEST (the standard): the patient holds the limb at the end of available range while you gradually build resistance over 2–3 seconds, trying to 'break' the hold. Build force smoothly — never jerk. The grade reflects the force needed to break the position.

MAKE TEST: the patient pushes maximally against your fixed, immovable resistance. Less standardized manually, but this is what handheld dynamometry effectively measures.

Apply and release resistance GRADUALLY. A sudden application catches the patient before recruitment is complete and falsely grades them weak — a very common bedside error.

Grade 3 Is the Pivot — and Grade 4 Is the Problem

Grade 3 (full ROM against gravity, no added resistance) is the critical dividing point of the whole scale. Establish grade 3 FIRST. If the patient cannot achieve it, re-test in a gravity-eliminated plane for grades 0–2; if they can, add resistance to sort 4 vs. 5. Grades 0, 1, and 2 are tested gravity-minimized; grades 3–5 are tested against gravity.

Grade 4 is a notoriously wide bucket — it spans everything from 'barely resists' to 'nearly normal,' which is why clinicians append 4−/4/4+. Be aware the evidence suggests these intermediate grades do NOT meaningfully improve inter-rater agreement. For serial tracking of a specific patient, handheld dynamometry is more reliable than MRC in the 4–5 range; MRC is most reliable at the weak end (0–3).

Always test the unaffected side first to establish that patient's baseline — 'normal' is relative to them, not to a textbook.

Clinical Pearls
  • Give-way weakness (ratchety, inconsistent 'catch and release') usually reflects pain inhibition or submaximal effort — not a true neurologic lesion. Document it as such ('give-way, effort-limited by pain') rather than assigning a hard number you do not believe.
  • Pain inhibits effort. A grade in a painful limb is a measure of pain tolerance as much as strength — say so in the note rather than pretending the number is clean.
  • Distinguish weakness patterns: proximal-predominant suggests myopathy; distal-predominant suggests neuropathy; a single myotome suggests radiculopathy; a peripheral nerve distribution suggests mononeuropathy.
  • In a suspected compartment syndrome, weakness is a LATE finding — never wait for it. Pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch come first.

A blunt truth from the evidence: most shoulder 'special tests' perform far worse than their reputation. The 2007/2012 Hegedus meta-analyses found NO impingement or AC-joint test with meaningful stand-alone diagnostic accuracy. Use them in CLUSTERS, know whether each rules in or rules out, and never let one positive test override the history.

Neer Impingement Sign

Subacromial impingement
TechniqueStabilize the scapula. Passively internally rotate the arm and forcibly forward-flex it fully overhead, driving the greater tuberosity under the coracoacromial arch.
PositivePain in the anterolateral shoulder during terminal forward flexion.
PerformancePooled sensitivity ~72–79%, specificity ~53–60% (Hegedus meta-analysis). Sensitive but NOT specific — useful to help rule OUT when negative; a positive tells you little.
PearlPassive terminal elevation hurts almost any inflamed shoulder. Treat a positive Neer as 'something in this shoulder hurts,' not as a diagnosis.

Hawkins-Kennedy Test

Subacromial impingement
TechniqueFlex the shoulder to 90° with the elbow flexed to 90°, then forcibly internally rotate the shoulder — driving the supraspinatus tendon under the coracoacromial ligament.
PositivePain with internal rotation at 90° of forward flexion.
PerformancePooled sensitivity ~79%, specificity ~59% (Hegedus). Like Neer: rules out better than it rules in.
PearlNeer and Hawkins are highly correlated — doing both adds little independent information. Pair them with a strength test (empty can, external rotation lag) instead.

Empty Can (Jobe) Test

Supraspinatus tear / tendinopathy
TechniqueAbduct to 90° in the scapular plane (~30° forward), internally rotate fully (thumb down, 'emptying a can'), and apply a downward force while the patient resists.
PositivePain AND/OR weakness. Weakness is the more meaningful finding.
PerformancePooled sensitivity ~69%, specificity ~62%. Separating pain from true weakness is what carries the diagnostic value.
PearlThe full can test (thumb up) is better tolerated and performs comparably — many clinicians now prefer it because it provokes less impingement pain and better isolates true weakness.

External Rotation Lag Sign

Infraspinatus / supraspinatus tear
TechniqueElbow at 90°, shoulder at ~20° abduction. Passively bring the arm to near-maximal external rotation, then ask the patient to HOLD it and let go.
PositiveThe arm drifts (lags) back into internal rotation — they cannot hold the position.
PerformanceOne of the few shoulder tests with BOTH good sensitivity and specificity per Hegedus. A true lag is close to structural.
PearlLag signs are far more useful than pain-provocation tests. A lag means the tendon cannot hold the load — that is structure, not irritation.

Lift-Off / Belly-Press Test

Subscapularis tear
TechniqueLift-off: dorsum of the hand on the mid-lumbar back; ask them to lift the hand away. Belly-press: press the palm into the abdomen while keeping the elbow forward.
PositiveInability to lift the hand off the back, or the elbow dropping backward during belly-press.
PerformanceLift-off pooled sensitivity ~42%, specificity ~97%, LR+ ~16. Poor screening test, excellent RULE-IN test.
PearlUse belly-press when the patient lacks the internal rotation to get the hand behind the back — common in the stiff or elderly shoulder.

Drop Arm Test

Large/massive rotator cuff tear
TechniquePassively abduct the arm to 90–160°, then ask the patient to slowly lower it to the side under control.
PositiveThe arm drops abruptly, or they cannot control the descent.
PerformanceHigh specificity, low sensitivity — a rule-IN test for a substantial tear.
PearlA positive drop arm in an acute traumatic shoulder warrants prompt orthopedic referral — acute cuff tears in younger patients are repaired earlier.

Apprehension / Relocation Test

Anterior glenohumeral instability
TechniqueSupine. Abduct to 90° and externally rotate. Apprehension = the patient guards or reports the shoulder feels like it will dislocate. Then apply a posteriorly-directed force on the humeral head (relocation) — apprehension should resolve.
PositiveApprehension (NOT just pain) that is abolished by the relocation force.
PerformancePer Hegedus, apprehension/relocation/anterior release are among the short list of shoulder tests with both good sensitivity and specificity.
PearlAPPREHENSION, not pain, is the positive. Pain alone with this maneuver is nonspecific. The look on the patient's face is the test.

Spurling Test

Cervical radiculopathy
TechniqueExtend, laterally flex, and rotate the neck TOWARD the symptomatic side, then apply gentle axial compression — narrowing the neuroforamen.
PositiveReproduction of RADICULAR pain/paresthesia radiating into the arm (not just neck pain).
PerformanceHighly specific, poorly sensitive — a strong rule-IN test for radiculopathy.
PearlNever perform in trauma before the C-spine is cleared, or with suspected myelopathy/instability. Local neck pain alone is a NEGATIVE test.

Phalen & Tinel (Carpal Tunnel)

Median neuropathy at the wrist
TechniquePhalen: hold full wrist flexion ~60 seconds. Tinel: percuss over the carpal tunnel.
PositiveParesthesias in the median distribution (thumb, index, middle, radial half of ring).
PerformanceBoth are moderate at best; Phalen slightly more sensitive, Tinel slightly more specific. Neither is diagnostic alone.
PearlThe most useful bedside findings for carpal tunnel are actually thenar atrophy and abnormal monofilament/two-point discrimination — not Phalen/Tinel. Nocturnal symptoms and the 'flick sign' carry more weight than either.

Finkelstein / Eichhoff Test

De Quervain tenosynovitis
TechniquePatient makes a fist over the flexed thumb; examiner ulnarly deviates the wrist (Eichhoff). True Finkelstein: examiner passively pulls the thumb into flexion/adduction.
PositiveSharp pain over the first dorsal compartment at the radial styloid.
PerformanceSensitive for de Quervain but poorly specific — also positive in first CMC arthritis (a very common confounder).
PearlAlways test the CMC grind separately. Radial-sided wrist pain in an older adult is far more often thumb CMC arthritis than de Quervain.
Clinical Pearls
  • The most valuable shoulder screening cluster is: painful arc + empty can/full can + external rotation lag. Multiple positives raise post-test probability far more than any single test.
  • For a shoulder that cannot actively elevate after trauma, the question is not 'which impingement test is positive' — it is 'is this a cuff tear, a nerve injury (axillary), or a fracture?' Check deltoid/axillary sensation and get imaging.
  • Document special tests by NAME and RESULT, not just 'special tests negative' — a defensible note says which tests you performed.

Lower extremity special tests generally outperform shoulder tests — the Lachman and pivot shift in particular have solid, replicated numbers. But note the 2022 Sokal meta-analysis: the Lachman's accuracy has historically been OVERESTIMATED, and once trials excluding concomitant ligament injury are pooled properly, the common ACL tests perform comparably to one another.

Lachman Test

ACL tear
TechniqueKnee flexed 20–30°. Stabilize the femur with one hand, apply an anterior force to the PROXIMAL TIBIA with the other. Assess translation AND endpoint quality.
PositiveIncreased anterior translation with a SOFT or absent endpoint.
PerformanceSokal 2022 bivariate pooled: sensitivity 81% (73–87), specificity 85% (73–92). Older reviews reported ~85%/94% — now considered overestimated.
PearlThe ENDPOINT is the test, not the millimeters. A soft endpoint with modest translation beats a firm endpoint with big translation. Hamstring guarding and a tense hemarthrosis both cause false negatives — which is why the exam under anesthesia is so much more sensitive.

Anterior Drawer Test

ACL tear
TechniqueSupine, hip 45°, knee 90°, foot stabilized. Grip the proximal tibia and pull anteriorly. Confirm the hamstrings are relaxed.
PositiveIncreased anterior tibial translation vs. the contralateral knee.
PerformanceSokal 2022 pooled: sensitivity 83%, specificity 85%. Notably better in CHRONIC/post-acute than acute injuries.
PearlPoor in the acute knee — pain, guarding, and hemarthrosis prevent 90° flexion and hamstring relaxation. Lachman is the acute-setting test.

Pivot Shift Test

ACL tear (rotatory instability)
TechniqueExtended knee, apply internal rotation + valgus while slowly flexing. The subluxed lateral tibial plateau reduces with a clunk at ~20–40°.
PositiveA palpable clunk/jerk as the tibia reduces.
PerformanceSokal 2022 pooled: sensitivity 55%, specificity 94%. Older data: specificity ~98%, sensitivity ~24%.
PearlThe most SPECIFIC ACL test — a true pivot shift is nearly diagnostic. But it is nearly impossible in an awake, guarding, acutely injured patient; its sensitivity climbs toward 100% under anesthesia.

McMurray Test

Meniscal tear
TechniqueSupine, fully flex the knee. For the MEDIAL meniscus: externally rotate the tibia and extend with a valgus force. For the LATERAL: internally rotate and extend with a varus force.
PositiveA palpable/audible CLICK or CLUNK with pain at the joint line.
PerformanceSensitivity only ~35–58%, specificity ~77–85% (worse for lateral). Better at ruling IN than ruling out.
PearlA click alone without pain is not a positive. Accuracy improves substantially when combined with joint line tenderness and the Thessaly test — use the cluster, not the single test.

Thessaly Test

Meniscal tear
TechniquePatient stands on the affected leg with the knee flexed 20° (holding your hands for balance) and rotates the body internally and externally three times.
PositiveJoint line discomfort, or a sense of locking/catching.
PerformancePerforms comparably to or better than McMurray in several series; a useful weight-bearing complement.
PearlWeight-bearing loads the meniscus the way real life does. Do not perform if a fracture or significant instability is suspected.

Valgus / Varus Stress Test

MCL / LCL injury
TechniqueTest at BOTH 0° (full extension) and 30° of flexion. Apply valgus (for MCL) or varus (for LCL) force at the ankle while stabilizing the joint line.
PositiveLaxity or pain. Grade I = pain, no laxity; II = laxity with endpoint; III = laxity with NO endpoint.
PerformancePosition matters critically: laxity at 30° only = isolated collateral injury. Laxity at 0° (full extension) implies a CRUCIATE and/or posterior capsule injury as well — a much more serious knee.
PearlLaxity in FULL EXTENSION is the red flag. That knee is not an isolated MCL sprain — it implies multiligament injury, and multiligament knee injuries carry real risk of popliteal artery and peroneal nerve injury.

Posterior Drawer / Sag Sign

PCL tear
TechniqueKnee at 90°. Sag sign: observe from the side for posterior droop of the tibial tubercle. Posterior drawer: push the tibia posteriorly.
PositivePosterior sag or increased posterior translation.
PerformancePosterior drawer is the most accurate PCL test; the sag sign is highly specific when present.
PearlA posteriorly sagging tibia creates a FALSE-POSITIVE anterior drawer (you are just pulling it back to neutral). Always look for the sag FIRST — this is the classic trap.

Straight Leg Raise (SLR)

Lumbar radiculopathy / disc herniation
TechniqueSupine, knee extended, passively raise the leg. Note the angle at which radicular pain appears (normal is ~68–80° before hamstring tightness).
PositiveRADICULAR pain radiating BELOW THE KNEE between 30–70°. Back pain alone is negative.
PerformanceSensitive (~91%) but not specific (~26%) for disc herniation. The CROSSED SLR (raising the unaffected leg reproduces symptoms on the affected side) is the reverse: highly SPECIFIC (~88–90%), poorly sensitive.
PearlA positive crossed SLR is a strong rule-in for a large/central disc herniation. Pain only in the back or hamstring is NOT a positive SLR — this is the most common misdocumentation in the ED.

FABER (Patrick) Test

Hip vs. SI joint pathology
TechniqueSupine. Flexion, ABduction, External Rotation — the ankle rests on the contralateral knee (figure-4); apply gentle downward pressure on the flexed knee and opposite ASIS.
PositiveGROIN pain → hip joint pathology. POSTERIOR/buttock pain → SI joint pathology.
PerformanceSensitive for hip pathology, not specific. Where the pain is localizes the source.
PearlThe location of pain is the whole point of FABER. 'FABER positive' with no location documented is a useless note.

FADIR / Anterior Impingement Test

Femoroacetabular impingement, labral tear
TechniqueSupine. Flex the hip to 90°, then ADduct and Internally Rotate.
PositiveSharp anterior groin pain.
PerformanceSensitive for FAI/labral pathology but poorly specific — good screening, poor confirmation.
PearlA negative FADIR is genuinely useful — it makes intra-articular hip pathology considerably less likely.

Trendelenburg Test

Hip abductor (gluteus medius) insufficiency
TechniquePatient stands on one leg for 30 seconds. Watch the pelvis from BEHIND.
PositiveThe pelvis DROPS on the CONTRALATERAL (unsupported) side — indicating weakness of the abductors on the STANCE side.
PerformanceReasonable for detecting significant abductor dysfunction.
PearlThe confusing part: the sign appears on the opposite side from the pathology. Pelvis drops right = LEFT abductors are weak. Also positive in superior gluteal nerve injury and after hip arthroplasty.

Thompson (Simmonds) Test

Achilles tendon rupture
TechniquePatient PRONE with feet hanging off the table. Squeeze the calf.
PositiveABSENCE of passive plantarflexion = ruptured Achilles.
PerformanceHigh sensitivity and specificity — one of the best physical exam tests in all of orthopedics.
PearlPatients with a complete Achilles rupture can often still weakly plantarflex using the FHL/FDL/peroneals — so 'they can push the gas pedal' does NOT exclude rupture. A palpable gap plus a positive Thompson is the diagnosis. Missing this is a classic and litigated ED error.

Anterior Drawer / Talar Tilt (Ankle)

ATFL / CFL injury
TechniqueAnterior drawer: ankle in slight plantarflexion, stabilize the tibia, pull the heel anteriorly. Talar tilt: invert the hindfoot.
PositiveIncreased translation or tilt vs. the other side, with a soft endpoint.
PerformanceAccuracy improves markedly when performed 4–5 days AFTER injury, once swelling and guarding subside.
PearlThe acute ankle is too swollen and guarded to examine reliably — apply the Ottawa rules acutely and re-examine at 5 days. Do NOT chase ligament grading on day 0.
Clinical Pearls
  • For the acutely injured knee: get the Lachman (best acute ACL test), look for the SAG before the drawer, and stress at BOTH 0° and 30°. Laxity in full extension changes the whole disposition.
  • For the acutely injured ankle: Ottawa rules first, ligament testing at 5 days. Always palpate the proximal fibula (Maisonneuve) and the base of the 5th metatarsal.
  • A knee dislocation (or a multiligament-lax knee that may have spontaneously reduced) mandates urgent vascular assessment — ABI and often CT angiography — regardless of palpable pulses.

Palpation is where diagnosis actually happens — point tenderness over a specific structure localizes pathology better than most special tests. Work systematically from bony landmarks outward, and always palpate the joint ABOVE and BELOW the painful one.

Femoral triangle / groinTrue hip joint pain localizes to the GROIN, not the lateral hip. Also palpate the femoral pulse and check for hernia and lymphadenopathy.
Pearls
  • THE most important hip-pain discriminator is location: GROIN pain = hip joint (OA, labrum, fracture, septic). LATERAL pain = greater trochanteric pain syndrome. BUTTOCK pain = SI joint or lumbar spine. Documenting 'hip pain' without location is nearly useless.
  • An elderly patient with groin pain and inability to weight-bear after a fall has a hip fracture until proven otherwise — and a negative plain film does NOT exclude it. Occult fractures need MRI (or CT).

1

2

3

Pearls
  • Snuffbox tenderness with normal x-rays = thumb spica splint and repeat imaging/advanced imaging in 10–14 days. Missed scaphoid fractures go on to nonunion and wrist collapse — this is one of the most litigated misses in hand care.
  • Kanavel's four signs = flexor tenosynovitis = go to the OR, not to oral antibiotics. Pain on PASSIVE EXTENSION is the earliest and most reliable of the four.

These are the findings that change disposition from 'splint and follow up' to 'call the surgeon now.' Each one has a specific significance you should be able to articulate and document.

Joint Effusion

What fluid means
MechanismDistension of the joint capsule by synovial fluid, blood, or pus. Detect with the bulge/sweep test (small effusions) or the patellar ballottement/tap (large effusions) at the knee.
SignificanceThe TIMING tells you the etiology. Immediate (<2 hours) = HEMARTHROSIS — ~75–80% are ACL tears; also fracture (esp. tibial plateau), patellar dislocation, or PCL. Delayed (12–24 hours) = reactive/meniscal or synovitis. Atraumatic + hot + febrile = SEPTIC until proven otherwise. LIPOHEMARTHROSIS (fat-fluid level on x-ray/US) = INTRA-ARTICULAR FRACTURE, even if you cannot see the fracture line.
ActionTraumatic hemarthrosis → image and consider ortho. Atraumatic hot joint → ARTHROCENTESIS. A lipohemarthrosis on imaging mandates CT to find the occult fracture.
Documentation"Moderate effusion, positive ballottement. Onset within 1 hour of injury (hemarthrosis)."

Septic Arthritis / MRSA Concern

Cannot-miss
MechanismBacterial invasion of the joint space. Cartilage destruction begins within HOURS via bacterial and host proteolytic enzymes. S. aureus is the most common organism; MRSA is increasingly prevalent, especially in IVDU, healthcare exposure, indwelling lines, prior MRSA, and endemic-community patients.
SignificanceSynovial WBC >50,000/µL with >90% PMNs suggests septic arthritis (IDSA 2024). But this threshold is NOT absolute: prosthetic joints are septic at far lower counts (~>1,100–3,000), and crystal arthropathy can exceed 50,000. CRITICAL PITFALL: antibiotics given BEFORE aspiration substantially lower the synovial WBC — one series found ~51,000 vs ~92,000 (a >40% reduction) and cut culture positivity from ~59% to ~33%. A 'reassuring' count in a pre-treated patient can be falsely reassuring. Crystals do NOT exclude infection — they can coexist.
ActionARTHROCENTESIS BEFORE ANTIBIOTICS whenever feasible (send cell count, Gram stain, culture, crystals). Then empiric coverage — include MRSA coverage (vancomycin) given prevalence. Septic arthritis is a surgical emergency: washout, not just antibiotics. Do NOT aspirate through overlying cellulitis.
Documentation"Hot, swollen knee with pain through a 5° arc, unable to bear weight, T 38.7. Arthrocentesis performed prior to antibiotics; fluid cloudy. Ortho notified."

Open Fracture

Surgical emergency
MechanismAny wound communicating with a fracture — including tiny 'poke holes' from inside-out spikes. If there is a wound anywhere near a fracture, assume it is open until proven otherwise.
SignificanceGustilo-Anderson: I = wound <1 cm, clean, minimal soft tissue damage. II = 1–10 cm, moderate damage. IIIA = >10 cm/extensive soft tissue but adequate coverage. IIIB = extensive soft tissue loss requiring FLAP coverage (periosteal stripping). IIIC = ANY vascular injury requiring repair (regardless of wound size). Grade drives infection risk and the need for orthoplastic involvement — IIIB/C tibias have markedly higher deep infection, nonunion, and amputation rates.
ActionIV ANTIBIOTICS EARLY — within 1 hour of injury is the guideline target and is one of the strongest modifiable predictors of infection (more important than the historic '6-hour rule' for debridement). Tetanus status. Gross contamination removed, sterile saline-moistened dressing, splint, do NOT repeatedly uncover the wound. Urgent operative debridement. Photograph once so the wound isn't re-exposed.
Documentation"3 cm transverse laceration over the anteromedial tibia with visible bone and bleeding from the wound. Gustilo II. Cefazolin given at 14:22 (34 min from injury). Td updated. Ortho at bedside."

Compartment Syndrome

Time-critical
MechanismInterstitial pressure within a fascial compartment exceeds perfusion pressure → ischemia → muscle and nerve necrosis. The leg (esp. anterior compartment after tibial fracture) is the classic site.
SignificanceThe 5 P's are a TRAP — pallor, pulselessness, and paralysis are all LATE and mean the limb is already dying. Compartment pressure never has to exceed systolic to cause necrosis. The EARLY findings are: (1) PAIN OUT OF PROPORTION to the injury and escalating opioid requirement, and (2) PAIN ON PASSIVE STRETCH of the compartment's muscles. Paresthesia is the first neuro sign. Pulses are typically PRESENT.
ActionRemove circumferential casts/dressings down to skin. Limb at heart level (not elevated — that lowers perfusion pressure). Measure compartment pressures if uncertain or the patient is obtunded/unreliable: ΔP (diastolic − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg → FASCIOTOMY. Do not delay for imaging.
Documentation"Escalating pain despite 8 mg IV morphine; severe pain on passive toe extension. Anterior compartment firm. DP pulse 2+. Compartment pressure 48, diastolic 72, ΔP 24. Ortho notified for emergent fasciotomy."

Neurovascular Compromise

Always document
MechanismDirect injury, traction, or compression of named vessels/nerves by fracture fragments, dislocation, or swelling.
SignificanceHigh-risk pairings you must know: knee dislocation → POPLITEAL ARTERY (and peroneal nerve); supracondylar humerus fracture → brachial artery/AIN; shoulder dislocation → AXILLARY nerve; humeral shaft fracture → RADIAL nerve; hip dislocation → SCIATIC nerve; fibular neck fracture → COMMON PERONEAL nerve; distal radius fracture → MEDIAN nerve (acute carpal tunnel).
ActionDocument neurovascular status BEFORE and AFTER every reduction and splint — this is both clinical necessity and your medicolegal protection. For a knee dislocation, palpable pulses do NOT exclude arterial injury: obtain ABI (<0.9 → CTA) or CTA.
Documentation"Pre-reduction: axillary n. sensation intact over lateral deltoid, radial pulse 2+, cap refill <2s. Post-reduction: unchanged, sensation and pulse intact, repeat film shows concentric reduction."

Cauda Equina Syndrome

Cannot-miss (spine)
MechanismCompression of the lumbosacral nerve roots below the conus — most often a large central disc herniation.
SignificanceThe red flags: saddle anesthesia, urinary RETENTION (with overflow incontinence — retention is the more sensitive finding), bowel incontinence, bilateral leg symptoms, and decreased anal tone. Urinary retention is the most consistent finding; its absence makes CES considerably less likely.
ActionPost-void residual (>200 mL is concerning), assess perineal sensation and rectal tone, EMERGENT MRI, and immediate spine surgery consult. Time to decompression drives outcome.
Documentation"Denies saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction. PVR 40 mL. Perineal sensation intact, normal rectal tone. No bilateral leg symptoms." (Document the NEGATIVES explicitly — this is the classic missed-diagnosis suit.)

Documentation is where a good exam becomes a defensible exam. The recurring doctrine from your charting atlases applies directly here: objective findings + a named measurement + a comparison + a safety net beats any conclusory adjective.

The Core Rules of ROM Documentation

1. USE NUMBERS, NOT ADJECTIVES. 'Decreased ROM' is uninterpretable and indefensible. '0–95° flexion (contralateral 0–135°)' is a fact another clinician can act on and compare to next week.

2. STATE ACTIVE VS. PASSIVE. This is the single most useful distinction in the MSK exam. AROM << PROM implies a muscle/tendon/nerve problem (a cuff tear, an extensor mechanism rupture, a nerve palsy). AROM = PROM (both restricted) implies a mechanical block: capsular contracture, effusion, arthritis, or a bony block.

3. ALWAYS RECORD THE CONTRALATERAL SIDE. The patient's other limb is the only valid control for their normal.

4. USE THE STANDARD 3-NUMBER FORMAT for a joint with a contracture: extension/flexion. '−15° → 110°' means the knee lacks 15° of full extension and flexes to 110°.

5. NAME THE POSITION AND THE METHOD. 'Hip IR 20° measured seated, hip and knee at 90°' is reproducible. 'Hip IR reduced' is not.

Documenting the Patient With PRE-EXISTING Limited ROM

This is the scenario that trips people up, and it matters enormously: a patient with a prior stroke, chronic sciatica, known back pain, contractures, or an old injury has a baseline that is NOT the textbook norm. If you chart their exam against a textbook norm, you either manufacture a new abnormality or you paper over a real acute change.

THE PRINCIPLE: separate BASELINE from ACUTE CHANGE, explicitly, in the note. The clinically meaningful question is never 'is this ROM normal?' — it is 'is this DIFFERENT from their baseline, and if so, how and since when?'

ESTABLISH THE BASELINE FROM A SOURCE, AND NAME THE SOURCE. Patient/family report, prior notes, prior therapy records, the prior exam in the chart. Then say where it came from: 'Per patient and daughter at bedside, left arm has had no active movement since his 2019 CVA; passive ROM full and pain-free at baseline.'

FOR A STROKE PATIENT specifically: distinguish weakness/paralysis (an ACTIVE deficit — AROM lost, PROM preserved) from CONTRACTURE (a passive, structural deficit — PROM lost too). Also distinguish SPASTICITY (velocity-dependent resistance, catch on rapid stretch, quantified with the Modified Ashworth Scale) from a fixed contracture (resistance regardless of speed). These have completely different implications and treatments.

FOR SCIATICA / RADICULOPATHY: document the SLR angle at which radicular pain begins, where it radiates TO, and compare to prior. 'SLR positive at 45° on the right reproducing pain radiating to the lateral calf; previously positive at 70° per 3/14 note — worsened.' That is an actionable, defensible, comparative statement.

FOR CHRONIC BACK PAIN: anchor to function, not just degrees. 'Baseline able to walk 2 blocks and sit 30 min; today unable to walk to the bathroom.' Function is the clinically meaningful delta and it is far more reproducible than eyeballed lumbar degrees.

THE DEFENSIBLE PATTERN, applied to ROM: name the baseline → cite the source → state today's objective measurement → state the delta explicitly → close with the safety net. Never write 'ROM limited' and leave the reader unable to tell whether that is new.

Documenting Strength, Given the Lever Arm Problem

Because hand placement changes the grade, a bare number is only as good as the method behind it. State the position and, when you deviate from standard distal resistance, say so.

GOOD: 'Right knee extension 4/5 (resistance at the distal tibia just above the ankle, seated, knee at 90°); left 5/5.' Another clinician can reproduce that exactly.

ALSO GOOD when you must deviate: 'Knee extension 5/5 with resistance at the distal femur (ankle fracture precluded distal resistance) — note this position overestimates relative to standard distal testing.' You have been transparent about the inflation.

BAD: 'Strength 4/5 RLE.' Which muscle? Which position? Where was your hand? Compared to what?

For give-way or pain-limited effort, say that rather than assigning a false number: 'Hip flexion effort-limited by pain, give-way quality; unable to grade reliably — no atrophy, reflexes symmetric.' That is honest and more defensible than a number you do not believe.

Clinical Pearls
  • The three questions every MSK note should answer: Is it different from their baseline? Is it different from the other side? Is there a red flag?
  • Document the pertinent NEGATIVES for the cannot-miss diagnoses — cauda equina, compartment syndrome, septic joint, neurovascular injury. An explicitly documented negative is what separates a defensible chart from a vulnerable one.
  • Never write 'ROM limited' or 'weakness' without a number, a side-to-side comparison, and a baseline. Conclusory language is the same trap here as 'ruled out' is in a chest pain note.

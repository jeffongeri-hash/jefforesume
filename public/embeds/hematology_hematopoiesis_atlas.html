Hematopoiesis & Cellular Atlas

Bone marrow to bedside: production, lifespan, and destruction of every formed blood element, EPO physiology and its relationship to blood flow, and the hereditary RBC/hemolytic disorders that hinge on this pathway.

Bone Marrow & the Hematopoietic Hierarchy

Every circulating blood cell traces back to a single hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) pool residing in the marrow niche. Lineage commitment is a branching, transcription-factor-driven decision tree.

Clinical relevance: Marrow failure syndromes (aplastic anemia, MDS) and marrow-toxic chemotherapy hit this entire tree simultaneously — hence the classic triad of anemia + neutropenia + thrombocytopenia (pancytopenia) rather than isolated cytopenias. Isolated cytopenias point away from a global marrow problem and toward peripheral destruction or a single-lineage process.

Erythrocytes: Production, Lifespan & EPO

The 120-day RBC lifecycle, and the renal oxygen-sensing feedback loop that sets the pace of production.

The 120-day cycle

Erythropoiesis takes ~7 days from proerythroblast to reticulocyte release (nucleus is extruded just before release — mammalian RBCs are the only cells that shed their nucleus to maximize hemoglobin-carrying volume). Reticulocytes mature fully within 1–2 days in circulation. At steady state the marrow must replace ~1% of the total RBC mass daily just to keep up with senescent clearance.

EPO physiology & the relationship to blood flow

EPO is the master regulator linking oxygen delivery to RBC mass. Renal peritubular interstitial fibroblasts sense local pO₂ via prolyl hydroxylases acting on HIF-2α — under normoxia, HIF-2α is hydroxylated and degraded; under hypoxia, it escapes degradation and drives EPO gene transcription. EPO then binds EPO-R on marrow erythroid progenitors (BFU-E/CFU-E), activating JAK2/STAT5 signaling that blocks apoptosis and drives proliferation — it is primarily a survival signal for cells already committed to the erythroid line, not a lineage-switch signal.

Blood flow relationship: Hematocrit and blood viscosity relate roughly exponentially above Hct ~55–60% — this is why polycythemia vera and over-aggressive ESA dosing both raise thrombosis/stroke risk despite improving oxygen content. Conversely, anemia lowers viscosity and triggers a compensatory high-output state (↑ heart rate, ↑ stroke volume, ↑ 2,3-BPG shifting the oxygen dissociation curve right) to preserve tissue oxygen delivery — chronic severe anemia can precipitate high-output heart failure. This is the physiologic reason ESA trials (CHOIR, TREAT, CREATE) found normalizing hemoglobin in CKD patients (target >13 g/dL) increased cardiovascular events versus a more conservative target (~10–11.5 g/dL) — current KDIGO-anchored practice avoids full normalization.

Verify current ESA hemoglobin targets and labeling against the latest KDIGO anemia-in-CKD guideline before using as a hard threshold in practice.

Hemoglobin O₂ delivery in one line

O₂ delivery (DO₂) = cardiac output × arterial O₂ content, and arterial O₂ content ≈ (1.34 × Hgb × SaO₂) + dissolved O₂ (negligible clinically). This is why Hgb, not just SpO₂, determines whether tissue oxygen delivery is adequate — a patient can be 100% saturated and still be inadequately oxygen-delivering if profoundly anemic.

Leukocytes: The Five Lines

Granulocytes (neutrophil, eosinophil, basophil), monocytes, and lymphocytes — each with distinct kinetics, tissue behavior, and clinical tells.

Neutrophil

40–75% of WBC

Role First responders — phagocytosis, NETs (neutrophil extracellular traps), respiratory burst (NADPH oxidase) for bacterial/fungal killing.

Kinetics Marrow maturation ~10–14 days (myeloblast → promyelocyte → myelocyte → metamyelocyte → band → segmented). Marrow reserve pool is ~10× the circulating pool — can be mobilized within hours during acute infection/stress (demargination), producing a rapid left shift without new production.

Pearl Bandemia (>10% bands) suggests marrow release under stress even before absolute count rises. Corticosteroids cause neutrophilia via demargination + reduced tissue migration — not true infection.

Lymphocyte

20–45% of WBC

Role Adaptive immunity — B cells (antibody production), T cells (CD4 helper, CD8 cytotoxic), NK cells (innate cytotoxicity, MHC-I “missing self” recognition).

Kinetics T-cell maturation requires thymic education (positive/negative selection) — thymic involution with age reduces naive T-cell output, part of immunosenescence.

Pearl Viral infections classically cause relative/absolute lymphocytosis (e.g., EBV, pertussis in children can cause striking lymphocytosis).

Monocyte

2–10% of WBC

Role Phagocytosis, antigen presentation, differentiate into tissue macrophages and dendritic cells; key in chronic inflammation and granuloma formation (TB, sarcoidosis).

Kinetics Circulate briefly (1–3 days) before extravasating into tissue and differentiating long-term.

Pearl Persistent monocytosis raises concern for CMML (chronic myelomonocytic leukemia) in older adults — warrants peripheral smear review.

Eosinophil

0–6% of WBC

Role Defense against helminths, mediates allergic/atopic inflammation, contains major basic protein and eosinophil cationic protein (tissue-damaging).

Kinetics IL-5 is the master regulator of eosinophil production and survival — target of mepolizumab/benralizumab in eosinophilic asthma.

Pearl Marked eosinophilia (>1,500/µL) triggers workup for parasitic infection, drug reaction (DRESS), hypereosinophilic syndrome, or malignancy (“NAACP” mnemonic: Neoplasm, Allergy/Asthma, Addison's, Collagen-vascular disease, Parasites).

Basophil

0–1% of WBC

Role IgE-mediated immediate hypersensitivity, releases histamine/heparin/leukotrienes; tissue counterpart is the mast cell (different lineage origin nuance debated).

Kinetics Least understood kinetically due to low numbers.

Pearl Basophilia is a classic feature of CML (chronic myeloid leukemia) and should prompt BCR-ABL testing when otherwise unexplained.

Platelets: Production, Lifespan & the Spleen

Anucleate cytoplasmic fragments with an outsized role in primary hemostasis — full clotting-cascade interaction lives in the Coagulation & Anticoagulation atlas.

Megakaryopoiesis

Megakaryocytes undergo endomitosis (repeated DNA replication without cell division, reaching ploidy up to 64N) to build a massive cytoplasm, then extend long proplatelet processes into marrow sinusoids where shear forces fragment them into 2,000–5,000 platelets per megakaryocyte. The whole process from megakaryoblast to platelet release takes ~5 days and is driven primarily by thrombopoietin (TPO), made constitutively by the liver — platelet mass itself provides negative feedback by binding and clearing circulating TPO via the c-Mpl receptor (more platelets = less free TPO = less production signal).

Lifespan & splenic pooling

Platelets circulate 7–10 days before being cleared, primarily via desialylation-triggered hepatic clearance (Ashwell-Morell receptor) and splenic macrophage phagocytosis. At steady state, roughly one-third of total body platelet mass sits in the splenic pool at any moment — this is why splenomegaly (portal hypertension, myeloproliferative disease, storage disease) causes a dilutional thrombocytopenia even with normal marrow production, and why splenectomy transiently raises platelet counts (sometimes markedly, risking post-splenectomy thrombocytosis and VTE).

Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag, avatrombopag) exploit this axis directly for ITP and chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia by mimicking TPO signaling on marrow megakaryocytes.

Hereditary RBC & Hemolytic Disorders

Structural membrane defects, enzymopathies, and hemoglobinopathies — organized by defect, mechanism, labs, complications, and management.

Sickle Cell Disease (HbSS)

Genetic defect

Point mutation GAG→GTG at codon 6 of β-globin → Glu→Val substitution → HbS. Autosomal recessive; HbSS = disease, HbAS = trait (protective against P. falciparum malaria).

Mechanism

Deoxygenated HbS polymerizes into rigid fibers → RBC sickling → vaso-occlusion, hemolysis (both intra- and extravascular), and chronic endothelial activation/inflammation.

Labs / smear

Hgb 6–9 g/dL baseline, reticulocytosis 10–25%, unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin, Howell-Jolly bodies (functional asplenia), sickle cells + target cells on smear.

Complications

Vaso-occlusive pain crisis, acute chest syndrome (leading cause of death), splenic sequestration (pediatric), aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19), avascular necrosis, priapism, stroke (~11% by age 20 untreated), pulmonary hypertension, nephropathy.

Management

Hydroxyurea (↑HbF, first-line disease-modifying), L-glutamine, crizanlizumab (P-selectin inhibitor, reduces VOC frequency), voxelotor (Hb-oxygen affinity modulator, less used now after efficacy re-evaluation — verify current labeling), chronic transfusion for stroke prevention (abnormal TCD), hematopoietic stem cell transplant (curative), gene therapy (lovo-cel, exa-cel — FDA approved 2023, verify access/current status).

Stats / pearls

US incidence ~1 in 365 Black births; median survival historically 40s–50s but has improved substantially with hydroxyurea/comprehensive care — confirm current life-expectancy data before quoting to patients.

α-Thalassemia

Genetic defect

Deletion (usually) of 1–4 of the 4 α-globin gene copies (chr 16). Severity scales directly with number of genes deleted.

Mechanism

↓α-globin → relative excess β-globin (or γ in fetal life) → unstable tetramers (HbH = β₄, Hb Barts = γ₄) → ineffective erythropoiesis + hemolysis.

Labs / smear

1 gene del: silent carrier, normal CBC. 2 genes: thalassemia trait — microcytosis (MCV often 60s–70s) with normal/near-normal Hgb, normal RDW, normal Hb electrophoresis (key differentiator from iron deficiency, which has ↑RDW). 3 genes: HbH disease — moderate hemolytic anemia. 4 genes: Hb Barts hydrops fetalis — incompatible with life without in-utero transfusion.

Complications

HbH disease: splenomegaly, gallstones, folate demand, iron overload even without transfusion (ineffective erythropoiesis suppresses hepcidin → ↑ gut iron absorption).

Management

Trait: no treatment, genetic counseling. HbH: folate supplementation, avoid oxidant drugs, monitor for transfusion need, iron studies before ever giving iron (do NOT empirically treat microcytosis with iron without confirming deficiency).

Stats / pearls

Mentzer index (MCV/RBC count) <13 suggests thalassemia over iron deficiency (>13); definitive diagnosis by Hb electrophoresis/genetic testing.

β-Thalassemia

Genetic defect

Point mutations (>200 described) reducing (β⁺) or abolishing (β⁰) β-globin synthesis (chr 11).

Mechanism

Unpaired α-globin chains precipitate in erythroid precursors → marked ineffective erythropoiesis → marrow expansion (“chipmunk facies,” frontal bossing) + extramedullary hematopoiesis + severe hemolysis.

Labs / smear

Minor (trait, β⁺/β or β⁰/β): mild microcytic anemia, ↑HbA2 >3.5% (diagnostic), normal-ish RDW. Intermedia: moderate anemia, transfusion sometimes needed. Major (Cooley anemia, β⁰/β⁰): severe transfusion-dependent anemia presenting at 6–24 months as HbF declines.

Complications

Iron overload from both ineffective erythropoiesis and chronic transfusion → cardiomyopathy (leading cause of death if unchelated), cirrhosis, endocrinopathies (diabetes, hypothyroidism, hypogonadism, growth failure), extramedullary hematopoietic masses.

Management

Major: lifelong transfusion (goal pre-transfusion Hgb ~9–10.5 g/dL to suppress marrow drive) + iron chelation (deferasirox, deferoxamine, deferiprone) once ferritin threshold reached; monitor cardiac/hepatic iron via T2* MRI; luspatercept (activin receptor ligand trap, reduces transfusion burden); allogeneic HSCT and gene therapy (beti-cel) curative options.

Stats / pearls

Ferritin alone is an imperfect surrogate for organ iron — cardiac T2* MRI <20 ms signals high cardiac iron risk regardless of ferritin trend.

Hereditary Spherocytosis

Genetic defect

Autosomal dominant (75%) defect in RBC membrane skeleton proteins — ankyrin, band 3, spectrin, or protein 4.2 — causing vertical membrane instability.

Mechanism

Loss of membrane surface area (not cytoplasm) → spherocytic shape → ↓ deformability → splenic sequestration and destruction (extravascular hemolysis).

Labs / smear

↑MCHC (>36 g/dL, relatively specific), spherocytes on smear, ↑ osmotic fragility, ↑% reticulocytes, negative direct Coombs (distinguishes from autoimmune hemolytic anemia which also shows spherocytes), EMA-binding flow cytometry is current gold-standard confirmatory test.

Complications

Chronic hemolysis, pigment gallstones (even in children), aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19), splenomegaly.

Management

Folate supplementation. Splenectomy for moderate-severe disease (dramatically reduces hemolysis since spleen is the destruction site) — delay until age 5–6 if possible for immune maturation; partial splenectomy increasingly favored to preserve some immune function; ensure pneumococcal/meningococcal/Hib vaccination and penicillin prophylaxis given post-splenectomy sepsis risk.

Stats / pearls

Most common hereditary hemolytic anemia in people of Northern European descent (~1:2,000).

G6PD Deficiency

Genetic defect

X-linked recessive enzyme deficiency — predominantly affects males; G6PD protects RBCs from oxidative stress via the pentose phosphate pathway/NADPH generation.

Mechanism

Oxidative stressors (infection, certain drugs, fava beans) deplete glutathione → hemoglobin oxidizes and precipitates as Heinz bodies → splenic “bite cell” formation and both intra- and extravascular hemolysis.

Labs / smear

Acute episodic hemolysis (not chronic) — ↓Hgb, ↑retics, ↑LDH, ↓haptoglobin, hemoglobinuria if intravascular, Heinz bodies (supravital stain), bite/blister cells. G6PD enzyme activity assay is falsely normal during acute hemolysis (young RBCs have more enzyme) — recheck 2–3 months later.

Complications

Neonatal jaundice/kernicterus risk, acute hemolytic crisis with oxidant triggers, favism (severe hemolysis from fava bean ingestion, Mediterranean/A- variants).

Management

Avoid triggers: sulfonamides, dapsone, primaquine/tafenoquine, nitrofurantoin, rasburicase, fava beans, and during acute infection/DKA. Supportive care during hemolytic episodes (hydration, transfusion if severe); this is a key medication-safety atlas cross-check before prescribing any oxidant drug.

Stats / pearls

Most common human enzyme deficiency worldwide (~400 million people); confers partial malaria protection, explaining geographic distribution (Africa, Mediterranean, Middle East, Southeast Asia).

Hereditary Elliptocytosis

Genetic defect

Autosomal dominant defects in spectrin self-association (α- or β-spectrin) or protein 4.1R — horizontal membrane skeleton connections.

Mechanism

Weakened horizontal membrane linkages → RBCs deform into ellipses under shear stress and cannot recover normal shape → variable hemolysis (most patients are asymptomatic carriers).

Labs / smear

Elliptocytes (cigar/oval cells) >25–50% on smear; most patients have normal Hgb and are diagnosed incidentally. Hereditary pyropoikilocytosis is a severe neonatal variant (thermal instability of spectrin).

Complications

Usually mild/asymptomatic; occasional chronic hemolytic anemia, gallstones; homozygous/compound heterozygous forms can be severe.

Management

Most require no treatment; folate supplementation and splenectomy reserved for symptomatic hemolytic variants.

Stats / pearls

~1:2,000–1:4,000; higher carrier frequency in malaria-endemic regions (West Africa, Southeast Asia) suggesting selective advantage.

Quick differentiator on smear: Spherocytes + negative Coombs → hereditary spherocytosis. Spherocytes + positive Coombs → autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Sickle cells + target cells → sickle cell disease. Target cells alone, normal Hb electrophoresis, low-normal MCV, normal RDW → think thalassemia trait over iron deficiency (which has high RDW). Bite/blister cells + acute trigger → G6PD deficiency.

01 · Pathophysiology

Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity

Two integrated systems separated by speed and specificity — the innate system buys time in minutes to hours, the adaptive system arrives in days but remembers.

“Why does this patient mount fever and neutrophilia within hours, but their antibody titer doesn’t rise for a week?”

Innate immunity is the immediate, germline-encoded response: physical barriers, phagocytes, and the complement cascade recognize broad molecular patterns (PAMPs via pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors) rather than specific antigens. It is fast but non-specific and has no memory. Adaptive immunity is slower to activate on first exposure — clonal selection and expansion of antigen-specific T and B lymphocytes takes roughly 5–10 days — but it is exquisitely antigen-specific and generates immunologic memory, which is why re-exposure produces a faster, larger, higher-affinity response.

Figure 1. Innate and adaptive arms converge through antigen presentation.
Clinical correlation: a patient with recurrent, severe bacterial infections from early childhood suggests an innate or humoral defect (e.g., chronic granulomatous disease, complement deficiency, agammaglobulinemia). Recurrent viral/fungal/opportunistic infections point toward a T-cell or combined defect (e.g., SCID, DiGeorge, HIV).

How NK Cells Recognize a Target Without Prior Sensitization

Figure 1b. “Missing self” recognition — NK cells are held in check by inhibitory receptors reading normal MHC I, and fire when that signal disappears.
The elegant logic behind this: viruses and tumors both have strong incentives to downregulate MHC I to escape CD8+ T cell recognition (Volume I's antigen presentation section) — NK cells close that loophole by doing the opposite of a T cell: they kill when MHC I is absent rather than when a specific antigen is present, which is why NK cells need no prior exposure or priming to respond.

01 · Pathophysiology

T Lymphocytes

Thymically-educated, antigen-specific cells that either kill directly or orchestrate the response of others.

T cells undergo positive and negative selection in the thymus: positive selection retains only cells whose T-cell receptor (TCR) can weakly bind self-MHC (ensuring MHC restriction), and negative selection deletes cells that bind self-antigen with high affinity (central tolerance). Cells surviving both checkpoints emigrate as naive CD4+ or CD8+ T cells.

Figure 2b. Thymic selection — roughly 95% of thymocytes die by neglect or deletion; only self-restricted, non-self-reactive cells graduate.

Recognizes antigen presented on MHC II by professional APCs. Differentiates into functional subsets (Th1/Th2/Th17/Treg/Tfh) that orchestrate, rather than directly execute, the immune response.

Core marker: CD4, TCRαβ
MHC I restricted
CD8+ Cytotoxic (killer) T cell

Recognizes antigen presented on MHC I — present on essentially all nucleated cells. Directly kills infected or malignant cells via perforin/granzyme-mediated apoptosis and Fas-FasL signaling.

Core marker: CD8, TCRαβ · Effector of viral clearance, tumor immunosurveillance
Long-lived, antigen-experienced
Memory T cell

Persists after antigen clearance; re-encounter triggers a faster, larger secondary response without needing full priming. Central memory (lymph node homing) vs. effector memory (tissue surveillance) subsets.

Peripheral tolerance
Regulatory T cell (Treg)

Suppresses autoreactive lymphocytes via IL-10, TGF-β, and CTLA-4. Loss of function (e.g., FOXP3 mutation in IPEX syndrome) causes severe multi-organ autoimmunity.

How Cytotoxic Killing Actually Happens

Figure 2c. Two independent pathways — perforin/granzyme and Fas-FasL — both converge on target-cell apoptosis, a controlled death that avoids the collateral tissue damage of necrosis.
Why apoptosis, not necrosis, matters here: both CD8+ T cells and NK cells kill by triggering the target cell's own apoptotic machinery rather than lysing it outright — a controlled, non-inflammatory death that clears infected or malignant cells without releasing the damage-associated molecular patterns that would trigger a wider inflammatory cascade.

The Germinal Center Reaction

Figure 3b. Inside the germinal center, B cells simultaneously mutate their antigen receptor and switch antibody class — only the highest-affinity clones survive selection.
This is why a second exposure produces a better antibody than the first: somatic hypermutation randomly diversifies the antigen-binding region with each round of proliferation, and only B cells whose mutated receptor binds antigen better receive survival signals from follicular dendritic cells and Tfh cells — an iterative, Darwinian selection process happening over days inside a single lymph node.

Antigens & Antigen Presentation

The same molecule is processed through two distinct pathways depending on where it comes from — and this determines which T cell responds.

An antigen is any molecule capable of being recognized by the adaptive immune system; an epitope is the specific portion of that antigen actually bound by an antibody or TCR. A hapten is a small molecule too small to be immunogenic on its own but that becomes antigenic when covalently bound to a carrier protein — the mechanism behind most drug allergies (e.g., penicillin metabolites haptenating host proteins).

01 · Pathophysiology

Cytokine Signaling

The chemical language immune cells use to coordinate — and the shared pathway that makes JAK inhibitors work across so many diseases.

Most cytokine receptors (IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-2, and many others) lack intrinsic kinase activity and instead signal through associated Janus kinases (JAK1/2/3, TYK2). Receptor dimerization on cytokine binding brings JAKs into proximity, they trans-phosphorylate each other and the receptor tail, and this recruits and phosphorylates STAT proteins, which dimerize and translocate to the nucleus to drive transcription. This shared final pathway is why a single class of drug — JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib, ruxolitinib) — has effect across rheumatoid arthritis, myelofibrosis, and severe COVID-19 cytokine release.

02 · Cytokine Storms

Pathophysiology of Cytokine Storm

A self-amplifying loop, not a single event — recognizing where in the loop you are determines what you treat.

“Three liters of fluid in and the blood pressure is still soft — is this just under-resuscitated distributive shock, or is the immune system itself now the problem?”

A cytokine storm is a state of immune dysregulation in which pro-inflammatory cytokine production becomes self-sustaining and outpaces its normal counter-regulation. The common final pathway — regardless of trigger — is a surge of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ driving capillary leak, vasodilatory shock, coagulopathy (consumptive and pro-thrombotic simultaneously), and multi-organ injury. What differs between the syndromes below is the trigger and therefore the specific intervention that interrupts the loop — source control and antitoxin therapy in toxin-mediated shock, IL-6 blockade in CRS, etoposide-based cytotoxic therapy in HLH, and drug withdrawal plus steroids in DRESS.

Superantigen-Mediated Toxic Shock

Group A Streptococcus and Staphylococcus aureus toxins bypass normal antigen specificity entirely.

A conventional antigen activates roughly 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 T cells — only those whose TCR happens to match the processed peptide in the MHC groove. A superantigen (streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins, staphylococcal TSST-1 and enterotoxins) bypasses this entirely: it binds directly to the outside of the MHC II molecule on one end and the TCR Vβ chain on the other, outside the normal peptide-binding groove, cross-linking them non-specifically. The result is polyclonal activation of up to 20–30% of the entire T-cell repertoire at once — a cytokine surge orders of magnitude larger than any conventional antigen could produce.

Figure 8. Superantigens cross-link MHC II and TCR outside the antigen-binding groove, activating T cells non-specifically at massive scale.

Why clindamycin is added — not substituted — in invasive Group A Strep

In necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome (STSS), a beta-lactam alone is often insufficient for two reasons. First, the Eagle effect: beta-lactams act on actively dividing bacteria via cell-wall synthesis inhibition, but in the very high bacterial density of necrotizing infection, a large fraction of organisms are in stationary phase and express fewer penicillin-binding proteins — efficacy paradoxically drops as bacterial burden rises. Second, and more relevant to the cytokine storm itself: clindamycin suppresses toxin and virulence factor production at the ribosomal level (50S inhibition of protein synthesis) independent of bacterial killing. It directly reduces M-protein and exotoxin (superantigen) synthesis, which is the actual driver of the cytokine cascade — a beta-lactam kills the bacteria but does nothing to stop toxin already being produced, or being released as bacteria lyse.

Case pattern

A previously healthy 34-year-old with a minor forearm laceration returns at 36 hours with pain disproportionate to exam findings, bullae, and a temperature of 39.4°C. Within hours he develops hypotension refractory to 3L crystalloid and a lactate of 6. This trajectory — rapid progression, pain out of proportion, and early refractory shock — should trigger surgical consultation for exploration/debridement simultaneously with antibiotics, not after imaging. Empiric coverage in this setting is a beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) plus clindamycin for toxin suppression, pending culture data; IVIG is considered in refractory STSS for its capacity to neutralize circulating superantigen.

Myth: “If I'm covering with a broad-spectrum beta-lactam, I don't need anything else.” Broad-spectrum coverage treats the bacterial burden but does not address toxin already produced or being produced during bacterial lysis — clindamycin's protein-synthesis-inhibiting, toxin-suppressing effect is a distinct and complementary mechanism, not redundant coverage.

Sepsis-Associated Cytokine Dysregulation

The prototype — covered in full depth in the dedicated Sepsis Atlas (SSC 2026).

Sepsis represents the most common cause of cytokine-storm physiology encountered in the ED, arising from pattern-recognition-receptor activation (TLR4 by LPS in gram-negatives, TLR2 by lipoteichoic acid in gram-positives) triggering the same IL-1/IL-6/TNF-α cascade described above, layered on top of direct pathogen-mediated tissue injury and, frequently, a superimposed coagulopathy (sepsis-induced DIC). Hemodynamic management, source control timing, and antimicrobial selection are covered in depth in the six-volume Sepsis Atlas — this entry exists here only to place sepsis correctly within the broader cytokine storm framework rather than duplicate that content.

Cross-reference: Sepsis Atlas Vol. I–VI (2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign) and the companion Antibiotic Indications Atlas for full source-control and antimicrobial dosing detail.

CAR-T & Immunotherapy-Associated CRS

An iatrogenic, on-target cytokine storm — the immune system doing exactly what it was engineered to do.

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy and certain bispecific antibodies (e.g., blinatumomab) engineer massive, targeted T-cell activation against tumor antigen — and the resulting IL-6-dominant cytokine release syndrome (CRS) is a direct, expected consequence of that mechanism rather than an off-target complication. Onset is typically within the first 1–2 weeks after infusion and correlates with tumor burden and peak CAR-T expansion.

Corticosteroids are reserved for tocilizumab-refractory CRS or when concurrent immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) is present, since steroids cross the blood-brain barrier more reliably than tocilizumab and IL-6 blockade alone can occasionally worsen neurotoxicity by shunting IL-6 across an already-permeable BBB.

Verify before use: ASTCT grading thresholds and tocilizumab dosing are frequently updated by product-specific REMS programs — confirm against the specific CAR-T product's current label before applying at the bedside.

HLH & Macrophage Activation Syndrome

The storm that hides in plain sight — mimics sepsis, but the treatment is cytotoxic immunosuppression, not just antibiotics.

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) and its rheumatologic variant, macrophage activation syndrome (MAS, typically seen with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis/adult-onset Still's disease), result from failure of NK cells and cytotoxic T cells to terminate an immune response — either from an inherited perforin-pathway defect (primary HLH, usually pediatric) or triggered secondarily by infection (especially EBV), malignancy, or autoimmune disease. Uncontrolled macrophage and T-cell activation drives extreme hyperferritinemia, cytopenias from marrow hemophagocytosis, and often disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Verify before use: HLH-2004 remains the most widely taught criteria set, but HScore and evolving MAS-specific criteria are also used clinically and weight variables differently — confirm which tool your institution's hematology/rheumatology service anchors to.

DRESS Syndrome

A delayed, T-cell-driven drug reaction that behaves like a slow-motion cytokine storm — not a typical Type I drug allergy.

Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS) is fundamentally different from an IgE-mediated drug allergy in its timeline: onset is typically 2–8 weeks after starting the culprit drug (versus minutes to hours for Type I reactions), reflecting a delayed T-cell-driven mechanism with HHV-6 reactivation frequently implicated in propagating the syndrome once triggered. Classic culprits are the aromatic anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, lamotrigine — with cross-reactivity among them), allopurinol, sulfonamides, and vancomycin.

Case pattern

A patient started on vancomycin for a skin/soft tissue infection three weeks ago now returns with fever, a diffuse morbilliform rash with facial edema, and labs showing a leukocytosis with striking eosinophilia and atypical lymphocytes, alongside a new transaminitis. The temporal gap from vancomycin initiation to symptom onset — not the day the drug was started — is the clue that separates this from a typical drug rash, and liver involvement is the most common and most dangerous organ manifestation to monitor.

02 · Cytokine Storms

COVID-19-Associated Cytokine Storm

The syndrome that brought IL-6 biology and dexamethasone timing into mainstream emergency medicine.

Severe COVID-19 pneumonia produces an IL-6-dominant hyperinflammatory phase, typically in the second week of illness, correlating with clinical deterioration and ARDS. Two therapeutic strategies became standard from large randomized trials: dexamethasone, and IL-6 pathway blockade (tocilizumab) or JAK inhibition (baricitinib) in appropriately selected, more severely hypoxemic patients.

Safety-relevant number: the RECOVERY trial found dexamethasone reduced 28-day mortality in patients requiring supplemental oxygen, with the largest absolute benefit in those requiring invasive mechanical ventilation — but showed no benefit, and a signal toward harm, in patients not requiring any supplemental oxygen. Timing and hypoxemia severity, not a blanket “treat all COVID” approach, determined benefit.
Verify before use: specific trial-derived mortality deltas, dexamethasone dosing windows, and tocilizumab/baricitinib eligibility criteria have been revised repeatedly since 2020 — confirm against current institutional/IDSA guidance before applying to a specific patient.
03 · Hypersensitivity

The Gell & Coombs Classification

Four distinct immunologic mechanisms of tissue injury — the mechanism, not just the timing, determines the correct treatment.

“Is this rash immediate IgE-mediated anaphylaxis, or a delayed T-cell process that will look identical on skin exam but kill through a completely different pathway?”

All four types represent an otherwise normal immune mechanism (antibody binding, complement activation, immune complex formation, or T-cell cytotoxicity) directed at a target that causes harm rather than benefit — either because the target is self (autoimmunity), the target is harmless (allergy), or the magnitude of the otherwise-appropriate response is itself the problem. Onset timing is a genuinely useful bedside clue: Type I evolves over minutes, Type II and III over days to two weeks, and Type IV over 1–3 days on re-exposure (or up to 2 weeks on first sensitization).

03 · Hypersensitivity

Type I — Immediate (IgE-Mediated)

Sensitization is silent; the reaction is anything but.

Figure 9. First exposure sensitizes via IgE class switch; re-exposure cross-links surface IgE and triggers immediate mast cell degranulation.

The first exposure to an allergen is clinically silent — it drives Th2-directed IgE class switching, and that IgE then coats mast cells and basophils via FcεRI without producing symptoms. Only on re-exposure does allergen cross-link adjacent surface IgE molecules, triggering degranulation within minutes: histamine, tryptase, and rapidly synthesized leukotrienes and prostaglandin D2 drive vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchospasm, and mucosal edema. A late phase 4–8 hours later, driven by newly recruited eosinophils and Th2 cells, explains biphasic reactions.

Case pattern

Twenty minutes after eating a meal containing tree nuts, a patient develops lip swelling, diffuse hives, throat tightness, and a drop in systolic blood pressure to 82 mmHg. This combination — skin/mucosal findings plus either respiratory compromise or hypotension, following exposure to a likely allergen — meets clinical criteria for anaphylaxis and should prompt immediate intramuscular epinephrine, not antihistamines as a first step.

Why epinephrine, and why IM, and why first

Epinephrine is the only agent that addresses all three life-threatening mechanisms of anaphylaxis simultaneously: α1 agonism reverses vasodilation and mucosal edema, β1 agonism supports blood pressure and cardiac output, and β2 agonism produces bronchodilation and — clinically underappreciated — helps stabilize mast cells and reduce further mediator release. Antihistamines and steroids treat cutaneous symptoms and may blunt a late-phase/biphasic reaction respectively, but neither reverses airway or hemodynamic compromise, which is why delaying epinephrine to trial an antihistamine first is a recognized and preventable cause of fatal anaphylaxis. Intramuscular injection into the anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis) achieves faster, more reliable peak plasma concentration than subcutaneous administration.

Safety-relevant numbers: biphasic anaphylactic reactions — recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution, without re-exposure — have been reported in a wide range (roughly 1–20% depending on cohort and definition), typically within 1–72 hours, which is why observation periods of at least 4–6 hours (longer for severe reactions, refractory hypotension, or reactions requiring multiple epinephrine doses) remain standard practice.
Verify before use: biphasic reaction incidence estimates and specific observation-window recommendations vary meaningfully across published cohorts and society guidelines — confirm against your institution's current anaphylaxis protocol.
03 · Hypersensitivity

Type II — Cytotoxic (Antibody-Mediated)

IgG or IgM binds a cell-surface target directly — the cell itself is destroyed.

Figure 10. Antibody bound to a cell-surface antigen triggers complement lysis, ADCC, or opsonized phagocytosis.

Unlike Type I, no mast cell is required — IgG or IgM binds directly to an antigen physically anchored on a cell surface (a true self-antigen, an absorbed drug hapten, or a mismatched transfused cell), and destruction proceeds through one or more of three mechanisms: complement-mediated lysis via the membrane attack complex, opsonization leading to splenic/hepatic phagocytosis, or antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by NK cells engaging the antibody's Fc region.

Case pattern

Within minutes of starting a red cell transfusion, a patient develops fever, flank pain, hemoglobinuria, and hypotension. An acute hemolytic transfusion reaction from ABO incompatibility — almost always the result of a clerical/identification error rather than a laboratory testing failure — causes complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis severe enough to precipitate DIC and acute kidney injury. The transfusion must be stopped immediately, the unit and patient identity re-verified, and supportive care (aggressive fluid resuscitation to protect renal perfusion) initiated without waiting for confirmatory serology.

Type III — Immune Complex-Mediated

Not the antigen and not the antibody alone — it's the complex, deposited in the wrong place, that does the damage.

Figure 11. Circulating immune complexes deposit in small vessels/glomeruli and trigger complement-driven vasculitis.

Type III injury requires a soluble antigen (unlike the cell-bound antigen of Type II) that forms circulating antigen-antibody complexes once the host generates sufficient antibody — which is why symptom onset lags exposure by roughly 1–2 weeks, the time needed to mount that antibody response. These complexes deposit preferentially in small vessels, joints, and glomeruli (areas of high filtration pressure/turbulent flow), where they activate complement and recruit neutrophils, producing local vasculitis rather than direct cell killing.

Case pattern

Ten days after a course of an antibiotic (classically cefaclor) or antivenom/antitoxin administration, a patient develops fever, urticarial-appearing rash, symmetric arthralgias, and diffuse lymphadenopathy. Serum sickness is self-limited once the inciting antigen clears, and management is supportive (NSAIDs for arthralgia, antihistamines for rash, corticosteroids reserved for more severe presentations) — the key teaching point is recognizing the delayed timeline so the antibiotic given 10 days ago, not any medication started today, is correctly identified as the trigger.

Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (PSGN) is a Type III mechanism: circulating immune complexes from a preceding streptococcal throat or skin infection deposit in the glomerular basement membrane roughly 1–3 weeks later, producing the classic triad of hematuria, edema, and hypertension — a useful contrast to acute post-strep rheumatic fever, which is a Type II/molecular-mimicry mechanism instead.

Type IV — Delayed-Type (Cell-Mediated)

The only type with no antibody involvement at all — T cells do the recognizing and the damage.

Figure 12. Sensitized memory T cells recognize antigen on re-exposure and drive Th1 macrophage activation or direct CD8 cytotoxicity.

Type IV reactions require prior sensitization — typically 10–14 days for a first exposure to prime antigen-specific memory T cells — but on re-exposure, the reaction unfolds over 24–72 hours rather than minutes, since it depends on T-cell trafficking and cytokine release rather than pre-formed mediators. Subtypes matter clinically: Th1-driven (IVa) reactions activate macrophages via IFN-γ (contact dermatitis, tuberculin skin test); CD8-driven (IVc) reactions cause direct cytotoxic killing of keratinocytes and are the mechanism behind Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis.

Case pattern

Two days after a weekend hike, a patient presents with intensely pruritic, linear vesicular streaks on the exposed forearms and legs. Allergic contact dermatitis from urushiol (poison ivy/oak) is a textbook Th1-mediated Type IV reaction — the linear distribution reflects where the plant physically brushed the skin, and the 1–2 day delay (rather than immediate reaction) is itself diagnostic of the mechanism. Management is topical or systemic corticosteroids and avoidance; unlike Type I reactions, antihistamines have limited mechanistic rationale here beyond symptomatic pruritus control.

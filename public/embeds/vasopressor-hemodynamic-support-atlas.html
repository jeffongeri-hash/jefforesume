Receptor Pathway Quick Reference

ReceptorPhysiologic effect
α1Vascular smooth muscle vasoconstriction — the primary blood-pressure-raising receptor for most vasopressors on this list.
β1Cardiac inotropy (contractility) and chronotropy (heart rate) — the primary receptor for inotropic support.
β2Vascular and bronchial smooth muscle relaxation (vasodilation, bronchodilation) — works against alpha-1's vasoconstrictive effect when both are present in the same agent.
DopaminergicRenal and splanchnic vasodilation at low concentrations — the physiologic basis (now largely discredited as a clinical strategy) for 'renal-dose dopamine.'
V1 (vasopressin)Vascular smooth muscle vasoconstriction via a non-adrenergic pathway — vasopressin's mechanism, useful in catecholamine-resistant states.
AT1 (angiotensin II)Vascular smooth muscle vasoconstriction via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system — angiotensin II's mechanism, a third distinct pathway from adrenergic and vasopressin receptors.
PDE3 (milrinone)Increases intracellular cAMP downstream of (independent from) the beta-adrenergic receptor — milrinone's mechanism, retains effect even with beta-receptor blockade/downregulation.
Guanylate cyclase (methylene blue)Blocks the nitric oxide/cGMP pathway that drives pathologic vasodilation — methylene blue's mechanism, targets excess-NO vasoplegia directly.

💊 Agent Monographs

Norepinephrine

Levophed · NE · noradrenaline
First-line vasopressor — catecholamine
Dose range
0.01–3 mcg/kg/min (typical working range 0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min; doses above ~1 mcg/kg/min considered very high-dose/refractory)
Onset
Seconds
Half-life
~2 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

Isolated in the 1940s as the physiologic neurotransmitter released by sympathetic postganglionic neurons; adopted clinically as a vasopressor decades before the evidence base existed to support it over dopamine. Its position as the default first-line vasopressor for essentially all shock states is a relatively recent (post-2010) evidence-driven shift.

Physiology

Potent alpha-1 agonist with modest beta-1 activity and negligible beta-2 effect. The alpha-1-dominant profile produces reliable vasoconstriction (raising SVR and MAP) while the beta-1 component provides a modest inotropic/chronotropic boost that helps offset the afterload increase — a more favorable balance than pure alpha-agonists for maintaining cardiac output while raising pressure.

Indications
  • First-line vasopressor for septic shock per Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines
  • First-line vasopressor for essentially all forms of shock (cardiogenic, hypovolemic refractory to fluid, obstructive) where dopamine was previously used
  • Preferred over dopamine following SOAP II trial data
Evidence
SOAP II (De Backer 2010, NEJM, n=1,679): no significant difference in 28-day mortality between norepinephrine and dopamine as first-line vasopressor (52.5% vs 48.5%, p=0.10), but dopamine caused significantly more arrhythmias (24.1% vs 12.4%, p<0.001) and more severe arrhythmias requiring drug discontinuation (6.1% vs 1.6%, p<0.001). A prespecified cardiogenic shock subgroup showed significantly higher 28-day mortality with dopamine (p=0.03) — though this finding was not supported by formal interaction testing and should be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. A 2024 reanalysis using win-ratio and Bayesian methods found all analyses favored norepinephrine, with a 0.95 probability of harm from dopamine on mortality.
Downfalls / Risks
  • Peripheral extravasation risk was the traditional justification for mandatory central access — now substantially revised (see Peripheral Administration tab)
  • High-dose, prolonged norepinephrine is associated with digital/limb ischemia in a dose- and duration-dependent fashion
  • Reflex bradycardia possible via baroreceptor response to the rise in MAP
⚡ Pearl
The default answer for 'what vasopressor should I start' in almost any undifferentiated shock state absent a specific reason to choose otherwise.

Epinephrine

Adrenaline
Second-line vasopressor / first-line in anaphylaxis & arrest — catecholamine
Dose range
Shock infusion: 0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min. Anaphylaxis IM: 0.3–0.5 mg (1:1,000) q5–15min. Cardiac arrest IV/IO: 1 mg (1:10,000) q3–5min.
Onset
Seconds
Half-life
~2 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

The first hormone to be isolated and synthesized (early 1900s), and one of the oldest drugs in continuous clinical use — its role has shifted repeatedly as evidence accumulated, from a general-purpose pressor to a more specifically indicated second-line/rescue agent in shock, while remaining unambiguously first-line for anaphylaxis and cardiac arrest.

Physiology

Balanced alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 agonism — the beta-2 component (bronchodilation, some peripheral vasodilation) distinguishes it from norepinephrine and underlies its unique value in anaphylaxis (bronchodilation plus vasoconstriction plus mast-cell stabilization/mediator-release inhibition, the latter via beta-2 effects on mast cells) that no other vasopressor replicates.

Indications
  • First-line, undisputed treatment for anaphylaxis (IM, anterolateral thigh)
  • First-line vasopressor in cardiac arrest (ACLS)
  • Second-line add-on (or alternative first-line) vasopressor in septic shock when norepinephrine alone is inadequate
  • First-line inopressor in cardiogenic shock in some protocols given combined inotropic/vasoconstrictive effect
Evidence
Multiple RCTs (including a landmark 2007 double-blind trial) found epinephrine equivalent to norepinephrine plus dobutamine for septic shock hemodynamic and mortality outcomes, but epinephrine was associated with more tachycardia, transient lactic acidosis (a beta-2-mediated effect on skeletal muscle glycolysis, not tissue hypoperfusion — an important distinction when epinephrine-associated lactate elevation is misread as worsening shock), and increased splanchnic/gut perfusion concerns in some studies, which is why many protocols default to norepinephrine first and reserve epinephrine as an add-on or alternative.
Downfalls / Risks
  • Epinephrine-induced lactate elevation is a recognized phenomenon distinct from hypoperfusion-driven lactate — don't chase this lactate rise with more fluid/pressor if the patient is otherwise improving hemodynamically
  • More arrhythmogenic than norepinephrine at equivalent pressor effect given stronger beta-1 activity
  • Tachycardia can be problematic in patients with concurrent coronary ischemia (increased myocardial oxygen demand)
⚡ Pearl
The lactate-elevation phenomenon is one of the most common sources of bedside confusion in patients on an epinephrine infusion — trend the whole clinical picture, not lactate in isolation, once epinephrine is running.

Vasopressin

AVP · antidiuretic hormone (ADH), used pharmacologically at supraphysiologic doses
Adjunct / catecholamine-sparing agent — non-catecholamine
Dose range
Fixed-dose infusion, typically 0.03–0.04 units/min (not weight-based, and not titrated up or down like a catecholamine — it is added at a fixed rate)
Onset
Minutes
Half-life
10–35 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

Vasopressin's use as an adjunct vasopressor emerged from the observation that septic shock is frequently associated with a relative vasopressin deficiency, providing a physiologic rationale distinct from simply 'more vasoconstriction.'

Physiology

Acts on V1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle (vasoconstriction) rather than adrenergic receptors — a genuinely different mechanism from every catecholamine on this list, which is why it retains vasoconstrictive effect even in acidotic, catecholamine-resistant states where adrenergic receptors become less responsive.

Indications
  • Standard adjunct added to norepinephrine in septic shock, per Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance, typically once norepinephrine reaches a moderate dose — added as a fixed-dose infusion rather than titrated as a primary agent
  • Catecholamine-sparing effect reduces total adrenergic exposure and its associated arrhythmia/ischemia risk
Evidence
VASST trial (Russell 2008, NEJM): addition of vasopressin to norepinephrine in septic shock produced a catecholamine-sparing effect but did NOT demonstrate a mortality benefit over norepinephrine alone at 28 or 90 days — an important, frequently over-stated point; vasopressin is added for its physiologic rationale and catecholamine-sparing effect, not because trial data proves it saves lives. A post-hoc analysis suggested possible benefit in less severe septic shock (baseline norepinephrine <15 mcg/min) but this is hypothesis-generating.
Downfalls / Risks
  • No mortality benefit demonstrated in the pivotal trial — don't oversell it to trainees as more evidence-based than it is
  • Should be used as a fixed-dose adjunct, not titrated up as if it were a primary pressor — titrating vasopressin dose upward has not been shown to add benefit and increases risk of digital/mesenteric ischemia
  • Coronary and mesenteric vasoconstriction risk, particularly at higher-than-standard doses
⚡ Pearl
A machine-learning analysis found higher norepinephrine-equivalent dose at the time vasopressin is added correlates with worse outcomes — earlier addition (rather than as a last resort at very high catecholamine doses) may be more physiologically rational, an evolving area of practice.

Phenylephrine

Neo-Synephrine
Pure vasoconstrictor — non-catecholamine sympathomimetic
Dose range
Infusion: 0.1–5 mcg/kg/min (or 10–180 mcg/min fixed dosing). IV push: 50–200 mcg boluses (common in OB/anesthesia settings).
Onset
Seconds
Half-life
~5 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

Long used in anesthesia and obstetric practice for its favorable profile in specific narrow clinical scenarios where a pure vasoconstrictor without inotropic/chronotropic effect is actually the goal, rather than a downside.

Physiology

Pure alpha-1 agonist with essentially no beta activity — raises SVR and BP without directly increasing heart rate or contractility. Because it does nothing to support a failing heart, it is a poor general-purpose shock agent, but its 'no chronotropy' property is exactly what's wanted in a few specific situations below.

Indications
  • Spinal anesthesia-induced hypotension in obstetric anesthesia — preferred first-line vasopressor in this setting per modern OB anesthesia literature, having displaced ephedrine due to more favorable fetal acid-base outcomes in comparative trials
  • Hypotension with concurrent tachycardia (e.g., peri-procedural, tachyarrhythmia-associated) where you specifically don't want additional chronotropic drive
  • Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy with hypotension — raising afterload without increasing contractility or heart rate is specifically desirable in HOCM to avoid worsening the dynamic outflow obstruction
  • Severe aortic stenosis with hypotension, for similar reasons — avoiding tachycardia preserves diastolic filling time in a fixed-obstruction lesion
Downfalls / Risks
  • Reflex bradycardia via baroreceptor response — can be pronounced and is sometimes therapeutically exploited (see indications above) but can also be a problem in patients who need chronotropic support
  • Does nothing for a failing myocardium — poor choice as primary pressor in cardiogenic shock or any state where inotropic support is also needed
  • Pure vasoconstriction can reduce cardiac output in patients with limited cardiac reserve by increasing afterload without any inotropic compensation
⚡ Pearl
The single best 'why would you ever pick this over norepinephrine' teaching point: phenylephrine's lack of chronotropy is a feature, not a bug, specifically in HOCM, severe AS, and tachycardia-associated hypotension — everywhere else, its inability to support contractility makes it a second-tier choice.

Dopamine

Intropin
Largely historical first-line agent — catecholamine, dose-dependent receptor profile
Dose range
Historically dose-stratified (‘renal dose’ 1–3, ‘cardiac dose’ 3–10, ‘pressor dose’ 10–20 mcg/kg/min) — the dose-response tiers are now considered an oversimplified/largely discredited framework (see downfalls)
Onset
Seconds
Half-life
~2 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

The dominant first-line vasopressor for shock of all types through the late 20th century, including a specific AHA/ACC recommendation as first-line for hypotensive acute MI — a recommendation SOAP II data directly challenged and effectively overturned.

Physiology

Dose-dependent, receptor-tiered pharmacology was the classic teaching: low-dose selective dopaminergic (renal/splanchnic vasodilation), moderate-dose beta-1 predominant (inotropy/chronotropy), high-dose alpha-1 predominant (vasoconstriction). This clean tiering is now understood to be an oversimplification — substantial inter-patient variability in the actual dose-receptor relationship undermines the classic 'renal dose dopamine' teaching entirely.

Indications
  • Largely fallen out of favor as first-line therapy for any shock state following SOAP II and subsequent data
  • Occasionally still used for symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine, as a chronotropic bridge, though epinephrine/isoproterenol/pacing are generally preferred in current algorithms
Evidence
'Renal-dose dopamine' to prevent or treat AKI has been definitively disproven by multiple RCTs and meta-analyses showing no benefit on renal outcomes, mortality, or need for renal replacement therapy — this remains one of the most persistent zombie teachings in medicine despite the evidence, and should be treated as flatly incorrect if encountered in current practice. SOAP II (see Norepinephrine monograph) demonstrated significantly more arrhythmias with dopamine versus norepinephrine and a signal toward harm in cardiogenic shock specifically.
Downfalls / Risks
  • 'Renal-dose dopamine' is not real — does not prevent or treat AKI and should not be used for this indication
  • Significantly higher arrhythmia rate than norepinephrine (24.1% vs 12.4% in SOAP II), predominantly atrial fibrillation
  • Possible harm signal in cardiogenic shock specifically (SOAP II subgroup, hypothesis-generating but consistent across reanalyses)
⚡ Pearl
If you hear 'renal-dose dopamine' at the bedside in 2026, it's worth a gentle, evidence-based correction — the concept has been disproven for over two decades but persists in oral tradition.

Dobutamine

Dobutrex
Inotrope — catecholamine, beta-1 predominant
Dose range
2–20 mcg/kg/min
Onset
1–2 minutes
Half-life
~2 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

Developed specifically as a synthetic catecholamine with a more favorable inotrope-to-chronotrope ratio than isoproterenol, filling the need for a pure inotropic support agent distinct from the vasopressors above.

Physiology

Predominantly beta-1 agonism with mild beta-2 and minimal alpha-1 activity — the mild beta-2 component causes modest peripheral vasodilation, meaning dobutamine can transiently drop blood pressure, which is why it is an inotrope, not a pressor, and is typically used in combination with (not instead of) a vasoconstrictor when both inotropic support and blood pressure support are needed.

Indications
  • Cardiogenic shock / acute decompensated heart failure with low cardiac output and adequate-to-low blood pressure — the classic 'cold and wet' patient on the Forrester/Nohria clinical profile who needs inotropic support
  • Dobutamine stress echocardiography (pharmacologic stress testing) — unrelated to its shock indication but a major use case
  • Often combined with norepinephrine in cardiogenic shock with concurrent hypotension — dobutamine for contractility, norepinephrine for pressure
Downfalls / Risks
  • Can precipitate or worsen hypotension via its mild beta-2 vasodilatory effect — requires a concurrent vasopressor in a hypotensive patient
  • Tachyarrhythmias, particularly at higher doses
  • Tachyphylaxis with prolonged (>72 hour) continuous infusion due to beta-receptor downregulation
⚡ Pearl
Think of dobutamine and norepinephrine as a team in cardiogenic shock with hypotension: dobutamine supplies the squeeze (contractility), norepinephrine supplies the pressure — dobutamine alone in a hypotensive patient can make things worse.

Milrinone

Primacor
Inodilator — phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor, non-catecholamine
Dose range
0.125–0.75 mcg/kg/min (typically no loading dose in shock given hypotension risk; renal dose adjustment required)
Onset
5–15 minutes
Half-life
~2–3 hours (markedly prolonged in renal impairment — up to 20+ hours in severe renal failure)
Receptor Profile
History

Developed as a non-catecholamine alternative for inotropic support, working downstream of the adrenergic receptor entirely — valuable specifically in patients whose adrenergic receptors are downregulated or desensitized from chronic heart failure and chronic beta-blocker use.

Physiology

Inhibits phosphodiesterase-3, increasing intracellular cAMP independent of beta-adrenergic receptor stimulation — produces both positive inotropy (cardiac effect) and vasodilation (peripheral and pulmonary vascular effect, the latter making it particularly useful in right heart failure/pulmonary hypertension). Because it bypasses the beta-receptor entirely, it retains efficacy in patients on chronic beta-blocker therapy, where dobutamine's effect can be blunted.

Indications
  • Acute decompensated heart failure with low cardiac output, particularly in patients on chronic beta-blocker therapy where dobutamine may be less effective
  • Right heart failure / pulmonary hypertension — the pulmonary vasodilatory effect is specifically advantageous here, distinguishing it from dobutamine
  • Post-cardiotomy low cardiac output syndrome
Downfalls / Risks
  • Significant hypotension risk given its vasodilatory component — often avoided or used cautiously as a primary agent in hypotensive shock without concurrent vasopressor support
  • Long half-life becomes a real liability in renal impairment — an adverse hemodynamic effect can persist for many hours after stopping the infusion in a patient with reduced renal clearance, unlike the near-instantly-reversible catecholamines
  • Renal dose adjustment is essential and frequently overlooked
⚡ Pearl
The long, renally-dependent half-life is the single most important practical difference from every catecholamine on this list — you cannot 'just turn it off' and expect immediate reversal the way you can with norepinephrine or epinephrine.

Angiotensin II

Giapreza
Non-catecholamine vasoconstrictor — RAAS effector, refractory shock
Dose range
Starting 20 ng/kg/min, titrated (max initial 80 ng/kg/min; maintenance typically 1.25–40 ng/kg/min)
Onset
Minutes
Half-life
~1 minute (very short, requires continuous infusion)
Receptor Profile
History

FDA-approved in December 2017 following the ATHOS-3 trial — the newest agent in the standard vasopressor armamentarium, developed specifically to address catecholamine-refractory vasodilatory shock via a genuinely distinct mechanism (the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system) rather than another adrenergic or vasopressin-pathway agent.

Physiology

Directly activates the AT1 receptor on vascular smooth muscle, producing potent vasoconstriction through the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis — a third distinct mechanistic pathway alongside adrenergic (catecholamines) and V1 (vasopressin) receptor systems, useful precisely when both of the other pathways are exhausted or ineffective.

Indications
  • Catecholamine-refractory vasodilatory shock — FDA-approved specifically for adults with septic or other vasodilatory shock who remain hypotensive despite high-dose catecholamines and standard-of-care vasopressors including vasopressin
Evidence
ATHOS-3 trial (Khanna 2017, NEJM, n=321): angiotensin II significantly increased MAP within 30 minutes and produced a catecholamine-sparing effect versus placebo, with 97% of enrolled patients already on norepinephrine and 67% already on vasopressin at randomization. The trial was not powered for mortality, though a non-significant trend toward reduced mortality was seen overall, reaching statistical significance in the sickest subgroup (APACHE II >30). Post-hoc analyses suggest possible benefit in AKI and ARDS subgroups, and that earlier initiation (at lower background vasopressor doses) may be more effective than waiting until catecholamine doses are very high — an evolving area, not yet a firm recommendation.
Downfalls / Risks
  • Reserved for refractory shock after other standard agents have failed — not a first- or second-line agent in any current guideline
  • Thromboembolic events were numerically more common in the angiotensin II arm of ATHOS-3, warranting DVT prophylaxis consideration
  • Expensive and not universally stocked outside larger academic/tertiary centers
⚡ Pearl
When norepinephrine, vasopressin, and often epinephrine have all been tried and a patient remains refractory, angiotensin II offers a genuinely different mechanism rather than just 'more of the same' — worth knowing exists even if you rarely reach for it.

Isoproterenol

Isuprel
Pure beta-agonist — catecholamine, chronotrope
Dose range
2–10 mcg/min infusion (bradycardia/heart block); different dosing context for torsades
Onset
Seconds
Half-life
~2–5 minutes
Receptor Profile
History

One of the older synthetic catecholamines, now used in a small number of specific, well-defined scenarios rather than as a general shock agent given its lack of any alpha activity.

Physiology

Pure, non-selective beta-agonist (beta-1 and beta-2, zero alpha activity) — potent chronotropic and modest inotropic effect with peripheral vasodilation (from unopposed beta-2 activity) rather than vasoconstriction. This profile makes it useless as a pressor but valuable specifically for its chronotropic effect in a narrow set of bradyarrhythmia scenarios.

Indications
  • Symptomatic bradycardia or high-grade AV block refractory to atropine, as a temporizing chronotropic bridge to pacing
  • Torsades de pointes associated with bradycardia-dependent QT prolongation — increasing heart rate shortens the QT interval and can terminate the arrhythmia (used specifically in this bradycardia-dependent subtype, not as a general torsades treatment)
  • Heart transplant recipients with symptomatic bradycardia — the denervated transplanted heart doesn't respond to atropine (which works via vagal blockade), making isoproterenol's direct beta-agonism specifically useful in this population
Downfalls / Risks
  • No alpha activity means it will not support blood pressure and can worsen hypotension via beta-2-mediated vasodilation — not a shock/pressor agent under any circumstance
  • Significant arrhythmogenic potential given potent, non-selective beta stimulation
  • Increases myocardial oxygen demand substantially — caution in ischemic heart disease
⚡ Pearl
The heart transplant/atropine-refractory-bradycardia indication is a classic board and bedside pearl: atropine won't work on a denervated heart because there's no vagal tone to block — isoproterenol's direct receptor agonism bypasses that problem entirely.

Methylene Blue

MB
Vasoplegia rescue agent — guanylate cyclase inhibitor, non-catecholamine
Dose range
1–2 mg/kg IV bolus over 20–30 minutes, may repeat or follow with 0.25–1 mg/kg/hr infusion
Onset
Minutes
Half-life
~5–6 hours
Receptor Profile
History

A thiazine dye with a long, varied clinical history (methemoglobinemia treatment, historically as an antimalarial and antiseptic) repurposed for vasoplegic syndrome after cardiac surgery once its nitric oxide/guanylate cyclase mechanism was recognized as directly relevant to the pathophysiology of vasoplegia.

Physiology

Inhibits soluble guanylate cyclase, blocking the nitric oxide/cGMP pathway that drives pathologic vasodilation in vasoplegic states — a mechanism entirely distinct from every agent above, targeting the excess-NO pathophysiology directly rather than simply overpowering it with more vasoconstrictor stimulation.

Indications
  • Vasoplegic syndrome after cardiac surgery/cardiopulmonary bypass — the best-established indication, incidence 5–44% of on-pump cardiac surgery depending on definition, associated with higher mortality when untreated
  • Rescue therapy for refractory septic shock unresponsive to standard vasopressors — not recommended as routine therapy by Surviving Sepsis Campaign given limited prospective data, used as a last-resort rescue agent
  • Refractory vasoplegic shock from specific drug toxicity — case reports describe successful use in quetiapine and amlodipine overdose-associated vasoplegia
Evidence
A single-center retrospective cohort (n=200 vasoplegic syndrome patients post-cardiac surgery) found shorter ICU stay in patients receiving methylene blue versus controls; earlier smaller trials (Levin 2004) reported reduced mortality/morbidity with methylene blue in post-cardiac-surgery vasoplegia. Current evidence is graded as a weak (2C) recommendation for post-cardiac-surgery vasoplegia and explicitly not routinely recommended by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign for septic shock, reserved instead for refractory rescue use.
Downfalls / Risks
  • Serotonin syndrome risk — methylene blue is a potent MAO-A inhibitor; documented, sometimes severe serotonin syndrome cases have occurred when given to patients on serotonergic medications (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, certain opioids like meperidine/tramadol/fentanyl) — medication reconciliation for serotonergic agents before administration is a genuine patient-safety step, not a formality
  • Interferes with pulse oximetry readings (falsely lowers SpO2) and with methemoglobin/co-oximetry measurements for a period after administration
  • Blue-green discoloration of skin/urine, which can alarm patients and families if not anticipated and explained
  • Contraindicated or used with extreme caution in G6PD deficiency (risk of hemolytic anemia) and in pregnancy
⚡ Pearl
Always reconcile for serotonergic medications before giving methylene blue — documented severe serotonin syndrome cases in the literature involved concurrent meperidine or SSRI/SNRI therapy, and this is one of the more dangerous, under-recognized drug interactions in critical care.

🩹 Peripheral Vasopressor Administration

The mandate that vasopressors must run through a central line is one of the most consequential pieces of outdated dogma still shaping ED and ICU practice — and unlike some myths in this atlas series, this one has enough large, high-quality data behind it now that it has actually changed formal society guidance, not just bedside opinion.

✅ Guideline Status
The 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines include a conditional recommendation to initiate vasopressors peripherally, if needed, to avoid delaying treatment while central access is obtained — a direct, guideline-level endorsement of what emergency and critical care literature had already been demonstrating for several years.

Supporting Data

Study / protocolFinding
Pooled extravasation rate across peripheral vasopressor studies (2020–2026 meta-analyses)~1.4–2.2%, with a broader literature range of 0.035–5%
Pooled adverse event rate, most recent systematic review~1.8%
Norepinephrine (16–32 µg/mL) + vasopressin (0.16 IU/mL), forearm/antecubital 20–22G catheters, n=202Safe up to 7.5 days (norepinephrine) and 12.5 days (vasopressin) of peripheral infusion
Peripheral norepinephrine protocol (≤18G, at/above antecubital fossa, max 20 µg/min, max 4h), n=1774.5% extravasation rate, no surgical intervention required in any case
Perioperative norepinephrine, n=1,004 patients2.3% extravasation rate, zero cases of tissue necrosis
Peripheral epinephrine in cardiogenic shock, n=1,100Extravasation/phlebitis rates comparable to central venous administration
VCUHS ED protocol implementationPeripheral norepinephrine avoided central line placement in 51.6% of patients — roughly half avoided a CVC entirely

Protocol Essentials

  • Catheter: 18G or larger preferred, though 20–22G has been used safely in several studies — bigger and more proximal is better but smaller-gauge data isn't disqualifying
  • Site: at or above the antecubital fossa (or external jugular) — avoid hand/wrist/forearm distal sites specifically because they carry the highest extravasation-related tissue injury risk if it does occur
  • Duration: most protocols cap peripheral infusion at 4–72 hours as a safety buffer while central access is arranged, though several studies demonstrated safety well beyond this window — treat the time limit as an institutional safety margin, not a hard physiologic cutoff
  • Monitoring: frequent site checks (hourly is typical) specifically looking for swelling, blanching, or pain at the insertion site — catching early extravasation before it progresses is the entire safety strategy
  • No cases of ischemic necrosis have been reported across the large pooled peripheral vasopressor literature when a proximal, large-bore protocol was followed
Extravasation management
Phentolamine (an alpha-adrenergic antagonist) is the standard rescue treatment for vasopressor extravasation — infiltrated locally around the extravasation site to reverse local vasoconstriction and limit tissue injury. It accounted for roughly a third of documented extravasation-event interventions in pooled data, underscoring that even when extravasation does occur, it is a manageable, treatable event rather than an inevitable catastrophe.
⚡ Clinical Bottom Line
For a patient who needs a vasopressor now and doesn't yet have central access, starting norepinephrine (or another vasopressor) through a good peripheral IV at or above the antecubital fossa is current, guideline-supported practice — not a stopgap workaround. Central access remains appropriate for anticipated prolonged, high-dose, or multi-agent vasopressor therapy, but it should not be the rate-limiting step that delays vasopressor initiation in an acutely hypotensive patient.

🎯 Shock-Type Selection Framework

Recognizing the shock category correctly matters more than knowing every agent's dose — the wrong category-level decision (e.g., pouring pressors into obstructive shock instead of decompressing it) won't be rescued by good pharmacology.

Septic / Distributive

Mechanism: Pathologic vasodilation (low SVR) with initially preserved or elevated cardiac output — the vasculature has lost its normal tone.

First-line: Norepinephrine

Add-on / escalation: Vasopressin (fixed-dose adjunct once norepinephrine reaches moderate dose) → epinephrine or angiotensin II if still refractory → methylene blue as rescue in truly refractory vasoplegia

Avoid
Dopamine (more arrhythmias, no mortality benefit); pure inotropes alone (dobutamine) without a vasoconstrictor, since the primary problem is vascular tone, not pump function

Fluid resuscitation and source control remain co-equal pillars alongside vasopressor selection — see the companion Fluid Resuscitation Atlas and Sepsis Atlas series for full management.

Cardiogenic

Mechanism: Pump failure — inadequate cardiac output despite adequate or elevated filling pressures.

First-line: Depends on blood pressure: if hypotensive, norepinephrine (preferred over dopamine per SOAP II subgroup data); if adequate/borderline pressure with low output ('cold and wet'), dobutamine or milrinone

Add-on / escalation: Dobutamine + norepinephrine combination is standard when both inotropy and pressure support are needed; milrinone preferred over dobutamine in patients on chronic beta-blocker therapy or with concurrent right heart failure/pulmonary hypertension

Avoid
Dopamine (SOAP II subgroup harm signal); dobutamine or milrinone as monotherapy in a hypotensive patient (both can worsen hypotension via vasodilation)

Mechanical circulatory support (IABP, Impella, VA-ECMO) enters the algorithm for refractory cardiogenic shock — beyond this atlas's pharmacologic scope but worth remembering as the next escalation step.

Hypovolemic

Mechanism: Inadequate circulating volume — the primary problem is preload, not vascular tone or pump function.

First-line: Volume resuscitation is the primary therapy, not vasopressors — see the Fluid Resuscitation Atlas

Add-on / escalation: Norepinephrine as a bridge if hypotension is severe/immediately life-threatening while volume resuscitation catches up, or if hemorrhage control is not yet achieved — not a substitute for volume/blood product replacement

Avoid
Relying on vasopressors as primary therapy while under-resuscitating volume — this is treating the wrong variable

In hemorrhagic shock specifically, permissive hypotension and damage-control resuscitation principles (see Fluid Atlas Trauma section) generally take priority over vasopressor initiation until hemorrhage control is achieved.

Obstructive

Mechanism: Mechanical impediment to cardiac filling or output — tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive PE, severe dynamic hyperinflation.

First-line: Definitive mechanical intervention (needle decompression/chest tube, pericardiocentesis, thrombolysis/thrombectomy for massive PE) — vasopressors are a bridge, not a fix

Add-on / escalation: Norepinephrine as a temporizing bridge to definitive intervention; avoid excessive positive-pressure ventilation changes that could worsen obstructive physiology (e.g., breath-stacking in severe asthma/COPD)

Avoid
Treating obstructive shock as if it will respond to pressors/fluids alone without addressing the mechanical cause — it will not resolve without the definitive intervention

Recognizing the obstructive category quickly is the highest-yield cognitive step here — no amount of correct vasopressor selection substitutes for decompressing a tension pneumothorax.

Anaphylactic

Mechanism: Combined distributive (mast-cell-mediated vasodilation and capillary leak) and potential cardiogenic/obstructive components (bronchospasm, laryngeal edema) — genuinely multi-mechanism.

First-line: Epinephrine IM (anterolateral thigh) — not IV, not an antihistamine, not a steroid, as first-line therapy

Add-on / escalation: IV epinephrine infusion for refractory/severe cases requiring continuous support; aggressive crystalloid for the capillary-leak component; consider vasopressin or norepinephrine as second-line pressors in truly refractory anaphylactic shock

Avoid
Treating anaphylaxis primarily with antihistamines/steroids while delaying or under-dosing epinephrine — epinephrine is the only agent that addresses all three mechanistic components (vasoconstriction, bronchodilation, mediator-release inhibition) simultaneously

This is the one shock category where the first-line agent (epinephrine) is chosen for its unique multi-receptor profile rather than because of a mortality trial — the physiologic rationale is the evidence base here.

Neurogenic

Mechanism: Loss of sympathetic tone from spinal cord injury (typically at or above T6) — vasodilation plus, distinctively, bradycardia (unopposed vagal tone) rather than the reflex tachycardia seen in other distributive shock.

First-line: Norepinephrine (provides both the alpha-mediated vasoconstriction and beta-1-mediated chronotropic support that the bradycardic component specifically needs)

Add-on / escalation: Phenylephrine alone should generally be avoided as primary therapy here specifically because its pure alpha activity does nothing for the bradycardia and can worsen it via reflex baroreceptor response; atropine or a chronotrope (e.g., isoproterenol, or pacing) for symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to norepinephrine's beta-1 effect

Avoid
Assuming this is 'just another distributive shock' and reaching for phenylephrine out of habit — the bradycardia component is the key distinguishing feature that changes the preferred agent

The bradycardia-plus-hypotension combination (rather than the tachycardia-plus-hypotension seen in septic/anaphylactic/hypovolemic shock) is the single fastest bedside clue that you're dealing with neurogenic shock in a trauma patient.

📈 Invasive Hemodynamic Monitoring

Arterial Line & Waveform Interpretation

An indwelling arterial catheter (radial most common, femoral/axillary for larger vessels or when radial access is inadequate) transduces continuous, beat-to-beat pressure directly from the arterial system, providing a real MAP rather than the intermittent, motion-artifact-prone estimate from a cuff — essential when titrating a vasopressor infusion in real time.

Waveform Anatomy

A normal arterial waveform has a sharp systolic upstroke (anacrotic limb), a peak, a dicrotic notch on the downslope (marking aortic valve closure), and a gradual diastolic runoff. The dicrotic notch's position and sharpness carry physiologic information: vasodilation produces a wider pulse pressure with a delayed, less distinct dicrotic notch, while vasoconstriction narrows the pulse pressure.

The Square-Wave (Fast-Flush) Test

The fast-flush (square-wave) test is the essential quality check before trusting any arterial line reading: flush the line briefly and observe the tracing's return to baseline. A properly damped system shows a square wave followed by one or two small oscillations before settling. An underdamped system (common with long, narrow, or air-bubble-containing tubing) overshoots with multiple oscillations and artificially exaggerates systolic pressure while underestimating diastolic. An overdamped system (common with clots, kinks, or air bubbles in the line) shows a sluggish, rounded return with no oscillation, artificially narrowing the pulse pressure and often underestimating systolic pressure — both distortions can lead to real titration errors if not recognized and corrected before acting on the numbers.

Pitfall
Any pulse-contour-derived cardiac output estimate (see below) is only as good as the underlying waveform — an under- or overdamped line will produce a confidently wrong cardiac output number, not just a wrong blood pressure number, since these algorithms rely on precise identification of the dicrotic notch and waveform morphology.

Central Venous Pressure (CVP)

Central venous pressure was historically used as a surrogate for right ventricular preload and, by extension, fluid responsiveness — a single static number meant to answer 'will this patient's cardiac output improve with more fluid?'

⚠ Why It Fails
CVP has been repeatedly and consistently shown to poorly predict fluid responsiveness across a large body of literature — a normal or even low CVP does not reliably mean a patient will benefit from more fluid, and a high CVP does not reliably mean they won't. Right ventricular compliance, intrathoracic pressure (mechanical ventilation, PEEP), tricuspid valve function, and pulmonary vascular resistance all confound the CVP-to-preload relationship enough that static CVP alone is now considered an unreliable single-number decision tool.
Modern role
CVP retains value as a trend over time in a given patient, as one input among several in a broader hemodynamic assessment, and for specific indications (central line placement confirmation, some right heart failure assessments) — but dynamic measures (passive leg raise, pulse pressure variation, stroke volume variation, IVC ultrasound) are preferred over a static CVP number for actually answering the fluid-responsiveness question, consistent with the POCUS-forward approach referenced throughout the Fluid Resuscitation Atlas.

Cardiac Output Monitoring Devices

DeviceHow it works & tradeoffs
Pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz)The historical gold standard — directly measures cardiac output via thermodilution, plus pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, mixed venous oxygen saturation, and pulmonary artery pressures. Highly invasive with real procedural risk (arrhythmia during placement, pulmonary artery rupture, infection); use has declined substantially as less-invasive alternatives matured, though it remains valuable in complex right heart failure/pulmonary hypertension management where its unique pressure data isn't replicated by other devices.
PiCCO (transpulmonary thermodilution + pulse contour analysis)A 'calibrated' device: uses transpulmonary thermodilution (central venous cold-saline injection, measured by a specialized femoral arterial catheter) to periodically calibrate a continuous pulse-contour-derived cardiac output estimate. Less invasive than a PA catheter (no right heart catheterization) but still requires a specialized arterial catheter and periodic recalibration; accuracy depends on catheter position and requires the arterial waveform to be free of damping artifact.
FloTrac/Vigileo (uncalibrated pulse contour analysis)The least invasive of the three — requires only a standard arterial line and a specific pressure transducer/algorithm, no thermodilution calibration needed. Convenient, but reliability is more debated in the literature than calibrated devices, particularly during rapidly changing hemodynamics, high-dose vasopressor states, or vasoplegia; comparative studies against PiCCO and PA catheters show variable agreement depending on clinical state.
Echocardiography (POCUS or formal echo)Non-invasive, repeatable, and increasingly the first-line bedside tool for assessing cardiac output, contractility, and volume status together (LVOT VTI-based stroke volume estimation, IVC assessment) without any additional catheter — the tradeoff is that it's intermittent/operator-dependent rather than continuous, unlike the catheter-based systems above.
⚡ Clinical Bottom Line
The overall trend across the last 15–20 years of hemodynamic monitoring has been away from static, single-number invasive measurements (PA catheter wedge pressure, static CVP) and toward dynamic, trend-based, and increasingly non-invasive or minimally-invasive assessments (POCUS, pulse pressure/stroke volume variation, passive leg raise) — mirroring the same shift discussed for fluid responsiveness in the companion Fluid Resuscitation Atlas.

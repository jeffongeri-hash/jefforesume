1 · Primary Survey, Shock Classification & Hemorrhage Control

ABCDE, catastrophic hemorrhage-first variants, shock staging, and the hierarchy of bleeding control.

The Primary Survey

ATLS 10th ed.

Standard ABCDE

  1. A — Airway with C-spine protection. Look, listen, jaw thrust, suction, adjuncts.
  2. B — Breathing & ventilation. Expose the chest; treat tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, flail chest immediately when found.
  3. C — Circulation with hemorrhage control. External bleeding controlled by direct pressure/tourniquet before IV access; identify occult sources (chest, abdomen, pelvis, long bones, floor).
  4. D — Disability. GCS, pupils, gross motor/sensory, glucose.
  5. E — Exposure/Environment. Fully undress, log roll, prevent hypothermia — a warm patient clots.

Treat life threats the instant they're found — do not wait to finish the sequence.

<C>ABCDE / MARCH (catastrophic hemorrhage first)

Military-derived TCCC framework, increasingly adopted civilian-side for penetrating trauma and mass casualty:

  1. <C> / M — Massive hemorrhage: tourniquet or direct pressure on compressible external bleeding before airway.
  2. A — Airway (with C-spine)
  3. R — Respiration (needle/finger decompression, seal open chest wounds)
  4. C — Circulation (remaining hemorrhage control, IV/IO access, TXA)
  5. H — Hypothermia/Head injury
When to use whichAn exsanguinating extremity wound with an obvious tourniquet-amenable source justifies hemorrhage-first sequencing even in civilian EDs; a patient with a compromised airway and no visible catastrophic bleeding still gets airway first.

Known limitation — teach this alongside the table, not instead of it Multiple validation studies (including a >140,000-patient German trauma registry analysis) found most real patients don't fit neatly into one class — vital signs are individually poor predictors of blood loss, and only a small minority of major trauma patients land cleanly in Class III/IV by these criteria before deteriorating. The 10th edition added base deficit and need for blood products to the matrix for this reason. Teach the classification as a conceptual anchor for the physiology of compensated → decompensated shock, not as a rigid diagnostic tool — trend the patient's response to resuscitation and lactate/base deficit clearance over any single vital sign snapshot.

Hemorrhage Control Hierarchy

External / compressible hemorrhage

  • Direct pressure — first line, sustained, two-handed if needed.
  • Tourniquet — for exsanguinating extremity hemorrhage; place proximal to the wound, 2–3 inches above (not directly over a joint), tighten until bleeding and distal pulse stop. Document time of application — this travels with the patient.
  • Wound packing + pressure — for junctional or proximal wounds a tourniquet can't reach (groin, axilla, neck base). Hemostatic gauze (kaolin- or chitosan-based) packed directly into the wound tract with sustained 3-minute pressure.
  • Junctional tourniquets (e.g., abdominal aortic/junctional devices) for true junctional zones when packing fails.
Tourniquet myth checkProlonged civilian tourniquet time (<2h) is not associated with the limb-loss rates historically feared from anecdote — under-resuscitation and delayed hemorrhage control kill far more often than tourniquet time. Don't loosen a working tourniquet to "check" unless directed by a surgeon accepting the patient.

Non-compressible / occult hemorrhage

  • Pelvic binder — place at the greater trochanters (not iliac crests) for suspected pelvic ring injury with hemodynamic instability. See diagram in Domain 5.
  • REBOA (resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta) — Zone I (supraceliac) for abdominal/pelvic hemorrhage, Zone III (infrarenal) for pelvic/junctional — a bridge to surgical/IR control, not definitive therapy.
  • Damage control laparotomy/thoracotomy — definitive source control for cavitary hemorrhage; the ED's job is to buy time and correct physiology, not chase a normal INR before transfer to the OR.

The four places a trauma patient exsanguinates internally: chest, abdomen, pelvis/retroperitoneum, and long bones (femur can sequester 1–1.5L closed).

Secondary Survey & Damage Control Resuscitation Principles

Secondary survey & AMPLE

Head-to-toe exam, log roll, reassess vitals — only after the primary survey life threats are addressed and resuscitation is underway.

  • Allergies
  • Medications (anticoagulants change your entire hemorrhage calculus)
  • Past illness/pregnancy
  • Last meal
  • Events/environment of injury

Damage control resuscitation (DCR)

  • Permissive hypotension — target SBP ~80–90 mmHg (MAP ~50–60) in penetrating trauma without TBI until hemorrhage is controlled; avoid "normalizing" pressure with crystalloid, which raises hydrostatic pressure on unclotted vessels and dilutes clotting factors. Not used in TBI (needs adequate CPP) or in the field for the frail/elderly.
  • Balanced blood product resuscitation (1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets or whole blood) over crystalloid — detailed in Domain 3.
  • Minimize crystalloid — each liter of NS given pre-hospital/early ED has been associated with worsening coagulopathy in large trauma cohorts.
  • Correct the lethal triad early: hypothermia (warm fluids/blankets, avoid exposure), acidosis (perfusion, not bicarb), coagulopathy (blood products, calcium, TXA).
Verify before relying Specific numeric thresholds (permissive hypotension targets, tourniquet documentation practices, institutional MTP activation criteria) vary by trauma system and institutional protocol — confirm against your center's current protocol before teaching as an absolute.

2 · Airway & Chest Trauma + Procedures

Trauma airway decision-making, surgical airway, thoracic decompression, and resuscitative thoracotomy criteria.

Trauma Airway & RSI

Recognizing the difficult/failing trauma airway

  • Expanding neck hematoma, direct laryngeal/tracheal injury, facial fractures distorting anatomy, oral/airway hemorrhage, burns with inhalational injury (soot, singed vibrissae, hoarseness).
  • Reduced physiologic reserve: hypovolemic patients desaturate fast and tolerate apnea poorly — preoxygenate maximally, have push-dose pressors ready.
Peri-intubation arrestThe commonest trauma RSI complication isn't a failed tube — it's peri-intubation cardiovascular collapse in an under-resuscitated, hypovolemic patient from the combined effects of positive-pressure ventilation (↓ preload) and induction-agent vasodilation. Resuscitate before you push drugs when you have the luxury of time.

RSI agents — trauma-specific considerations

Etomidate0.3 mg/kg IVHemodynamically neutral; adrenal suppression is a real but generally not clinically decisive concern for a single dose in the acute trauma bay.
Rocuronium1.2 mg/kg IVPreferred over succinylcholine when hyperkalemia risk exists (crush injury, burns >24h old, prolonged immobility) or when a longer paralysis window is acceptable.

Surgical Airway

Procedure

Cricothyroidotomy

Indicated in "can't intubate, can't oxygenate" — typically massive facial/airway trauma or laryngeal disruption where orotracheal intubation and BVM both fail.

  1. Palpate the laryngeal prominence, slide inferiorly to the first soft depression — the cricothyroid membrane.
  2. Vertical skin incision, horizontal stab through the membrane.
  3. Insert bougie or hook, dilate, pass a 6.0 cuffed ET or trach tube.
  4. Confirm with EtCO2 and bilateral breath sounds.

Surgical cricothyroidotomy is generally preferred over needle cricothyroidotomy in adults for a definitive airway; needle cric with jet ventilation is a temporizing bridge, not a durable airway.

Tension Pneumothorax & Needle Decompression

Live controversy

Site A (2nd ICS-MCL, traditional) vs Site B (4th/5th ICS-AAL, ATLS 10th ed.)

Diagnosis

  • Clinical diagnosis in the crashing patient: hypotension, absent/decreased unilateral breath sounds, tracheal deviation (late, unreliable sign), distended neck veins, hyperresonance, hypoxia, rising peak pressures if ventilated.
  • Don't wait for imaging in the peri-arrest patient — needle/finger decompress on exam findings.
Where the evidence actually stands ATLS 10th edition moved the preferred site from the 2nd ICS-midclavicular line to the 4th/5th ICS-anterior axillary line, based on CT/cadaveric data showing thinner chest wall there in many patients. But a 2025 meta-analysis of >8,000 patients found needle length was the dominant driver of failure (each added cm of needle length reduced failure by ~7.8%), not site alone — and injury risk to adjacent structures was actually higher at the lateral sites in some series. In obese patients specifically, the 2nd ICS-MCL can have a thinner chest wall than the lateral sites. European Trauma Course guidelines still favor the 2nd ICS-MCL. Practical takeaway for teaching: know your institution's equipment (standard angiocatheter is often too short for either site in larger patients), and don't present either site as unambiguously superior — it's an active debate, not settled science.

ATLS 10th ed.; 2025 meta-analysis (ResearchGate preprint); PMID 26488319

Other Immediate Chest Threats

Open pneumothorax

"Sucking chest wound" — apply a vented (three-sided) occlusive dressing to prevent tension physiology from a fully sealed dressing; definitive management is tube thoracostomy (placed remote from the wound) plus wound closure.

Flail chest

≥3 contiguous ribs fractured in ≥2 places → paradoxical chest wall motion. The pulmonary contusion underneath the flail segment, not the flail motion itself, drives most of the morbidity. Priorities: analgesia (regional blocks reduce splinting/pneumonia risk), pulmonary toilet, judicious fluids (contused lung is fluid-sensitive), consider surgical rib fixation in selected patients.

Massive hemothorax

≥1500mL immediate output or ≥200mL/hr for 2–4h via chest tube → operative/IR intervention, not just more tubes. Autotransfusion devices can be used where available.

Cardiac tamponade

Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds) is insensitive — subxiphoid FAST view is the fastest reliable bedside tool. Pericardiocentesis is temporizing; definitive management is thoracotomy/sternotomy for pericardial drainage and source control, especially in penetrating trauma.

Tube Thoracostomy & the Triangle of Safety

Triangle of safety: pec major (ant.), lat dorsi (post.), 5th ICS/nipple line (inf.)

Technique pearls

  • Insert just above the rib margin (neurovascular bundle runs along the inferior rib edge).
  • Finger thoracostomy (blunt digital exploration through the incision before tube placement) confirms pleural entry and is itself therapeutic in the peri-arrest patient — part of the TCA priority stack (Domain 3) precisely because it treats tension physiology and tamponade-adjacent pathology in one motion.
  • Confirm placement: fogging in the tube, respirophasic swing, chest X-ray, resolution of exam findings.

Survival numbers to know Penetrating thoracic injury with signs of life on ED arrival: survival ~35% in some series — the single best-performing indication. 3 · Traumatic Cardiac Arrest & Damage Control Resuscitation

Why TCA is not medical arrest, the epinephrine controversy, whole blood, and the current joint position statement.

The Tank / Pipes / Pump Framework

Medical arrest is overwhelmingly a pump problem (VF/VT, ~70–80%). Traumatic arrest is overwhelmingly an empty tank or blocked pipes problem — the heart itself is rarely primarily diseased.

Traumatic arrest etiologyApprox. frequency
Hemorrhage (empty tank)~48%
Tension pneumothorax (blocked pipes)~13%
Cardiac tamponade (blocked pipes)~13%
Hypoxia, TBI, rare primary cardiac causesremainder

This is why the TCA algorithm inverts standard ACLS priorities: hemorrhage control and decompression come before compressions and epinephrine, because compressing an empty tank moves no volume.

The TCA Priority Stack

Sequenced algorithm
  1. Hemorrhage control — tourniquets, wound packing, surgical control.
  2. Bilateral finger thoracostomies if chest trauma is suspected — treats tension pneumothorax and tamponade-adjacent pathology simultaneously.
  3. Whole blood (low-titer O whole blood preferred) or 1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets + calcium — blood restores oxygen-carrying capacity and clotting factors; compressions do not.
  4. EFAST — tamponade? free fluid? cardiac standstill? Views in <10 seconds; never delay hemorrhage control or thoracostomy to get them.
  5. Airway/ventilation.
  6. Compressions — secondary priority; far more effective once volume is restored (compressing an empty ventricle moves nothing).
  7. TXA 1g IV if <3h from injury.
  8. Resuscitative thoracotomy if penetrating mechanism and <15 minutes from loss of vitals (see Domain 2 for full EAST/WTA criteria).
  9. Epinephrine — do NOT give routinely, and never before other life-saving interventions.
  10. Defibrillation — only if VF/pulseless VT is the rhythm; think commotio cordis.

Consolidated from PMID 40068140, 31077754, 31317856 and the 2025 NAEMSP/ACS-COT/ACEP joint statement.

The Epinephrine Controversy

Verified against primary sources

Against routine epi

Witt et al., Prehospital Emergency Care, Feb 2025 (multicenter, 7 Level I/II centers, 2011–2017) — ~1,960 patients with prehospital TCA.

  • Blunt trauma: 3% survival with epi vs. 16% without (p<0.001).
  • Penetrating: no significant difference (4% vs. 6%, p=0.19).
  • Adjusted: epi associated with 67% lower survival overall, 80% lower in blunt trauma specifically (no significant difference in penetrating).

Note for teaching: the overall/blunt-specific numbers are easy to conflate — the "3% vs 16%" figure is the blunt-trauma subgroup, not the whole cohort. Cite the subgroup you mean.

For epi (or at least, "it's complicated")

Contrasting registry data (e.g., Korean and Japanese nationwide OHCA registries) have found prehospital epinephrine associated with increased prehospital ROSC even when overall survival benefit is unclear or absent — consistent across several TCA cohorts internationally.

Reconciling this: epi reliably raises perfusion pressure enough to generate ROSC, but in an empty, injured circulatory system that hasn't had its volume or obstruction addressed, that pressure may do more harm than good (afterload against unrepaired vasculature, arrhythmogenic risk, diverting attention from definitive hemorrhage control).

I could not independently verify the specific "22,105-patient Korean registry, aOR 1.38/3.63" citation referenced in some derivative slide decks — confirm that exact citation before using it in course materials.

The governing statement (verified, current as of this writing) NAEMSP, ACS-COT, and ACEP's 2025 joint position statement on prehospital management of traumatic out-of-hospital circulatory arrest (Annals of Emergency Medicine, March 2025): epinephrine should not be routinely used, and if used, should not be administered before other life-saving interventions. This is the single most citable, current source for teaching this topic.

PMID 39984237; Annals of Emergency Medicine 85(3):e25-e39

EFAST Timing & the POCUS Trap

POCUS during arrest is associated with longer compression pauses, longer overall resuscitations, and more downstream interventions — without a proven outcome benefit. The 2025 joint statement is explicit: cardiac monitoring and POCUS should occur after indicated life-saving interventions, not before or during them.

  • Do: subxiphoid view for tamponade (if positive, moves you toward thoracotomy); confirm cardiac standstill for prognostication after LSIs; keep views under 10 seconds; revisit after ROSC/LSIs are complete.
  • Don't: delay thoracostomy or blood administration to scan; pause compressions >10 seconds for the probe.

PMID 40068140; 2025 NAEMSP/ACS-COT/ACEP joint statement.

Whole Blood, Massive Transfusion & Calcium

Why not crystalloid

Normal saline in a bleeding patient: no oxygen-carrying capacity, dilutes remaining clotting factors and platelets, is room temperature (worsens hypothermia), and its own chemistry (pH ~5.5, 154 mEq/L chloride) drives hyperchloremic acidosis. All three accelerate the lethal triad: hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy.

Whole blood or 1:1:1 fixes the tank and its contents — crystalloid only fills the tank with the wrong material.

Calcium repletion

Citrate anticoagulant in stored blood products chelates ionized calcium → decreased myocardial contractility → decreased cardiac output — the opposite of the goal in a resuscitation.

  • Empiric: 1g CaCl2 (or ~3g calcium gluconate) per ~2 units of blood product transfused.
  • Ideally guided by iSTAT/point-of-care ionized calcium, targeting >1.0–1.2 mmol/L.
  • CaCl2 delivers ~3x the elemental calcium of an equal volume of calcium gluconate and is preferred through central/large-bore access in active resuscitation.
Whole blood: strong trend, evolving RCT evidence Civilian low-titer O whole blood (LTOWB) programs have expanded rapidly, extrapolating from military experience and a growing body of retrospective/observational civilian data associating whole blood with improved 24-hour and in-hospital survival compared with component therapy. Prospective RCT data in civilian trauma is still maturing relative to the strength of the retrospective signal — teach whole blood as the current best-practice direction with strong observational support, not yet as an unambiguous, fully RCT-proven mortality benefit in every population.

TXA

1g IV over 10 minutes, within 3 hours of injury, followed by a 1g infusion over 8 hours in the original CRASH-2 protocol.

  • Benefit is time-critical: CRASH-2 and subsequent analyses show mortality benefit shrinks and may reverse toward harm the later it's given past the 3-hour window — this is not a "give it whenever you remember" drug.
  • Reduces death from bleeding without a significant increase in thrombotic events in the trial population.

The Exception: Commotio Cordis

Verified

Blunt, low-energy chest impact during the vulnerable T-wave upstroke (a ~20–40ms window) → primary VF. Classic mechanisms: baseball, hockey puck, lacrosse ball, other direct precordial impact (e.g., the Damar Hamlin case).

Unlike typical TCA, the tank is full and the pipes are open — this is genuinely a pump problem, so it gets standard ACLS: CPR, early defibrillation, epinephrine per protocol.

Verified survival data US Commotio Cordis Registry data show survival has climbed from ~10% (1970–1993) to ~34% (1994–2012) to 58% in the most recent cohort (2006–2012 data), driven almost entirely by time-to-resuscitation and time-to-defibrillation — 40% survival when CPR starts <3 minutes vs. 5% when delayed beyond 3 minutes. AED-treated cases show a similarly large survival advantage over non-AED cases in registry data.

PMID 37495440 (Heart Rhythm 2013, Maron et al.); AHA newsroom statement 2023

Clinical rule: trauma patient + VF on the monitor + a mechanism consistent with a precordial blow → think commotio cordis → defibrillate, don't withhold shock because "it's a trauma patient."

4 · Adult BLS/ACLS Integration in Trauma

Where standard resuscitation science still applies to a trauma patient, and where it doesn't.

2025 AHA Guidelines note The AHA released a comprehensive update to the CPR/ECC guidelines on October 22, 2025 — the first full update since 2020. Core adult CPR quality targets are unchanged from 2020, but a few things moved: the four previously separate Chains of Survival (adult/pediatric, in-hospital/out-of-hospital) have been consolidated into a single unified 6-link chain, mechanical CPR devices are no longer recommended for routine use, and chest compression fraction is now a required reported metric on course rosters. Verify current specifics against the published 2025 Circulation guideline documents before teaching, since implementation details continue to roll out.

High-Quality CPR Metrics (Adult)

Depth >2.4in is associated with increased injury without added benefit — this is a genuine ceiling, not just a floor of "at least 2 inches."

When Standard ACLS Applies vs. When It Doesn't

Standard ACLS/BLS applies

  • Etiology of arrest is unclear or clearly medical (no signs of life-threatening trauma) — the 2025 joint statement explicitly says treat as medical arrest with standard BLS/ACLS in this scenario.
  • Commotio cordis (Domain 3) — genuine primary VF, gets CPR + early defibrillation + epi per protocol.
  • Hypothermic arrest — prolonged/modified resuscitation, "not dead until warm and dead," limited defibrillation attempts below certain core temperatures pending rewarming.
  • Electrocution/lightning injury — can cause primary VF or asystole from the electrical insult itself; treat per standard ACLS in addition to trauma survey for associated injuries (falls, burns).

Standard ACLS is modified/de-prioritized

  • Confirmed traumatic mechanism with likely hemorrhagic/obstructive etiology — TCA priority stack (Domain 3) takes over: hemorrhage control, thoracostomy, blood products before compressions/epi.
  • Compressions are explicitly secondary in hypovolemic TCA — they move negligible volume against an empty ventricle until preload is restored.
  • Epinephrine should not be given routinely or before other LSIs in TCA (Domain 3).

Special Circumstances

Pregnancy

  • Manual left uterine displacement (LUD) or left lateral tilt during compressions once the uterus is palpable above the umbilicus (roughly ≥20 weeks) to relieve aortocaval compression.
  • Perimortem cesarean delivery (resuscitative hysterotomy) considered at ~4 minutes of maternal arrest without ROSC if the fundus is at/above the umbilicus — goal delivery by ~5 minutes; benefits both maternal (relieves aortocaval compression, improves venous return/CPR efficacy) and fetal outcomes.
  • All standard trauma priorities (hemorrhage control, airway, chest decompression) still apply and are not deferred for the pregnancy itself.

Opioid-associated arrest

  • If clearly respiratory-drive in origin (not yet full arrest), naloxone plus ventilatory support; once in cardiac arrest, standard high-quality CPR/ACLS take priority over naloxone administration.
  • Trauma patients with concomitant intoxication are common — don't let a presumed overdose distract from a full trauma survey, and vice versa.

Hypothermia

  • Severely hypothermic myocardium may be refractory to defibrillation and pharmacologic therapy — many protocols limit defibrillation attempts and hold further epinephrine doses until core temp rises above a threshold, then resume standard dosing intervals.
  • Aggressive rewarming (active external/internal, ECMO where available) alongside continued CPR; "no one is dead until warm and dead" reflects real cases of intact neurologic survival after prolonged hypothermic arrest.

Commotio cordis (cross-reference)

Full detail in Domain 3 — the single most important "exception that proves the rule" for trauma providers: a trauma-mechanism arrest that is nonetheless a genuine primary dysrhythmia and should not be managed with the hemorrhage-first TCA algorithm.

5 · Head, Spine, Abdominal & Pelvic Trauma

TBI management, C-spine clearance decision rules, FAST, and pelvic hemorrhage control.

Traumatic Brain Injury

Assessment

GCSSeverity
13–15Mild
9–12Moderate
≤8Severe — secure the airway

Signs of herniation: unilateral (or bilateral) blown pupil, decorticate/decerebrate posturing, Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations), rapidly falling GCS.

Management priorities

  • Avoid hypotension and hypoxia — both independently worsen secondary brain injury; a single episode of SBP <90 is associated with increased mortality in TBI.
  • Maintain adequate cerebral perfusion pressure — permissive hypotension strategies used elsewhere in trauma are not applied when significant TBI is present.
  • Elevate head of bed ~30° if C-spine cleared/stabilized and no contraindication.
  • Avoid routine prophylactic hyperventilation — reserve brief hyperventilation (target PaCO2 ~30–35) as a temporizing bridge specifically for acute herniation, not baseline management.
  • Hyperosmolar therapy for suspected herniation/elevated ICP: mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg IV bolus, or hypertonic saline (e.g., 3% NS bolus, dosed per protocol) — choice often depends on volume status (hypertonic saline favored if hypotensive, since mannitol is an osmotic diuretic).
  • Reverse anticoagulation urgently in TBI on blood thinners — this is one of the highest-yield, most time-sensitive interventions in geriatric trauma.

Verify specific hyperosmolar dosing and ICP-monitoring thresholds against current Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines and institutional neurosurgical protocol.

C-Spine Clearance

Decision rules

NEXUS criteria (all 5 must be met to clear clinically)

  • No posterior midline cervical tenderness
  • No focal neurologic deficit
  • Normal alertness
  • No intoxication
  • No distracting injury

Canadian C-Spine Rule

Generally more sensitive in appropriately selected populations; sequentially assesses high-risk factors mandating imaging (age ≥65, dangerous mechanism, paresthesias in extremities), then low-risk factors permitting safe range-of-motion assessment (simple rear-end MVC, sitting position in ED, ambulatory at any time, delayed onset of neck pain, absence of midline tenderness), then active range of motion (able to rotate neck 45° left and right).

Not intended for GCS <15, unstable vitals, age <16, acute paralysis, known vertebral disease, or prior C-spine surgery.

Abdominal Trauma & EFAST

Four core EFAST windows: subxiphoid, RUQ, LUQ, suprapubic (extended: bilateral thoracic)

Clinical correlation

  • FAST specificity for free fluid is high (often quoted >99% in some series), but sensitivity is imperfect — a negative FAST does not exclude intra-abdominal injury, especially retroperitoneal or hollow viscus injury.
  • Seatbelt sign (abdominal wall ecchymosis in a belt pattern) is an independent marker for increased risk of hollow viscus and mesenteric injury even with an initially benign exam/negative FAST — keep a lower threshold for serial exams/CT.
  • Hemodynamically unstable patient + positive FAST → goes to the OR, not the CT scanner.
  • Hemodynamically stable patient with concerning mechanism/exam → CT abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast remains the diagnostic standard even with a negative FAST.

Pelvic Trauma

Pelvic binder placement at the greater trochanters, not the iliac crests

Why the binder position matters

  • A binder placed too high (over the iliac crests, the intuitive "belt" position) fails to close an open-book pelvic ring effectively — the greater trochanter level is where circumferential compression actually reduces pelvic volume and re-approximates the fracture.
  • Pelvic hemorrhage is frequently from the presacral venous plexus rather than a single arterial source — reducing pelvic volume itself is therapeutic, independent of any single vessel.
  • Unstable pelvic fracture with continued hemodynamic instability despite binder + transfusion → angioembolization (if arterial blush on CT) and/or REBOA Zone III as a bridge, with definitive orthopedic/surgical fixation to follow.

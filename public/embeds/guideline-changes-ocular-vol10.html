Guideline Changes Atlas Ocular · Vol. 10 — RAO as Stroke
What Changed · February 2025

Retinal artery occlusion is now formally an "eye stroke"

The AAO's updated Retinal and Ophthalmic Artery Occlusions Preferred Practice Pattern aligns with the AHA's 2021 scientific statement to reframe acute CRAO, BRAO, and ophthalmic artery occlusion as stroke equivalents — with a same-day referral expectation to the nearest stroke center. Real-world data published the same year shows most EDs aren't there yet.

02 · Deep Dive

Why RAO is being treated as a stroke now

Retinal and Ophthalmic Artery Occlusions PPP Updated, Feb 2025

Central retinal artery occlusion, branch retinal artery occlusion, cilioretinal artery occlusion, and ophthalmic artery occlusion can all signal life-threatening underlying disease — carotid occlusive disease or cardiac valve pathology chief among them. The updated PPP calls for immediate referral to the nearest stroke referral center for prompt assessment for acute intervention in any patient with acute, symptomatic retinal or ophthalmic arterial occlusion. This mirrors how a limb-threatening embolic event or an acute ischemic stroke elsewhere in the body would be triaged — the retina is, functionally, an extension of the central nervous system.

The rationale is time-sensitive: patients with symptomatic RAO carry their highest risk of stroke in the two weeks before and up to one month after the occlusion — a concurrent-risk window, not a distant future risk, which is why the same-day stroke-center pathway matters rather than a routine outpatient ophthalmology referral.

03 · Deep Dive · Safety-Critical

The guideline exists — the practice gap is the real story

National ED trends, 2016–2021 Practice gap identified

2x
Increase in CRAO ED presentation rate over the study period
>50%
CRAO patients receiving no stroke workup imaging (CT/CTA or MRI/MRA) at all

A National Emergency Department Sample analysis of adult CRAO presentations from 2016–2021 found stroke-workup imaging utilization rising over time, but still landing short: more than half of CRAO patients received no brain imaging for stroke workup despite existing AAO/AHA guidance predating even this update. This is the clearest evidence that the "reframe RAO as stroke" message from the guideline hasn't fully reached ED practice yet.

⚠ Practical implication

A patient with sudden painless monocular vision loss and a cherry-red spot or diffuse retinal whitening on exam should trigger the same urgency as any other acute stroke code — noncontrast head CT, stroke team involvement, and consideration of vascular imaging — not a "follow up with ophthalmology this week" plan.

04 · Deep Dive

Don't skip the GCA screen in patients over 50

In any RAO patient over age 50, giant cell arteritis should be actively suspected. When GCA is strongly suspected or confirmed, urgent systemic corticosteroid therapy should be considered — both to preserve vision in the contralateral eye and to prevent broader systemic morbidity and mortality from untreated GCA (aortic and other large-vessel complications). This isn't a new concept, but the updated PPP reinforces it as a parallel, time-critical track alongside the stroke workup, not a sequential afterthought.

05 · Evidence Watch

Thrombolysis for CRAO: evidence accumulating, not yet standard

⚡ Genuinely unsettled — flagged, not resolved

IV thrombolysis for CRAO remains outside routine standard of care in most US EDs, but the evidence base is actively growing: an individual-participant-data meta-analysis (Xie et al., Stroke, August 2025) examined early thrombolysis and outcomes in CRAO, and a prospective randomized phase 3 trial (TenCRAOS) is currently comparing tenecteplase against aspirin. Neither has yet translated into a guideline-level recommendation to treat CRAO with thrombolytics the way an acute ischemic stroke would be treated. This is a space to watch, not a current standard to adopt.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Treat acute symptomatic RAO as a stroke code, not a routine ophthalmology consult — immediate referral to your nearest stroke center pathway.
  • Don't skip brain imaging for stroke workup in CRAO — national data shows this is happening in more than half of cases, and it shouldn't be.
  • In any RAO patient over 50, actively screen for GCA symptoms (jaw claudication, temporal tenderness, scalp tenderness, new headache) and don't hesitate on urgent steroids if suspicion is real.
  • Don't reach for thrombolytics in CRAO based on this update — the evidence is accumulating but hasn't crossed into a standard-of-care recommendation.

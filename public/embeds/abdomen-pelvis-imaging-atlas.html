Free Air / Pneumoperitoneum

5 findings

1. Pneumoperitoneum, large volume

Concerning

Free intraperitoneal gas from a perforated hollow viscus.

Imaging Characteristics

Air seen anterior to the liver on supine images, under the diaphragm, or outlining bowel loops from both sides (Rigler sign); lung-window settings improve detection.

Differential
  • Perforated peptic ulcer
  • Perforated diverticulitis
  • Perforated appendicitis
  • Bowel perforation from ischemia/obstruction
  • Iatrogenic (recent laparotomy/laparoscopy, expected up to ~7-10 days)
Next Step

Surgical consultation; correlate with recent surgical history before assuming pathologic.

2. Pneumoperitoneum, small volume / trace

Concerning

Small collections of free air that are easy to miss but still signal perforation.

Imaging Characteristics

Tiny locules of gas adjacent to bowel wall, in the falciform ligament, or in the lesser sac; best seen on lung windows and thin slices.

Differential
  • Microperforation (appendiceal, diverticular)
  • Recent endoscopy/paracentesis
  • Peritoneal dialysis catheter
Next Step

Do not dismiss as artifact; correlate clinically and consider surgical evaluation.

3. Falciform ligament sign

Concerning

Free air outlining the falciform ligament, a classic but subtle pneumoperitoneum sign.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear soft-tissue density (the ligament) sharply outlined by gas on both sides in the right upper quadrant.

Differential
  • Perforated viscus
  • Post-surgical air
Next Step

Treat as pneumoperitoneum until proven otherwise; correlate with history.

4. Pneumatosis intestinalis

Variable

Gas within the bowel wall itself, ranging from benign to life-threatening depending on cause.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear or bubbly lucencies tracking within the bowel wall on soft-tissue and lung windows; may extend into mesenteric or portal venous gas.

Differential
  • Benign: COPD, steroids, immunosuppression, post-endoscopy, connective tissue disease
  • Concerning: bowel ischemia/infarction, especially with portal venous gas or pneumatosis plus lactic acidosis
Next Step

Correlate with lactate, exam, and clinical trajectory; portal venous gas plus pneumatosis is a surgical emergency until excluded.

5. Retropneumoperitoneum

Concerning

Free air confined to the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneal cavity.

Imaging Characteristics

Gas outlining the kidneys, psoas muscles, or duodenal sweep without free intraperitoneal air.

Differential
  • Perforated duodenum (retroperitoneal portion)
  • Perforated ascending/descending colon
  • Rectal perforation
Next Step

Look specifically at the duodenum and colon; surgical consult regardless of free intraperitoneal air status.

Free Fluid / Hemoperitoneum

5 findings

1. Simple free fluid, small volume

Variable

Small physiologic or reactive fluid collection, common and frequently incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin rim of low-attenuation (near-water density, 0-15 HU) fluid in the pelvis (rectouterine or rectovesical pouch) or Morison pouch.

Differential
  • Physiologic (mid-cycle ovulation)
  • Reactive to any inflammatory process
  • Early ascites
Next Step

Usually no action beyond correlating with the suspected primary process.

2. Ascites, moderate-to-large volume

Variable

Larger free fluid collections layering throughout the abdomen and pelvis.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid outlining bowel loops, in bilateral paracolic gutters and pelvis; simple ascites is water-attenuation, complex ascites shows septations or higher attenuation.

Differential
  • Cirrhosis/portal hypertension
  • Heart failure
  • Malignancy/carcinomatosis
  • Nephrotic syndrome
  • Infection (spontaneous bacterial peritonitis)
Next Step

Correlate with liver disease history; consider diagnostic paracentesis if new or infection suspected.

3. Hemoperitoneum, high-attenuation free fluid

Concerning

Free intraperitoneal blood, often from solid organ injury or ruptured pathology.

Imaging Characteristics

Fluid attenuation 30-45 HU (higher than simple fluid); a 'sentinel clot' (highest attenuation, 45-70 HU) localizes the bleeding source.

Differential
  • Traumatic solid organ injury (spleen, liver)
  • Ruptured ectopic pregnancy
  • Ruptured ovarian cyst
  • Ruptured hepatocellular adenoma/HCC
  • Anticoagulant-related spontaneous hemorrhage
Next Step

Hemodynamic assessment first; surgical/IR consultation for active extravasation or instability.

4. Active contrast extravasation ('blush')

Concerning

Active arterial bleeding seen as contrast leaking into a hematoma or free space on the arterial phase.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of hyperdensity matching arterial blood pool attenuation within a hematoma, often changing in size/shape between arterial and delayed phases.

Differential
  • Traumatic vascular injury
  • Bleeding tumor
  • Aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm rupture
  • Post-procedural bleeding
Next Step

Emergent surgical or interventional radiology consultation; do not delay for further imaging if unstable.

5. Loculated / complex fluid collection

Concerning

Fluid collection with internal septations, debris, or a defined wall suggesting abscess or organized hematoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, thick-walled collection, sometimes with internal gas locules; distinct from simple free-flowing ascites.

Differential
  • Abscess (post-surgical, appendiceal, diverticular)
  • Organized/infected hematoma
  • Urinoma/biloma
  • Loculated malignant effusion
Next Step

Correlate with clinical picture; percutaneous drainage often indicated if infected.

Bowel Obstruction & Motility

10 findings

1. Small bowel obstruction (SBO), simple

Concerning

Mechanical blockage of the small bowel without evidence of vascular compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated small bowel loops (>2.5-3 cm) with air-fluid levels proximal to a transition point, and decompressed/collapsed bowel distally.

Differential
  • Adhesions (most common cause overall)
  • Hernia (internal or external)
  • Malignancy
  • Intussusception
  • Bezoar
  • Crohn stricture
Next Step

Identify the transition point and look for signs of strangulation; surgical/medicine correlation for trial of NG decompression vs. operative management.

2. Small bowel obstruction with closed-loop configuration

Concerning

A segment of bowel obstructed at two adjacent points, at high risk for rapid strangulation.

Imaging Characteristics

U-shaped or C-shaped fixed dilated loop with radially converging mesenteric vessels toward a torsion point; often has a 'whirl sign.'

Differential
  • Adhesive band
  • Internal hernia
  • Volvulus
Next Step

Time-sensitive surgical emergency; expedite surgical consultation.

3. Small bowel obstruction with strangulation

Concerning

SBO complicated by bowel ischemia from vascular compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel wall thickening, reduced or absent mural enhancement, mesenteric fat stranding/edema, pneumatosis, or portal venous gas in the setting of obstruction.

Differential
  • Closed-loop obstruction
  • Volvulus
  • Incarcerated hernia
Next Step

Surgical emergency; do not delay operative management for additional imaging.

4. Large bowel obstruction

Concerning

Mechanical blockage of the colon, often from an obstructing mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Colonic dilation (>6 cm, cecum >9 cm) proximal to a transition point; competent ileocecal valve causes progressive cecal distension and perforation risk.

Differential
  • Colorectal malignancy (most common)
  • Diverticular stricture
  • Volvulus
  • Hernia
  • Fecal impaction
Next Step

Assess cecal diameter for perforation risk; surgical/GI consultation.

5. Sigmoid volvulus

Concerning

Torsion of a redundant sigmoid colon around its mesenteric axis.

Imaging Characteristics

Massively dilated, ahaustral loop of sigmoid colon extending from the pelvis toward the right upper quadrant with a 'coffee bean' or 'whirl' sign at the mesenteric twist.

Differential
  • Cecal volvulus
  • Large bowel obstruction from mass
  • Toxic megacolon
Next Step

Emergent surgical/GI consultation; endoscopic detorsion may be attempted if no ischemia/perforation.

6. Cecal volvulus

Concerning

Torsion or upward folding of a mobile cecum, typically due to incomplete peritoneal fixation.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly dilated, often ectopic cecum (displaced to mid or left abdomen) with a whirl sign of the mesentery; small bowel is often also dilated.

Differential
  • Sigmoid volvulus
  • Cecal bascule
  • Ileocecal intussusception
Next Step

Usually requires surgery (detorsion rarely durable endoscopically); surgical consultation.

7. Intussusception, adult

Concerning

Telescoping of one bowel segment into another; in adults, usually has an identifiable lead point.

Imaging Characteristics

'Target' or 'sausage-shaped' mass with layered bowel-within-bowel and mesenteric fat/vessels pulled into the lumen.

Differential
  • Lipoma or polyp lead point
  • Malignancy (especially in colonic intussusception)
  • Meckel diverticulum
  • Post-surgical/idiopathic (small transient small-bowel-small-bowel intussusception is often incidental)
Next Step

Short-segment small-bowel intussusception without obstruction is often incidental; ileocolic or colocolic intussusception warrants surgical/GI workup for a lead point.

8. Gallstone ileus

Concerning

Mechanical SBO caused by a gallstone that has eroded through a cholecystoenteric fistula.

Imaging Characteristics

Rigler triad: pneumobilia, ectopic gallstone (often in the terminal ileum), and small bowel obstruction.

Differential
  • Other causes of SBO
  • Bezoar
Next Step

Surgical consultation; stone extraction with or without fistula management.

9. Adynamic (paralytic) ileus

Variable

Diffuse bowel hypomotility without a mechanical obstructing point.

Imaging Characteristics

Generalized dilation of small and large bowel with air-fluid levels but no discrete transition point; gas often present throughout the colon and rectum.

Differential
  • Post-operative state
  • Electrolyte disturbance (hypokalemia)
  • Opioids/anticholinergics
  • Intra-abdominal inflammation (pancreatitis, peritonitis)
  • Retroperitoneal process (hematoma, spine fracture)
Next Step

Treat the underlying cause; correlate with electrolytes and recent surgery/medications.

10. Bezoar

Variable

Intraluminal mass of accumulated indigestible material, most often in the stomach or at a transition point causing obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined intraluminal mass with mottled, gas-containing 'Swiss cheese' or speckled appearance, without wall enhancement.

Differential
  • Retained food/fiber (phytobezoar)
  • Hair (trichobezoar)
  • Medication concretion
Next Step

Correlate with history (prior gastric surgery, psychiatric history); endoscopic or surgical removal if obstructing.

Appendix

6 findings

1. Acute uncomplicated appendicitis

Concerning

Inflammation of the appendix without perforation or abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated appendix >6-7 mm in diameter with wall thickening/hyperenhancement, periappendiceal fat stranding; may contain an appendicolith.

Differential
  • Epiploic appendagitis (less inflammation, more localized fat stranding)
  • Mesenteric adenitis
  • Cecal diverticulitis
  • Right-sided colitis
Next Step

Surgical consultation; antibiotics-first vs. appendectomy per shared decision-making and institutional protocol.

2. Appendicolith

Variable

Calcified fecalith within the appendiceal lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded or ovoid calcific density within the appendix, with or without surrounding inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Incidental appendicolith without appendicitis (occurs in a minority)
  • Marker of impending or current appendicitis
Next Step

In the absence of appendiceal wall thickening or fat stranding, an isolated appendicolith is often followed clinically rather than treated.

3. Perforated appendicitis with abscess

Concerning

Appendiceal rupture with a contained, organized fluid/pus collection.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection adjacent to the appendix, often containing gas, with surrounding inflammatory stranding; the appendix itself may not be well visualized.

Differential
  • Complicated diverticulitis abscess
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess (if periadnexal)
  • Crohn-related phlegmon/abscess
Next Step

Interventional radiology drainage plus antibiotics with interval appendectomy, or emergent surgery depending on size/stability.

4. Perforated appendicitis with free perforation

Concerning

Appendiceal rupture causing diffuse peritoneal contamination rather than a contained abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal appendiceal wall, extraluminal appendicolith, diffuse free fluid/air, and diffuse peritoneal fat stranding.

Differential
  • Other causes of perforated viscus
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation.

5. Appendiceal mucocele

Variable

Cystic dilation of the appendix from mucus accumulation, which can be benign or a marker of neoplasm.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, cystic, low-attenuation tubular structure in the expected appendiceal location, often with a thin, sometimes calcified wall; may show a characteristic 'onion-skin' layering.

Differential
  • Mucocele from mucosal hyperplasia (benign)
  • Mucinous cystadenoma
  • Low-grade appendiceal mucinous neoplasm (LAMN)
  • Mucinous cystadenocarcinoma
Next Step

Surgical referral; avoid preoperative biopsy/aspiration given risk of pseudomyxoma peritonei if malignant.

6. Normal appendix / appendix not clearly visualized

Benign

A normal-caliber appendix or one that cannot be confidently identified on CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Appendix, when seen, measures ≤6 mm with no wall thickening or stranding; nonvisualization is common and does not reliably exclude appendicitis in a low-probability patient.

Differential
  • True normal appendix
  • Appendicitis with a nonvisualized/atypically located appendix
Next Step

Correlate with clinical probability; consider ultrasound (especially in children/pregnancy) or clinical observation if suspicion remains.

Diverticular Disease

6 findings

1. Diverticulosis (no inflammation)

Benign

Outpouchings of colonic mucosa through the muscular wall, extremely common and usually asymptomatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small outpouchings from the colonic wall, most often sigmoid, without adjacent fat stranding or wall thickening.

Differential
  • Incidental finding in the majority of older adults
Next Step

No acute action; routine health maintenance/colonoscopy per age-appropriate screening.

2. Uncomplicated acute diverticulitis

Concerning

Inflammation of one or more diverticula without abscess, perforation, or fistula.

Imaging Characteristics

Segmental colonic wall thickening with pericolonic fat stranding centered on diverticula; no discrete abscess or free air.

Differential
  • Colitis (infectious, ischemic, inflammatory)
  • Epiploic appendagitis
  • Colonic malignancy with adjacent inflammation
Next Step

Outpatient antibiotics (selected cases) or admission per severity and comorbidities; colonoscopy 6-8 weeks after resolution to exclude malignancy.

3. Complicated diverticulitis with abscess

Concerning

Diverticulitis with a contained pericolonic or pelvic abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection adjacent to the inflamed colonic segment, often with internal gas.

Differential
  • Perforated colon cancer
  • Crohn-related abscess
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
Next Step

IR drainage for abscesses generally >3-4 cm plus antibiotics; smaller collections may be treated medically.

4. Diverticulitis with free perforation

Concerning

Diverticular perforation causing diffuse peritoneal contamination (Hinchey III/IV physiology).

Imaging Characteristics

Free intraperitoneal air and/or diffuse free fluid with extensive pericolonic stranding, sometimes frank fecal spillage.

Differential
  • Other causes of perforated viscus
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation; often requires resection with diversion (Hartmann procedure).

5. Diverticular fistula

Concerning

Abnormal communication between the inflamed colon and an adjacent structure, most often the bladder.

Imaging Characteristics

Colovesical: air within an otherwise unexplained bladder in the absence of instrumentation, with a thickened colonic-bladder interface; colovaginal and enterocolic fistulas also occur.

Differential
  • Malignancy-related fistula
  • Crohn disease fistula
  • Radiation-related fistula
Next Step

Surgical referral for elective resection; correlate with recurrent UTI/pneumaturia history for colovesical fistula.

6. Giant colonic diverticulum

Benign

Rare, markedly enlarged single diverticulum (>4 cm), usually sigmoid.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, gas- or fluid-filled cystic structure contiguous with the colon, distinct from adjacent bowel loops.

Differential
  • Colonic duplication cyst
  • Abscess
  • Volvulus of the diverticulum itself
Next Step

Elective surgical resection is often recommended given risk of perforation.

Bowel Wall / Colitis / Ischemia

8 findings

1. Infectious colitis

Variable

Colonic wall inflammation from bacterial, viral, or parasitic infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential, often long-segment colonic wall thickening with mucosal hyperenhancement and pericolonic fat stranding; ascending colon predominance is common with bacterial causes.

Differential
  • C. difficile colitis
  • Bacterial colitis (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, E. coli)
  • Viral colitis (CMV in immunocompromised)
  • Inflammatory bowel disease flare
Next Step

Correlate with stool studies; supportive care and targeted antibiotics as indicated.

2. Clostridioides difficile colitis

Concerning

Toxin-mediated colitis, often after antibiotic exposure, ranging from mild to fulminant.

Imaging Characteristics

Marked, often pancolonic wall thickening with an 'accordion sign' (thickened haustra trapping oral contrast) and target sign; severe cases show low-attenuation submucosal edema.

Differential
  • Other infectious colitis
  • Ischemic colitis
  • IBD flare
Next Step

Correlate with antibiotic exposure and stool toxin/PCR testing; watch for toxic megacolon in severe disease.

3. Toxic megacolon

Concerning

Severe colonic dilation with systemic toxicity, a complication of severe colitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Colonic diameter >6 cm (often transverse colon) with loss of haustration, mucosal islands, and wall thinning; may progress to perforation.

Differential
  • Fulminant C. difficile colitis
  • Fulminant ulcerative colitis
  • Ischemic colitis
Next Step

Surgical and GI emergency; close monitoring for perforation.

4. Ischemic colitis

Concerning

Colonic wall injury from reduced blood flow, classically affecting watershed areas.

Imaging Characteristics

Segmental wall thickening, often at the splenic flexure or rectosigmoid junction, with submucosal edema ('thumbprinting'); may show pneumatosis or reduced enhancement if advanced.

Differential
  • Infectious colitis
  • IBD flare
  • Diverticulitis
Next Step

Correlate with hemodynamics/hypotension history; surgical consultation if transmural signs (pneumatosis, portal venous gas, lack of enhancement).

5. Acute mesenteric ischemia (bowel)

Concerning

Small bowel ischemia from arterial, venous, or nonocclusive hypoperfusion, a surgical emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel wall thinning (not thickening) with poor or absent enhancement in early ischemia; later stages show thickening, pneumatosis, mesenteric/portal venous gas, and mesenteric fat stranding.

Differential
  • Arterial embolism/thrombosis (SMA)
  • Mesenteric venous thrombosis
  • Nonocclusive mesenteric ischemia (low-flow states)
  • Strangulated obstruction
Next Step

Time-critical; immediate surgical and/or interventional radiology consultation.

6. Inflammatory bowel disease - Crohn disease (active)

Variable

Chronic transmural inflammatory disease with skip lesions, most often terminal ileum and colon.

Imaging Characteristics

Segmental, asymmetric bowel wall thickening with mural stratification ('target sign'), engorged vasa recta ('comb sign'), and mesenteric fat proliferation ('creeping fat').

Differential
  • Infectious ileitis (Yersinia)
  • Lymphoma
  • Ischemia
  • Tuberculosis (in endemic areas)
Next Step

Correlate with known IBD history; GI consultation for flare management and to assess for stricture/fistula/abscess.

7. Inflammatory bowel disease - ulcerative colitis (active)

Variable

Chronic mucosal inflammatory disease, continuous and rectum-involving.

Imaging Characteristics

Continuous, circumferential colonic wall thickening beginning at the rectum and extending proximally without skip lesions; loss of haustration in chronic disease ('lead pipe colon').

Differential
  • Infectious colitis
  • Crohn colitis
  • Ischemic colitis
Next Step

GI consultation; assess for toxic megacolon in severe flares.

8. Bowel wall thickening, nonspecific

Variable

Focal or segmental bowel wall thickening without a clearly diagnostic pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Wall thickness >3 mm (small bowel) or >5 mm (colon, distended) without a specific enhancement or distribution pattern to suggest a single etiology.

Differential
  • Underdistended bowel (pseudothickening)
  • Infectious/inflammatory colitis
  • Ischemia
  • Neoplasm
  • Radiation change
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context and consider follow-up imaging or endoscopy if unexplained.

Liver

10 findings

1. Hepatic cyst, simple

Benign

Extremely common benign fluid-filled lesion of the liver.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, round, water-attenuation (0-15 HU) lesion with a thin or imperceptible wall and no enhancement.

Differential
  • Biliary hamartoma (multiple tiny cysts)
  • Polycystic liver disease (if numerous)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic simple cyst.

2. Hepatic hemangioma

Benign

The most common benign liver tumor, composed of vascular channels.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined hypodense lesion on non-contrast/portal venous images showing peripheral nodular discontinuous enhancement with progressive fill-in on delayed phases, matching blood pool.

Differential
  • Hepatocellular carcinoma (atypical enhancement)
  • Hypervascular metastasis
  • Focal nodular hyperplasia
Next Step

Classic small hemangiomas need no follow-up; atypical or large lesions may warrant MRI for confirmation.

3. Hepatic steatosis (fatty liver)

Variable

Fat accumulation within hepatocytes, ranging from benign to a marker of metabolic/liver disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely (or focally/geographically) decreased liver attenuation relative to the spleen, sometimes with vessels appearing relatively hyperdense against the low-attenuation parenchyma.

Differential
  • Simple/nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
  • Alcohol-related liver disease
  • Chemotherapy-related steatosis
  • Focal fatty infiltration or sparing (geographic, non-mass-like)
Next Step

Correlate with metabolic risk factors and liver enzymes; outpatient workup for steatohepatitis/fibrosis if persistent.

4. Cirrhosis

Concerning

Chronic liver disease with fibrosis and architectural distortion, a substrate for portal hypertension and HCC.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular hepatic contour, relative hypertrophy of the caudate/lateral segments with right lobe atrophy, and signs of portal hypertension (splenomegaly, varices, ascites).

Differential
  • Various etiologies (viral hepatitis, alcohol, NASH, autoimmune)
Next Step

Surveillance ultrasound or MRI/CT with AFP per HCC screening guidelines; hepatology referral.

5. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

Concerning

Primary liver malignancy, usually arising in a cirrhotic liver.

Imaging Characteristics

Arterial phase hyperenhancement with washout on portal venous/delayed phases and a pseudocapsule (LI-RADS 5 pattern); may show vascular invasion (portal vein thrombus).

Differential
  • Hemangioma
  • Focal nodular hyperplasia
  • Dysplastic nodule
  • Hypervascular metastasis
Next Step

Hepatology/oncology referral; dedicated contrast-enhanced MRI or CT with LI-RADS categorization.

6. Hepatic metastases

Concerning

Secondary malignant deposits in the liver, the most common hepatic malignancy overall.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple lesions of variable size, often hypodense on portal venous phase; hypervascular primaries (neuroendocrine, renal cell, thyroid) may show arterial hyperenhancement.

Differential
  • Multifocal HCC
  • Abscesses
  • Biliary hamartomas (if tiny and numerous)
Next Step

Correlate with known primary malignancy; oncology referral for staging.

7. Pyogenic hepatic abscess

Concerning

Bacterial infection forming a liver abscess, often from biliary or portal venous seeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, often complex/loculated fluid collection with surrounding edema; may contain gas ('cluster sign' with multiple adjacent small abscesses).

Differential
  • Necrotic metastasis or HCC
  • Amebic abscess
  • Infected cyst/biloma
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics; blood cultures and search for a biliary/portal source.

8. Focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH)

Benign

Benign hyperplastic liver lesion, more common in women, not a true neoplasm.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous arterial hyperenhancement with a central non-enhancing scar that becomes hyperdense on delayed phases; isodense to liver on portal venous phase.

Differential
  • Hepatic adenoma
  • Fibrolamellar HCC (also has a central scar but typically non-enhancing on delay)
  • Hypervascular metastasis
Next Step

No treatment needed for classic FNH; MRI with hepatobiliary contrast can confirm if atypical.

9. Hepatic adenoma

Variable

Benign liver tumor associated with oral contraceptive use and anabolic steroids, with risk of bleeding and (rarely) malignant transformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, often heterogeneous mass with arterial hyperenhancement that does not persist (unlike FNH); may show fat or hemorrhage.

Differential
  • FNH
  • Well-differentiated HCC
  • Hypervascular metastasis
Next Step

Referral for consideration of discontinuing hormonal therapy and resection if large (>5 cm) or in males (higher malignant potential).

10. Hepatic laceration (traumatic)

Concerning

Parenchymal liver injury from blunt or penetrating trauma, graded by AAST scale.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or branching low-attenuation defect within the parenchyma; graded by depth, involvement of vasculature, and associated hemoperitoneum/active bleeding.

Differential
  • Laceration vs. contusion vs. active hemorrhage vs. vascular injury (each changes management)
Next Step

Grade injury, assess for active extravasation, and correlate with hemodynamic status for operative vs. angioembolization vs. observation.

Gallbladder & Biliary Tree

8 findings

1. Cholelithiasis

Variable

Gallstones within the gallbladder lumen; CT is less sensitive than ultrasound for detecting them.

Imaging Characteristics

Round or faceted densities within the gallbladder, variably calcified (some stones are isodense to bile and occult on CT).

Differential
  • Symptomatic vs. asymptomatic cholelithiasis
  • Gallbladder polyp (non-mobile, non-dependent)
  • Adenomyomatosis
Next Step

Ultrasound is the preferred confirmatory study; surgical referral if symptomatic.

2. Acute cholecystitis

Concerning

Gallbladder inflammation, most often from cystic duct obstruction by a stone.

Imaging Characteristics

Gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fat stranding and/or fluid, gallbladder distension, and often a visible obstructing stone.

Differential
  • Gallbladder wall thickening from other causes (hepatitis, hypoalbuminemia, heart failure, ascites)
  • Adenomyomatosis
Next Step

Surgical consultation for cholecystectomy; percutaneous cholecystostomy if poor surgical candidate.

3. Gangrenous / emphysematous cholecystitis

Concerning

Severe, complicated cholecystitis with wall necrosis or gas-forming infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular or absent gallbladder wall enhancement, intramural gas (emphysematous), membranes sloughing into the lumen, or frank perforation.

Differential
  • Uncomplicated acute cholecystitis
Next Step

Surgical emergency; higher mortality than simple cholecystitis, especially in diabetics.

4. Choledocholithiasis

Concerning

Gallstone within the common bile duct.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded filling density within a dilated common bile duct, sometimes with a 'target' appearance on axial images.

Differential
  • Sludge/debris
  • Ampullary or bile duct tumor causing obstruction
Next Step

MRCP or ERCP for confirmation and stone extraction; correlate with liver enzymes.

5. Biliary ductal dilation

Concerning

Widening of the intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic bile ducts, indicating downstream obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Common bile duct >6-8 mm (higher normal threshold post-cholecystectomy or with age); intrahepatic ducts visible extending to the liver periphery when dilated.

Differential
  • Choledocholithiasis
  • Pancreatic head mass
  • Cholangiocarcinoma
  • Ampullary tumor
  • Post-cholecystectomy/benign stricture
Next Step

MRCP to characterize the level and cause of obstruction.

6. Cholangiocarcinoma

Concerning

Malignancy arising from the bile duct epithelium, intrahepatic or extrahepatic (including hilar/Klatskin tumors).

Imaging Characteristics

Intrahepatic mass with delayed peripheral enhancement and capsular retraction, or an infiltrative stricture causing ductal dilation proximal to the lesion.

Differential
  • Hepatic metastasis
  • Benign biliary stricture
  • HCC (intrahepatic form)
Next Step

Oncology/hepatobiliary surgery referral; MRCP and tissue sampling for staging.

7. Gallbladder wall thickening, reactive/non-cholecystitis

Benign

Diffuse gallbladder wall thickening from a systemic rather than primary gallbladder process.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, often circumferential wall thickening without pericholecystic stranding or a sonographic Murphy sign equivalent.

Differential
  • Hypoalbuminemia
  • Heart failure/volume overload
  • Hepatitis
  • Ascites
Next Step

Correlate with albumin, liver function, and volume status rather than assuming cholecystitis.

8. Porcelain gallbladder

Variable

Extensive calcification of the gallbladder wall from chronic cholecystitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear or complete calcific rim outlining the gallbladder wall.

Differential
  • Chronic cholelithiasis/cholecystitis
Next Step

Historically associated with gallbladder cancer risk (more recent data suggests lower risk than once thought); surgical referral for consideration of cholecystectomy.

Pancreas

8 findings

1. Acute interstitial edematous pancreatitis

Concerning

Inflammation of the pancreas without necrosis, the milder and more common form.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or focal pancreatic enlargement with peripancreatic fat stranding and normal, homogeneous parenchymal enhancement.

Differential
  • Peripancreatic fat stranding from an adjacent process (e.g., perforated ulcer)
Next Step

Supportive care (fluids, analgesia); identify etiology (gallstones, alcohol, triglycerides, medications).

2. Necrotizing pancreatitis

Concerning

Severe pancreatitis with non-viable pancreatic and/or peripancreatic tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse lack of pancreatic parenchymal enhancement; best assessed after 72 hours as necrosis may not be apparent early.

Differential
  • Interstitial edematous pancreatitis (no necrosis)
Next Step

Higher risk of infection and complications; serial imaging and possible IR/surgical intervention if infected necrosis develops.

3. Pancreatic pseudocyst

Variable

Encapsulated peripancreatic fluid collection, a late complication of pancreatitis (>4 weeks).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, round/oval fluid collection with a mature, non-epithelialized wall, lacking significant internal debris.

Differential
  • Acute peripancreatic fluid collection (earlier, less organized)
  • Walled-off necrosis (contains solid debris)
  • Cystic pancreatic neoplasm
Next Step

Observation if asymptomatic; drainage if infected, symptomatic, or enlarging.

4. Walled-off pancreatic necrosis

Concerning

Mature, encapsulated collection containing necrotic solid debris, a late complication of necrotizing pancreatitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous collection with a defined wall containing both fluid and non-liquefied necrotic material, unlike the simple fluid of a pseudocyst.

Differential
  • Pseudocyst (should be uniformly fluid)
  • Cystic neoplasm with debris
Next Step

Drainage (endoscopic or percutaneous) if infected or symptomatic; multidisciplinary GI/surgery management.

5. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma

Concerning

The most common pancreatic malignancy, often presenting late given nonspecific early symptoms.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, hypoenhancing mass (best seen on pancreatic parenchymal phase) often causing upstream pancreatic and/or biliary ductal dilation ('double duct sign') and encasing peripancreatic vessels.

Differential
  • Focal (mass-forming) pancreatitis
  • Autoimmune pancreatitis
  • Neuroendocrine tumor (usually hyperenhancing, distinguishing feature)
Next Step

Oncology/surgery referral for resectability staging; CA 19-9 and dedicated pancreatic protocol CT/MRI.

6. Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor

Variable

Tumor arising from pancreatic islet cells, which may be functional (hormone-secreting) or non-functional.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, hypervascular mass showing arterial phase hyperenhancement, distinguishing it from the hypovascular ductal adenocarcinoma.

Differential
  • Hypervascular metastasis to pancreas
  • Accessory spleen (if at the tail)
  • Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm
Next Step

Endocrine/oncology referral; correlate with functional hormone symptoms (hypoglycemia, flushing) if present.

7. Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN)

Variable

Mucin-producing cystic pancreatic tumor with malignant potential, arising from the main duct, branch ducts, or both.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic, often multilocular ('cluster of grapes') lesion communicating with a dilated pancreatic duct; main-duct involvement carries higher malignant risk.

Differential
  • Side-branch IPMN (lower risk)
  • Main-duct IPMN (higher risk)
  • Mucinous cystic neoplasm (does not communicate with the duct)
  • Serous cystadenoma
Next Step

GI/surgery referral; features suggesting high risk (main duct >10 mm, enhancing mural nodule, obstructive jaundice) warrant resection consideration per current consensus guidelines.

8. Pancreatic pseudoaneurysm

Concerning

Vascular complication of pancreatitis from enzymatic erosion into an adjacent artery (often splenic).

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded, arterially enhancing structure adjacent to or within pancreatitis-related inflammation/pseudocyst, matching blood pool attenuation.

Differential
  • True aneurysm
  • Hypervascular mass
Next Step

High rupture risk; urgent interventional radiology consultation for embolization.

Spleen

6 findings

1. Splenomegaly

Variable

Enlarged spleen (>12-13 cm craniocaudal), a nonspecific finding with a broad differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Splenic length exceeding normal range for the patient's size; can be measured but is often assessed qualitatively (extending below costal margin, crowding adjacent organs).

Differential
  • Portal hypertension/cirrhosis
  • Hematologic malignancy (lymphoma, leukemia)
  • Infection (mononucleosis, malaria)
  • Congestive states
  • Myeloproliferative disorders
Next Step

Correlate with CBC, liver disease history, and infectious symptoms; avoid contact sports if significantly enlarged given rupture risk.

2. Splenic laceration (traumatic)

Concerning

Parenchymal splenic injury from blunt or penetrating trauma, the most commonly injured solid organ in blunt trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear/branching low-attenuation defect in the parenchyma, graded by depth and vascular/capsular involvement (AAST grading); look for active extravasation and associated hemoperitoneum.

Differential
  • Laceration vs. contusion vs. active hemorrhage vs. vascular injury/pseudoaneurysm
Next Step

Grade injury; hemodynamically stable patients may be managed non-operatively with close monitoring, unstable patients need emergent surgery.

3. Splenic infarct

Variable

Area of ischemic splenic tissue, from embolic or thrombotic occlusion of splenic arterial branches.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral, wedge-shaped area of hypoenhancement extending to the capsule, with the base at the splenic surface.

Differential
  • Atrial fibrillation-related embolism
  • Hypercoagulable states
  • Sickle cell disease (autoinfarction)
  • Pancreatitis-related splenic vein thrombosis
Next Step

Correlate with cardiac rhythm and coagulation workup; usually managed conservatively unless complicated by abscess or rupture.

4. Splenic cyst

Benign

Fluid-filled splenic lesion, either congenital (epidermoid) or post-traumatic (pseudocyst).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, water-attenuation lesion with a thin wall, sometimes with peripheral calcification (more typical of post-traumatic pseudocysts).

Differential
  • Splenic abscess (if wall is thick/irregular)
  • Lymphangioma
  • Hydatid cyst (endemic areas)
Next Step

No follow-up typically needed for a simple cyst; larger cysts may warrant surveillance given rare rupture risk.

5. Splenic abscess

Concerning

Infection within splenic parenchyma, often from hematogenous seeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, hypodense collection, occasionally with internal gas; may be single or multiple (miliary pattern in disseminated infection).

Differential
  • Infarct (abscesses have thicker/irregular enhancing walls and clinical infection signs)
  • Necrotic metastasis (rare)
Next Step

Blood cultures, IR drainage or splenectomy depending on size/multiplicity, and source control.

6. Accessory spleen (splenule)

Benign

Congenital ectopic splenic tissue, a common normal variant.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round nodule near the splenic hilum (most common location) or elsewhere in the abdomen, enhancing identically to the native spleen on all phases.

Differential
  • Lymph node
  • Pancreatic tail mass (if adjacent to pancreas)
Next Step

No action needed; important to recognize so it is not mistaken for pathology, especially in patients who have had splenectomy for hematologic disease.

Kidneys & Ureters

14 findings

1. Nephrolithiasis (renal stone)

Variable

Calculus within the collecting system, extremely common and usually benign unless obstructing.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense focus within the renal collecting system on non-contrast CT (the standard technique for stone protocol); measure size and location.

Differential
  • Non-obstructing stone (incidental)
  • Obstructing stone with hydronephrosis
  • Phleboliths (pelvic, not renal) can mimic distal ureteral stones
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms and degree of obstruction; urology referral for stones unlikely to pass spontaneously (generally >5-6 mm or symptomatic).

2. Obstructing ureteral stone with hydronephrosis

Concerning

Stone causing upstream urinary tract dilation, particularly worrisome with signs of infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Hydronephrosis and hydroureter proximal to a visible stone at the ureterovesical junction, pelvic brim, or ureteropelvic junction (common impaction sites); perinephric stranding suggests acute obstruction.

Differential
  • Uncomplicated obstructing stone
  • Obstructing stone with superimposed infection (urgent)
Next Step

Assess for fever/leukocytosis suggesting infected obstructed system (urologic emergency requiring decompression); otherwise urology follow-up.

3. Infected obstructed kidney (obstructive pyelonephritis)

Concerning

Urinary obstruction with superimposed infection proximal to the blockage, a true urologic emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Hydronephrosis with an obstructing stone (or other cause) in a febrile patient; may show perinephric stranding, gas in severe cases, or a dilated, debris-filled collecting system.

Differential
  • Uncomplicated pyelonephritis (no obstruction)
  • Emphysematous pyelonephritis (gas-forming)
Next Step

Emergent urologic decompression (stent or percutaneous nephrostomy) plus antibiotics; do not delay for stone size assessment.

4. Acute pyelonephritis

Concerning

Renal parenchymal infection, usually ascending from the lower urinary tract.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped or striated areas of decreased enhancement (striated nephrogram) radiating from the papilla to the cortex, with perinephric fat stranding; kidney may be enlarged.

Differential
  • Renal infarct (sharper wedge margins, no perinephric stranding)
  • Renal contusion (if trauma history)
  • Lymphoma infiltration (rare)
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to urine culture; assess for abscess formation if not improving clinically.

5. Renal/perinephric abscess

Concerning

Localized collection of infected fluid within or around the kidney, a complication of pyelonephritis.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, low-attenuation fluid collection within the renal parenchyma or perinephric space, sometimes with internal gas.

Differential
  • Pyelonephritis without abscess
  • Infected renal cyst
  • Necrotic renal mass
Next Step

IR drainage for larger abscesses (generally >3-5 cm) plus antibiotics; urology involvement.

6. Emphysematous pyelonephritis

Concerning

Severe, gas-forming necrotizing renal infection, most common in diabetics.

Imaging Characteristics

Gas within the renal parenchyma, collecting system, or perinephric space, often with parenchymal destruction.

Differential
  • Emphysematous pyelitis (gas confined to collecting system, less severe)
  • Recent instrumentation (benign air)
Next Step

Surgical/urologic emergency; may require nephrectomy in severe cases along with aggressive antibiotics and glycemic control.

7. Simple renal cyst

Benign

Extremely common benign fluid-filled renal lesion (Bosniak I).

Imaging Characteristics

Round, water-attenuation (0-20 HU), thin/imperceptible wall, no septations or enhancement.

Differential
  • Bosniak II-IV cystic lesions (increasing complexity/malignant potential)
  • Hydronephrosis (if connected to collecting system - cysts are not)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic Bosniak I cyst.

8. Complex renal cyst (Bosniak II-III)

Variable

Cystic renal lesion with some complexity (septa, minimal calcification, or thickened walls), with graded malignant risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Bosniak II: thin septa, fine calcification, minimally complex. Bosniak III: thickened irregular walls/septa with measurable enhancement.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic/infected cyst
  • Cystic renal cell carcinoma
Next Step

Bosniak II typically observed; Bosniak III has substantial malignant risk (~50%) and generally warrants urologic referral for resection or biopsy.

9. Renal cell carcinoma

Concerning

The most common primary renal malignancy, often discovered incidentally on cross-sectional imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid, enhancing renal mass (enhancement is the key differentiator from a cyst); may be exophytic, endophytic, and can invade the renal vein/IVC.

Differential
  • Oncocytoma (benign, can mimic RCC - often needs biopsy/resection to distinguish)
  • Angiomyolipoma without visible fat
  • Complex cyst (Bosniak IV)
  • Metastasis to kidney
Next Step

Urology/oncology referral for staging (including renal vein/IVC assessment) and surgical planning.

10. Angiomyolipoma (AML)

Benign

Benign renal tumor composed of fat, smooth muscle, and abnormal vessels.

Imaging Characteristics

Renal mass containing macroscopic fat (negative Hounsfield units, typically less than -10 to -20 HU), which is diagnostic when clearly present.

Differential
  • Fat-poor AML (mimics RCC, may need MRI or biopsy)
  • Renal cell carcinoma with rare intratumoral fat (associated with calcification, unlike AML)
Next Step

Surveillance for growth; larger lesions (>4 cm) or those with large aneurysms carry bleeding risk and may warrant prophylactic embolization.

11. Renal infarct

Concerning

Ischemic renal tissue from arterial occlusion (embolic or thrombotic).

Imaging Characteristics

Sharply marginated, wedge-shaped area of absent enhancement extending to the capsule; a thin rim of preserved cortical enhancement ('cortical rim sign') may be seen from collateral supply.

Differential
  • Pyelonephritis (less sharply marginated, associated stranding)
  • Renal contusion
Next Step

Evaluate for embolic source (atrial fibrillation, endocarditis) and consider anticoagulation/vascular consultation.

12. Renal artery/vein thrombosis

Concerning

Occlusion of the main renal vasculature, causing acute kidney injury or infarction.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the renal artery or vein on contrast-enhanced imaging; venous thrombosis often shows an enlarged kidney with delayed/persistent nephrogram.

Differential
  • Renal artery: embolism, dissection, trauma
  • Renal vein: hypercoagulable state, nephrotic syndrome, tumor thrombus from RCC, dehydration in neonates
Next Step

Vascular/nephrology consultation; anticoagulation typically indicated.

13. Renal trauma (contusion/laceration/shattered kidney)

Concerning

Spectrum of renal parenchymal and collecting system injury after blunt or penetrating trauma, graded by AAST scale.

Imaging Characteristics

Contusion shows ill-defined decreased enhancement; laceration shows a discrete parenchymal defect; higher grades show collecting system injury (urine extravasation on delayed phase) or a shattered, devascularized kidney.

Differential
  • Grade determines management (I-III often conservative, IV-V may need intervention)
Next Step

Delayed phase imaging to assess for urine leak; urology consultation, especially for higher-grade injuries or vascular pedicle injury.

14. Duplicated collecting system

Benign

Common congenital variant with two separate renal pelves/ureters from a single kidney.

Imaging Characteristics

Two distinct renal pelves with either a single ureter (incomplete duplication) or two ureters (complete duplication) draining the kidney.

Differential
  • Normal variant, usually incidental
Next Step

No action needed unless associated with obstruction, reflux, or ureterocele causing symptoms.

Adrenal Glands

5 findings

1. Adrenal adenoma, lipid-rich

Benign

Extremely common benign adrenal nodule, found incidentally in a substantial minority of abdominal CTs.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, well-circumscribed nodule with non-contrast attenuation ≤10 HU, reflecting high intracellular lipid content.

Differential
  • Lipid-poor adenoma (indeterminate on non-contrast, needs washout protocol)
  • Metastasis (in a patient with known malignancy)
Next Step

No further workup needed for a classic lipid-rich adenoma <4 cm without hormonal symptoms.

2. Adrenal adenoma, lipid-poor / indeterminate

Variable

Adenoma without enough intracellular fat to be diagnostic on non-contrast CT alone.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-contrast attenuation >10 HU; requires a dedicated adrenal washout protocol (absolute washout ≥60% or relative washout ≥40% supports adenoma).

Differential
  • True lipid-poor adenoma
  • Metastasis
  • Pheochromocytoma
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma (if large/heterogeneous)
Next Step

Dedicated adrenal CT washout protocol or MRI with chemical shift imaging; correlate with size and interval growth.

3. Adrenal myelolipoma

Benign

Benign tumor composed of fat and myeloid (bone marrow) elements.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined adrenal mass containing macroscopic fat (markedly negative HU), sometimes with scattered soft-tissue/calcified components.

Differential
  • Retroperitoneal liposarcoma (much rarer, less well-defined fat)
  • Adrenal adenoma with fat (rare)
Next Step

No treatment needed unless large and symptomatic (hemorrhage risk with very large lesions).

4. Adrenal hemorrhage

Concerning

Bleeding into the adrenal gland, seen in trauma, stress states, or anticoagulation.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged adrenal gland with high attenuation (acute blood, 50-90 HU) that decreases in density and size over time; may be bilateral in critical illness.

Differential
  • Adrenal trauma (usually right-sided, associated with severe injury)
  • Anticoagulation/coagulopathy
  • Sepsis/stress (Waterhouse-Friderichsen in meningococcemia)
  • Underlying adrenal tumor bleeding
Next Step

Assess for adrenal insufficiency if bilateral; correlate with trauma severity and coagulation status.

5. Pheochromocytoma

Concerning

Catecholamine-secreting tumor of the adrenal medulla.

Imaging Characteristics

Often large (>3 cm), heterogeneous, avidly enhancing mass with non-contrast attenuation typically >10 HU; may show cystic/necrotic areas.

Differential
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma
  • Large indeterminate adenoma
  • Adrenal metastasis
Next Step

Correlate with hypertension/palpitations/diaphoresis symptoms; biochemical testing (plasma/urine metanephrines) before any biopsy given hypertensive crisis risk.

Bladder

6 findings

1. Bladder wall thickening, benign/reactive

Variable

Diffuse bladder wall thickening from an underdistended bladder or chronic outlet process.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth, circumferential wall thickening, often more pronounced when the bladder is poorly distended (a common pitfall).

Differential
  • Underdistension (pseudothickening)
  • Chronic outlet obstruction (BPH, stricture)
  • Chronic cystitis
  • Neurogenic bladder
Next Step

Repeat imaging with adequate bladder distension if thickening is the only finding and clinical suspicion for malignancy is low.

2. Bladder calculus

Variable

Stone within the bladder lumen, often related to outlet obstruction or a foreign body nidus.

Imaging Characteristics

Round or faceted calcified density, typically mobile/dependent within the bladder lumen (distinguishing it from a fixed calcified mass).

Differential
  • Calcified bladder tumor (fixed, wall-based)
  • Ureterocele with stone
Next Step

Urology referral, especially to address underlying outlet obstruction.

3. Bladder mass / urothelial carcinoma

Concerning

Malignant or potentially malignant bladder wall lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, often papillary or sessile wall-based soft-tissue mass projecting into the lumen, with focal wall thickening at the base; may show local invasion in advanced disease.

Differential
  • Blood clot (non-fixed, changes position/dissolves on repeat imaging)
  • Benign polyp
  • Prostate or cervical cancer invasion
Next Step

Urology referral for cystoscopy and biopsy.

4. Bladder rupture, extraperitoneal

Concerning

Bladder wall disruption with urine leaking into the perivesical space, usually associated with pelvic fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Contrast extravasation (on CT cystogram) confined around the bladder in a 'molar tooth' or flame-shaped pattern without extending into the peritoneal cavity.

Differential
  • Intraperitoneal rupture (contrast outlines bowel loops, extends into paracolic gutters)
Next Step

Usually managed with catheter drainage alone; urology consultation.

5. Bladder rupture, intraperitoneal

Concerning

Bladder wall disruption with urine leaking into the peritoneal cavity, classically from a direct blow to a distended bladder.

Imaging Characteristics

Contrast extravasation outlining bowel loops and extending into the paracolic gutters/pelvis on CT cystogram.

Differential
  • Extraperitoneal rupture
Next Step

Requires surgical repair; urology/trauma surgery consultation.

6. Urachal remnant abnormality

Variable

Persistent embryologic urachus (connecting bladder to umbilicus) with cyst, sinus, or malignant transformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Midline cystic or tubular structure between the bladder dome and umbilicus; wall thickening, enhancement, or calcification raises concern for infection or urachal carcinoma.

Differential
  • Urachal cyst (benign)
  • Infected urachal cyst/abscess
  • Urachal adenocarcinoma (rare but important to exclude if solid/enhancing components)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or complex urachal remnants.

Uterus, Ovaries & Adnexa

6 findings

1. Uterine fibroid (leiomyoma)

Benign

Extremely common benign smooth muscle tumor of the uterus; CT is not the primary modality for characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often calcified (popcorn-like) mass distorting the uterine contour; may be heterogeneous if degenerating.

Differential
  • Adenomyosis (less well-defined, diffuse)
  • Uterine sarcoma (rare, consider with rapid growth)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; pelvic ultrasound or MRI is the preferred modality for further characterization.

2. Ovarian cyst, simple

Benign

Common physiologic or benign ovarian cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Round, thin-walled, water-attenuation lesion; incidentally noted on CT which is not the primary modality for adnexal characterization.

Differential
  • Functional cyst (follicular, corpus luteum)
  • Benign cystadenoma
Next Step

Pelvic ultrasound for definitive characterization if not already obtained; most resolve or are followed conservatively if small.

3. Ruptured ovarian cyst

Concerning

Cyst rupture causing acute pelvic pain and variable free fluid, usually self-limited but can mimic surgical emergencies.

Imaging Characteristics

Collapsed or irregular ovarian cyst with adjacent free fluid (simple or hemorrhagic) in the pelvis/pelvic cul-de-sac.

Differential
  • Ectopic pregnancy (check hCG)
  • Ovarian torsion
  • Appendicitis/other pelvic inflammatory process
Next Step

Correlate hCG to exclude ectopic; usually managed conservatively with analgesia unless significant hemoperitoneum/instability.

4. Ovarian torsion

Concerning

Twisting of the ovary on its vascular pedicle, a surgical emergency for ovarian salvage.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, edematous ovary, often with peripherally displaced follicles and a twisted vascular pedicle ('whirl sign'); underlying mass (often a dermoid) is a common predisposing factor.

Differential
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
  • Hemorrhagic cyst
  • Ovarian mass without torsion
Next Step

Gynecologic surgical emergency; ultrasound with Doppler is the confirmatory study of choice when torsion is suspected clinically.

5. Tubo-ovarian abscess

Concerning

Complication of pelvic inflammatory disease with a complex inflammatory adnexal mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, thick-walled, multiloculated adnexal collection, often bilateral, with surrounding fat stranding and free pelvic fluid.

Differential
  • Ovarian torsion
  • Ruptured ovarian cyst
  • Appendiceal abscess (if right-sided)
Next Step

IV antibiotics; IR or surgical drainage for large or non-responding abscesses.

6. Adnexal mass, complex/suspicious

Concerning

Adnexal lesion with features raising concern for malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid components, thick septations, papillary projections, or associated ascites/peritoneal implants in an adnexal mass.

Differential
  • Ovarian malignancy (primary or metastatic, e.g., Krukenberg tumor)
  • Endometrioma
  • Mature/immature teratoma
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral; dedicated pelvic ultrasound and CA-125 as part of workup.

Prostate & Seminal Vesicles

3 findings

1. Benign prostatic hyperplasia

Benign

Common age-related enlargement of the prostate, particularly the transition zone.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged prostate, often with a bulging contour into the bladder base; CT cannot reliably distinguish zonal anatomy the way MRI can.

Differential
  • Prostate cancer (CT is insensitive for detecting/excluding this)
  • Prostatitis
Next Step

Correlate with PSA and urinary symptoms; MRI or biopsy if cancer is a concern.

2. Acute prostatitis / prostatic abscess

Concerning

Prostate gland infection, ranging from simple inflammation to abscess formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, heterogeneous prostate with surrounding fat stranding; abscess shows a rim-enhancing fluid collection within the gland.

Differential
  • Prostate malignancy (less acute presentation)
  • BPH with reactive changes
Next Step

Antibiotics; IR or urologic drainage for abscess.

3. Seminal vesicle abnormality (cyst/mass)

Variable

Cystic or solid lesion of the seminal vesicles, uncommon and often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic lesion (benign) versus asymmetric enlargement/infiltration (concerning for local extension of prostate cancer).

Differential
  • Simple seminal vesicle cyst
  • Local extension of prostate malignancy
  • Ejaculatory duct obstruction
Next Step

Urology referral if solid/infiltrative features present.

Vascular

11 findings

1. Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), intact

Concerning

Focal dilation of the abdominal aorta ≥3 cm, with rupture risk rising sharply with size.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter ≥3 cm, measured outer-wall to outer-wall; assess for mural thrombus, involvement of visceral/iliac vessels, and precise measurements for surveillance.

Differential
  • Ectasia (mild diffuse dilation <3 cm)
  • Pseudoaneurysm (focal, often post-surgical/traumatic)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral; repair generally considered around 5.0-5.5 cm or with rapid growth (>0.5 cm/6 months), per current guidelines.

2. Abdominal aortic aneurysm, ruptured or leaking

Concerning

AAA with active or impending rupture, a surgical emergency with high mortality.

Imaging Characteristics

Periaortic or retroperitoneal hematoma, loss of the normal fat plane around the aorta, the 'draped aorta sign' (posterior wall contours to adjacent vertebra), or active contrast extravasation.

Differential
  • Contained rupture vs. free rupture (both emergencies)
Next Step

Immediate vascular surgery activation; do not delay transfer/intervention for additional imaging.

3. Aortic dissection, abdominal extension

Concerning

Intimal tear with a propagating false lumen extending into the abdominal aorta.

Imaging Characteristics

Intimal flap separating true and false lumens, often with the false lumen larger and showing slower flow/thrombus; assess visceral vessel origin (branch from true vs. false lumen) for malperfusion.

Differential
  • Aortic dissection confined to the thoracic aorta (assess full extent)
  • Intramural hematoma (no visible flap)
Next Step

Emergent vascular surgery/cardiology consultation; assess for end-organ malperfusion (renal, mesenteric, limb).

4. Mesenteric artery stenosis/occlusion (SMA/celiac)

Concerning

Narrowing or occlusion of the mesenteric arterial supply, a substrate for acute or chronic mesenteric ischemia.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal narrowing or abrupt cutoff of the SMA, celiac axis, or IMA on CT angiography, often at the vessel origin from atherosclerotic disease.

Differential
  • Acute embolic occlusion (abrupt cutoff, often without calcified plaque)
  • Chronic atherosclerotic stenosis (calcified, gradual)
  • Median arcuate ligament syndrome (celiac only, positional)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms (postprandial pain, weight loss for chronic; acute severe pain for embolic); vascular surgery/IR referral.

5. Portal vein thrombosis

Concerning

Clot within the portal venous system, from cirrhosis, malignancy, hypercoagulability, or intra-abdominal infection/inflammation.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the portal vein lumen; bland thrombus is non-enhancing while tumor thrombus (from HCC) shows internal enhancement and vessel expansion.

Differential
  • Bland thrombus (cirrhosis, hypercoagulable state, post-splenectomy)
  • Tumor thrombus (HCC)
  • Pylephlebitis (septic thrombophlebitis, usually from intra-abdominal infection)
Next Step

Anticoagulation for bland thrombus (hepatology input in cirrhotics); treat underlying source if septic (pylephlebitis).

6. Mesenteric/portal venous gas

Concerning

Gas within the mesenteric or portal venous system, classically associated with bowel ischemia/infarction.

Imaging Characteristics

Branching lucencies extending to within 2 cm of the liver capsule (portal gas), distinct from the more central branching of pneumobilia (biliary gas).

Differential
  • Bowel ischemia/infarction (most concerning)
  • Recent endoscopy/bowel instrumentation
  • IBD flare (rarely)
Next Step

Correlate with lactate and exam; surgical consultation given strong association with transmural bowel ischemia.

7. Median arcuate ligament syndrome

Variable

Extrinsic compression of the celiac axis by the median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, characteristic 'hooked' narrowing at the celiac origin, worse on expiration, often with post-stenotic dilation.

Differential
  • Atherosclerotic celiac stenosis (not positional, calcified)
Next Step

Correlate with postprandial pain/weight loss in a younger, thin patient; vascular surgery referral if symptomatic.

8. Visceral artery aneurysm/pseudoaneurysm

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of a visceral branch vessel (splenic artery most common), with rupture risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, rounded outpouching from a visceral artery matching blood pool enhancement; splenic artery aneurysms are most common, especially in multiparous women and portal hypertension.

Differential
  • True aneurysm (all wall layers)
  • Pseudoaneurysm (contained rupture, often post-pancreatitis or trauma)
Next Step

IR consultation for embolization, particularly for splenic artery aneurysms >2 cm, symptomatic lesions, or any pseudoaneurysm.

9. Renal artery aneurysm

Variable

Focal dilation of the renal artery, often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded outpouching from the renal artery or a branch, sometimes with a calcified rim.

Differential
  • True aneurysm
  • Pseudoaneurysm (post-biopsy, post-trauma)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/IR referral for larger lesions (generally >2 cm) or in women of childbearing age given rupture risk with pregnancy.

10. IVC filter, thrombus, or anomaly

Variable

Findings related to the inferior vena cava, including prior filter placement, thrombosis, or congenital variants.

Imaging Characteristics

Filter: metallic strut device within the infrarenal IVC. Thrombosis: filling defect within the IVC lumen. Anomaly: duplicated or left-sided IVC, azygos continuation.

Differential
  • IVC thrombosis (bland vs. tumor thrombus from RCC)
  • Retrievable filter with retained/embedded struts
  • Congenital IVC variant (important to note before any planned intervention)
Next Step

Correlate filter retrieval timeline if applicable; anticoagulation and hematology/vascular input for thrombosis.

11. Aortoenteric fistula

Concerning

Abnormal communication between the aorta (often at a prior graft site) and adjacent bowel, classically the duodenum.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal fat plane between the aorta/graft and adjacent bowel, ectopic gas adjacent to or within the aortic wall/graft, and sometimes overt extravasation.

Differential
  • Post-surgical change without fistula
  • Aortitis/graft infection without fistula
Next Step

Surgical emergency, especially with a history of prior aortic graft and GI bleeding ('herald bleed'); emergent vascular surgery consultation.

Retroperitoneum & Lymph Nodes

5 findings

1. Reactive/benign lymphadenopathy

Benign

Mildly enlarged lymph nodes from a reactive process, common and usually not a primary concern.

Imaging Characteristics

Oval, normal fatty hilum preserved, short-axis diameter typically <1 cm (varies by nodal station).

Differential
  • Reactive to adjacent inflammation/infection
  • Early/mild involvement by systemic process
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; typically no isolated workup needed.

2. Pathologic lymphadenopathy

Concerning

Enlarged lymph nodes suspicious for malignant or significant infectious/inflammatory involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Short-axis diameter >1 cm (retroperitoneal/mesenteric stations), loss of fatty hilum, rounded (rather than oval) shape, or nodal conglomeration/matting.

Differential
  • Lymphoma
  • Metastatic disease
  • Tuberculosis/granulomatous disease
  • Castleman disease
Next Step

Correlate with known malignancy history; consider biopsy of the most accessible/largest node for tissue diagnosis if etiology unclear.

3. Retroperitoneal fibrosis

Concerning

Fibro-inflammatory process encasing the retroperitoneal structures, often the ureters and great vessels.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue rind surrounding the infrarenal aorta and often medially deviating (rather than displacing laterally, as tumor typically does) the ureters, causing hydronephrosis.

Differential
  • IgG4-related disease
  • Malignancy-related desmoplastic reaction (retroperitoneal lymphoma/sarcoma - tends to displace rather than encase)
  • Medication-related (ergot derivatives, historically)
Next Step

Urology/rheumatology referral; biopsy may be needed to exclude malignancy, especially if atypical features.

4. Retroperitoneal hematoma

Concerning

Bleeding into the retroperitoneal space, from trauma, anticoagulation, or a ruptured vascular lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, high-attenuation collection in the retroperitoneum, often tracking along the psoas muscle; look for an active bleeding source (vascular injury, ruptured aneurysm).

Differential
  • Traumatic injury
  • Anticoagulant-related spontaneous bleed
  • Ruptured aneurysm
  • Post-procedural (femoral access site)
Next Step

Assess hemodynamic stability; IR embolization or surgery for active extravasation.

5. Retroperitoneal sarcoma

Concerning

Primary malignant tumor arising from retroperitoneal soft tissue, uncommonly encountered but important not to miss.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, often heterogeneous mass displacing (rather than encasing) adjacent retroperitoneal structures, distinguishing it from retroperitoneal fibrosis.

Differential
  • Retroperitoneal fibrosis
  • Lymphoma
  • Nodal metastasis conglomerate
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral; biopsy for tissue diagnosis prior to definitive treatment planning.

Peritoneum, Omentum & Mesentery

6 findings

1. Epiploic appendagitis

Benign

Torsion or venous thrombosis of a fatty epiploic appendage on the colon, a benign self-limited mimic of diverticulitis/appendicitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, ovoid, fat-attenuation lesion adjacent to the colon with a thin hyperattenuating rim and surrounding fat stranding; a central dot of high attenuation (thrombosed vein) may be seen.

Differential
  • Diverticulitis (larger area of inflammation, may show diverticula)
  • Omental infarction (larger, more central)
  • Appendicitis (if right-sided)
Next Step

Supportive care only (NSAIDs); self-limited over 1-2 weeks, no antibiotics or surgery needed.

2. Omental infarction

Benign

Ischemic omental fat, idiopathic or from omental torsion, another self-limited mimic of surgical abdomen.

Imaging Characteristics

Large (often >5 cm), ill-defined fatty mass with surrounding stranding, typically in the right side of the abdomen, without an identifiable causative pathology like appendicitis.

Differential
  • Epiploic appendagitis (smaller, colon-adjacent)
  • Appendicitis
  • Omental metastasis (if history of malignancy)
Next Step

Supportive care; self-limited, though occasionally requires surgery if diagnosis uncertain or symptoms persist.

3. Peritoneal carcinomatosis

Concerning

Diffuse peritoneal spread of malignancy, most often from GI or ovarian primary tumors.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular or plaque-like peritoneal/omental enhancement ('omental caking'), often with ascites; may coat bowel serosa and mesentery.

Differential
  • Peritoneal tuberculosis (can mimic, especially in endemic areas)
  • Peritoneal mesothelioma
  • Pseudomyxoma peritonei (mucinous, from appendiceal or ovarian mucinous tumor)
Next Step

Oncology referral; ascites sampling or peritoneal biopsy for cytologic/histologic confirmation.

4. Pseudomyxoma peritonei

Concerning

Mucinous ascites filling the peritoneal cavity, classically from a ruptured appendiceal mucinous neoplasm.

Imaging Characteristics

Low-attenuation, scalloping mucinous material surrounding the liver and spleen and filling the abdomen/pelvis, distinct from simple ascites.

Differential
  • Simple/malignant ascites
  • Mucinous peritoneal carcinomatosis from ovarian primary
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral for consideration of cytoreductive surgery with HIPEC.

5. Mesenteric panniculitis

Variable

Chronic inflammatory/fibrotic process of the mesenteric fat, often idiopathic.

Imaging Characteristics

Hazy increased attenuation of the mesenteric fat ('misty mesentery') with a hyperattenuating pseudocapsule and preserved fat halo around mesenteric vessels/nodes ('fat ring sign').

Differential
  • Mesenteric edema (heart failure, hypoalbuminemia, venous congestion - lacks pseudocapsule)
  • Mesenteric lymphoma/carcinomatosis (nodes are larger, fat ring sign lost)
Next Step

Usually benign and self-limited; consider malignancy workup only if atypical features (large nodes, mass effect) are present.

6. Intra-abdominal abscess, unspecified location

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the peritoneal cavity from any of many possible sources.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, low-attenuation fluid collection, often with internal gas or debris and surrounding inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Post-surgical abscess
  • Perforated viscus with contained collection
  • Infected hematoma/seroma
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics and source identification/control.

Hernias

6 findings

1. Inguinal hernia, reducible

Benign

Protrusion of abdominal contents (fat and/or bowel) through the inguinal canal, without evidence of obstruction/strangulation.

Imaging Characteristics

Fat or bowel loop tracking through the inguinal canal, medial (direct) or lateral (indirect) to the inferior epigastric vessels, without wall thickening or obstruction.

Differential
  • Femoral hernia (below the inguinal ligament, higher strangulation risk)
  • Lymphadenopathy (if fat-only hernia is subtle)
Next Step

Elective surgical referral; no urgent action if asymptomatic and non-obstructed.

2. Femoral hernia

Concerning

Protrusion through the femoral canal, medial to the femoral vein; higher strangulation risk than inguinal hernias.

Imaging Characteristics

Herniated contents seen inferior and medial to the femoral vessels, below the inguinal ligament.

Differential
  • Inguinal hernia (above the inguinal ligament)
  • Enlarged lymph node (Cloquet node)
Next Step

Higher urgency surgical referral given strangulation risk, even if currently reducible/asymptomatic.

3. Incarcerated/strangulated hernia

Concerning

Hernia contents trapped and unable to reduce (incarcerated), with strangulation implying vascular compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-reducible bowel or fat within a hernia sac with wall thickening, reduced enhancement, mesenteric edema, or free fluid within the sac suggesting strangulation.

Differential
  • Simple reducible hernia (no obstruction or ischemic signs)
Next Step

Surgical emergency, particularly with signs of strangulation (bowel wall changes, free fluid, lack of enhancement).

4. Umbilical/paraumbilical hernia

Benign

Protrusion through the umbilical or paraumbilical fascial defect, common especially with obesity, pregnancy, or ascites.

Imaging Characteristics

Fat or bowel loop protruding through a midline abdominal wall defect at or near the umbilicus.

Differential
  • Diastasis recti (fascial thinning/widening without a true defect, no hernia sac)
Next Step

Elective surgical referral if symptomatic; monitor for incarceration.

5. Incisional/ventral hernia

Variable

Hernia through a defect at a prior surgical incision site.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel or fat protruding through a fascial defect corresponding to a surgical scar, which can occur anywhere on the abdominal wall.

Differential
  • Recurrent hernia after prior repair
  • Seroma/fluid collection at incision site (no fascial defect)
Next Step

Elective surgical referral unless incarcerated/strangulated, which requires urgent repair.

6. Internal hernia (post-surgical, e.g., post-bariatric)

Concerning

Bowel herniation through a surgically created or congenital mesenteric defect, notably after Roux-en-Y gastric bypass.

Imaging Characteristics

Clustered, engorged mesenteric vessels swirling toward a defect ('mesenteric swirl sign'), with bowel loops in an abnormal location relative to expected post-surgical anatomy.

Differential
  • Adhesive SBO
  • Normal post-bariatric anatomy (can be a subtle distinction)
Next Step

High index of suspicion needed given normal-appearing bowel caliber is common even with significant internal hernia; surgical consultation given closed-loop obstruction/strangulation risk.

Bones & Musculature

8 findings

1. Degenerative changes, spine/pelvis

Benign

Common age-related osteoarthritic and degenerative disc changes incidentally seen on abdominal/pelvic CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Osteophytes, disc space narrowing, facet arthropathy, and sacroiliac joint sclerosis; nonspecific and very common with age.

Differential
  • Normal aging
  • Inflammatory arthropathy (if sacroiliac changes are erosive/bilateral in a younger patient)
Next Step

No acute action; correlate with symptoms if back pain is a presenting complaint.

2. Compression fracture, vertebral body

Variable

Loss of vertebral body height, benign (osteoporotic) or pathologic (malignant) in etiology.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped or biconcave loss of vertebral body height; benign fractures typically preserve normal marrow fat signal and posterior elements, while pathologic fractures show a soft-tissue mass, posterior element involvement, or convex posterior cortical bulging.

Differential
  • Osteoporotic compression fracture
  • Pathologic fracture from metastasis or myeloma
  • Traumatic acute fracture
Next Step

Correlate with trauma mechanism and malignancy history; MRI can further characterize acute vs. chronic and benign vs. pathologic if unclear.

3. Pelvic fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the bony pelvis, ranging from stable to life-threatening with associated hemorrhage.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the pelvic ring, acetabulum, or sacrum; assess ring stability (single vs. multiple breaks) and look for associated pelvic hematoma or active extravasation.

Differential
  • Stable, isolated fracture (e.g., pubic ramus)
  • Unstable ring disruption with high hemorrhage risk
Next Step

Orthopedic trauma consultation; assess hemodynamic stability and screen for active bleeding requiring IR embolization.

4. Sacral fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the sacrum, from trauma or insufficiency (osteoporotic) causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertical lucent or sclerotic line through the sacral ala, sometimes with a horizontal component forming an 'H-shaped' or 'Honda sign' pattern in bilateral insufficiency fractures.

Differential
  • Traumatic sacral fracture (higher energy)
  • Insufficiency fracture (osteoporosis, pelvic radiation)
Next Step

Correlate with trauma history and bone density risk factors; orthopedic/spine consultation.

5. Lytic bone lesion

Concerning

Area of bone destruction, with a broad differential requiring correlation with number, location, and patient history.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of decreased bone density/cortical destruction; aggressive features include a wide zone of transition, cortical breakthrough, and an associated soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Metastasis (most common in adults, especially multiple lesions)
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Primary bone tumor
  • Infection (osteomyelitis)
Next Step

Oncology workup if malignancy suspected; correlate with known primary cancer history and consider biopsy if solitary and indeterminate.

6. Sclerotic bone lesion

Variable

Area of increased bone density, benign in most incidental cases but occasionally a marker of metastatic disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse increased bone density; benign lesions (bone islands) are well-defined with a 'brush border,' while metastatic sclerotic lesions (prostate, breast) may be multiple and less well-defined.

Differential
  • Bone island (enostosis, benign)
  • Osteoblastic metastasis (prostate, breast)
  • Paget disease
  • Healed fracture/prior infarct
Next Step

Correlate with malignancy history; bone scan or MRI if indeterminate or multiple lesions present.

7. Psoas abscess

Concerning

Infection within the psoas muscle, either primary (hematogenous) or secondary (from adjacent spine/bowel infection).

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection within or expanding the psoas muscle, sometimes with gas; look for a contiguous source (discitis-osteomyelitis, Crohn disease, diverticulitis).

Differential
  • Psoas hematoma (no rim enhancement, high attenuation acutely)
  • Psoas malignancy (rare)
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics; identify and treat the source, especially spinal infection.

8. Rectus sheath hematoma

Variable

Bleeding within the rectus abdominis muscle sheath, often from a tear in the epigastric vessels.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform or lentiform high-attenuation collection within the rectus muscle, often unilateral; may show active extravasation if acute/expanding.

Differential
  • Anticoagulation-related (most common)
  • Post-traumatic/post-procedural
  • Strenuous exercise/coughing
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively (reverse anticoagulation if indicated); IR embolization for active extravasation or expanding hematoma.

Postoperative, Devices & Foreign Bodies

6 findings

1. Surgical clips/staples

Benign

Expected metallic material from prior surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete metallic densities along a surgical bed or anastomotic line, often with associated streak artifact.

Differential
  • Normal post-surgical material
Next Step

No action; correlate with surgical history to confirm expected location.

2. Postoperative fluid collection (seroma/hematoma)

Variable

Expected early postoperative fluid collection, which can be benign or become infected.

Imaging Characteristics

Simple fluid collection along the surgical bed without rim enhancement (early/uncomplicated) versus a thick, enhancing wall with gas suggesting abscess.

Differential
  • Simple seroma/hematoma (expected, resolving)
  • Infected collection/abscess
Next Step

Correlate with fever/wound symptoms; drainage if infected or symptomatic from mass effect.

3. Anastomotic leak

Concerning

Disruption of a surgical bowel anastomosis with luminal contents leaking extraluminally.

Imaging Characteristics

Extraluminal oral contrast (if given) or free air/fluid adjacent to a recent anastomosis, sometimes with a discrete abscess.

Differential
  • Expected postoperative free air (up to ~7-10 days) without other leak signs
  • Simple postoperative fluid collection
Next Step

Surgical consultation; may require reoperation, diversion, or percutaneous drainage with bowel rest depending on severity.

4. Feeding tube/drain malposition

Concerning

Enteric tube, drain, or catheter located outside its intended position.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube tip located outside the expected structure (e.g., gastrostomy tube tip free within the peritoneal cavity rather than the gastric lumen).

Differential
  • Correctly positioned device (compare to expected course)
Next Step

Notify the proceduralist/surgical team promptly; malpositioned feeding tubes risk peritonitis if used.

5. Ingested foreign body

Variable

Swallowed object visualized within the GI tract.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete, often linear or geometric density within bowel lumen distinct from normal contents; location, sharpness, and battery composition determine urgency.

Differential
  • Blunt, non-toxic object likely to pass spontaneously
  • Sharp object or button battery (high complication risk)
  • Object impacted at a narrowing (pylorus, ileocecal valve)
Next Step

Urgent endoscopic/surgical consultation for button batteries, sharp objects, or signs of perforation/obstruction; otherwise may observe for passage.

6. Retained surgical item

Concerning

Surgical sponge, instrument, or other material inadvertently left after a prior operation.

Imaging Characteristics

A gossypiboma (retained sponge) classically shows a spongiform, gas-containing mass with a radiopaque marker; retained instruments are usually obvious metallic densities.

Differential
  • Abscess (gossypiboma can closely mimic this)
  • Postoperative fluid collection
Next Step

Surgical consultation for removal; important medicolegal and patient safety issue to document clearly.

Incidental & Miscellaneous

8 findings

1. Vascular calcifications (atherosclerotic)

Benign

Calcified atherosclerotic plaque within the aorta or its branches, extremely common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear or coarse calcific densities along the vessel wall, most often aorta, iliac, and splenic/pancreatic arteries.

Differential
  • Normal age-related finding
  • Marker of systemic atherosclerotic burden
Next Step

No direct action; consider cardiovascular risk factor optimization if extensive for age.

2. Phleboliths (pelvic)

Benign

Calcified thrombi within pelvic veins, extremely common and often mistaken for distal ureteral stones.

Imaging Characteristics

Round calcific densities in the pelvis, often with a lucent center; located off the expected course of the ureter (key distinguishing feature from a ureteral stone).

Differential
  • Distal ureteral stone (look for the 'soft-tissue rim sign' present with stones but not phleboliths, and for associated hydroureter)
Next Step

No action needed once distinguished from a ureteral stone.

3. Lipoma, intra-abdominal/retroperitoneal

Benign

Benign fat-containing tumor, which can occur essentially anywhere including bowel wall, mesentery, or retroperitoneum.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, fat-attenuation (negative HU) lesion with a thin, imperceptible capsule and no soft-tissue enhancing components.

Differential
  • Well-differentiated liposarcoma (larger, may have thick septa or soft-tissue nodularity - key distinguishing features)
  • Angiomyolipoma (if renal)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous, small lipoma; larger or heterogeneous fatty masses warrant further characterization.

4. Diastasis recti

Benign

Widening/thinning of the linea alba without a true fascial defect or hernia sac.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric bulging of abdominal contents in the midline with an intact, though attenuated and widened, linea alba (no discrete fascial defect).

Differential
  • True midline (umbilical/epigastric) hernia (has a discrete fascial defect)
Next Step

No surgical urgency; cosmetic/core-strengthening referral if desired, distinct from true hernia management.

5. Perivesical/pelvic varices

Benign

Dilated venous structures in the pelvis, common incidental finding, especially in multiparous women.

Imaging Characteristics

Serpiginous, enhancing tubular structures in the broad ligament/parametrial region or perivesical space, following venous phase enhancement.

Differential
  • Pelvic congestion syndrome (if symptomatic with chronic pelvic pain)
  • Arteriovenous malformation (rare, would show early arterial filling)
Next Step

No action if asymptomatic; gynecology referral if correlating with chronic pelvic pain (pelvic congestion syndrome).

6. Horseshoe kidney

Benign

Congenital fusion of the lower poles of the kidneys across the midline, the most common renal fusion anomaly.

Imaging Characteristics

Kidneys connected across the midline by an isthmus of parenchymal or fibrous tissue anterior to the great vessels, with malrotated, low-lying renal pelves.

Differential
  • Normal variant, usually incidental
Next Step

No action needed; associated with slightly increased risk of stones/UPJ obstruction and important to note before any planned aortic/renal intervention.

7. Situs anomaly (situs inversus/ambiguus)

Benign

Abnormal left-right positioning of abdominal organs, congenital.

Imaging Characteristics

Mirror-image or indeterminate positioning of the liver, spleen, stomach, and great vessels relative to normal anatomy.

Differential
  • Situs inversus totalis (complete mirroring, usually benign/incidental)
  • Situs ambiguus/heterotaxy (associated with cardiac and splenic anomalies, higher clinical relevance)
Next Step

Usually incidental if isolated; heterotaxy syndromes warrant correlation with cardiac anatomy.

8. Calcified granuloma (liver/spleen)

Benign

Healed prior granulomatous infection (histoplasmosis, TB), extremely common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, discrete, densely calcified foci within the liver or spleen parenchyma without surrounding soft tissue or enhancement.

Differential
  • Healed histoplasmosis/tuberculosis (most common)
  • Calcified metastasis (rare, usually mucinous primary)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for classic, stable calcified granulomas.

Free Air / Pneumoperitoneum

5 findings

1. Free air under the diaphragm (upright/erect film)

Concerning

The classic plain-film sign of a perforated hollow viscus, best seen on an erect chest or abdominal film.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin crescent of lucency between the diaphragm and liver/spleen on an upright film; as little as 1-2 mL of free air can be visible.

Differential
  • Perforated peptic ulcer
  • Perforated diverticulitis/appendicitis
  • Recent surgery (expected up to ~7-10 days)
  • Chilaiditi syndrome (interposed colon, not true free air)
Next Step

Surgical consultation; correlate with recent surgical history and confirm with CT if uncertain.

2. Rigler sign (double wall sign)

Concerning

Visualization of both the inner (mucosal) and outer (serosal) bowel wall, indicating free air on both sides of the bowel.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel wall appears unusually well-defined and thin because gas outlines it from both the lumen and the peritoneal cavity, best seen on a supine film.

Differential
  • Pneumoperitoneum from perforation
  • Post-surgical free air
Next Step

Treat as pneumoperitoneum; surgical consultation.

3. Football sign

Concerning

Massive pneumoperitoneum outlining the entire peritoneal cavity as an oval lucency, classically described in neonates but seen in adults with large-volume free air.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, oval, central lucency over the abdomen on a supine film representing a huge volume of free intraperitoneal gas, with the falciform ligament sometimes visible as the 'lace' of the football.

Differential
  • Massive perforation
  • Post-laparotomy with large residual air
Next Step

Surgical emergency evaluation.

4. Cupola sign

Concerning

Free air trapped centrally over the spine on a supine film, an easily missed subtle sign.

Imaging Characteristics

Small oval lucency projecting over the lower spine/pelvis on a supine abdominal film, representing air trapped anterior to the spine.

Differential
  • Small-volume pneumoperitoneum
Next Step

Correlate clinically; obtain an erect or lateral decubitus view or CT if suspicion remains.

5. Falciform ligament sign (plain film)

Concerning

Free air outlining the falciform ligament, seen as a line in the right upper quadrant on a supine film.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin linear soft-tissue density (the ligament) sharply outlined by surrounding gas, projecting over the right upper quadrant.

Differential
  • Perforated viscus
Next Step

Correlate clinically; treat as pneumoperitoneum.

Bowel Gas Pattern & Obstruction

12 findings

1. Small bowel obstruction pattern

Concerning

Plain-film pattern suggesting mechanical blockage of the small bowel.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple dilated small bowel loops (>3 cm) with a 'stepladder' configuration of air-fluid levels on an upright/decubitus view, and valvulae conniventes crossing the full lumen width.

Differential
  • Adhesions
  • Hernia
  • Malignancy
  • Adynamic ileus (less discrete transition, more diffuse gas)
Next Step

Correlate with exam; CT is the next step to identify the transition point and assess for strangulation.

2. Large bowel obstruction pattern

Concerning

Plain-film pattern suggesting colonic obstruction, often from a distal mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Colonic distension outlining the peripheral 'picture frame' of the abdomen with haustra that do not cross the full lumen width; cecal diameter is the key measurement for perforation risk.

Differential
  • Colorectal malignancy
  • Volvulus
  • Stricture
  • Adynamic ileus with colonic predominance
Next Step

Measure cecal diameter; CT and surgical/GI consultation.

3. Sigmoid volvulus - 'coffee bean' sign

Concerning

Classic plain-film appearance of sigmoid volvulus.

Imaging Characteristics

Massively dilated, ahaustral loop of sigmoid colon shaped like a coffee bean or bent inner tube, typically rising out of the pelvis toward the right upper quadrant, with a visible central dividing line (the mesenteric twist).

Differential
  • Cecal volvulus (different location/orientation)
  • Massive colonic distension from another cause
Next Step

Emergent surgical/GI consultation; endoscopic detorsion may be attempted absent ischemia/perforation signs.

4. Cecal volvulus - 'kidney bean' sign

Concerning

Classic plain-film appearance of cecal volvulus, with the cecum twisted and displaced.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly dilated, kidney bean or comma-shaped cecum, often displaced toward the mid or left upper abdomen, with a paucity of gas in the distal colon.

Differential
  • Sigmoid volvulus
  • Massively dilated cecum from distal obstruction
Next Step

Surgical consultation; usually requires operative management.

5. Adynamic (paralytic) ileus

Variable

Diffuse bowel hypomotility without a discrete mechanical obstruction point.

Imaging Characteristics

Generalized gaseous distension of both small and large bowel, including the rectum, without a clear transition zone.

Differential
  • Post-operative state
  • Electrolyte disturbance
  • Medication effect (opioids/anticholinergics)
  • Intra-abdominal inflammatory process
Next Step

Treat the underlying cause; correlate with electrolytes and recent surgery.

6. Toxic megacolon pattern

Concerning

Severe colonic dilation with loss of normal haustral markings, a marker of fulminant colitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Transverse colon diameter >6 cm with loss of haustration and mucosal islands ('thumbprinting'); a marker of impending perforation.

Differential
  • Fulminant C. difficile colitis
  • Fulminant ulcerative colitis
  • Ischemic colitis
Next Step

Surgical/GI emergency; serial films and close clinical monitoring for perforation.

7. Sentinel loop sign

Variable

A single, localized dilated loop of bowel adjacent to an area of intra-abdominal inflammation, a nonspecific but classic localizing sign.

Imaging Characteristics

Isolated dilated small bowel loop with air-fluid levels near the site of the underlying process (e.g., left upper quadrant with pancreatitis).

Differential
  • Pancreatitis
  • Cholecystitis
  • Appendicitis
  • Any focal inflammatory process
Next Step

Correlate with clinical localization; further imaging (CT/ultrasound) to identify the underlying process.

8. String of pearls sign

Concerning

Small pockets of trapped gas aligned along the top of a fluid-filled, obstructed small bowel loop.

Imaging Characteristics

A row of small, discrete gas bubbles arranged linearly within a dilated, fluid-filled small bowel loop, best seen on an upright film.

Differential
  • High-grade small bowel obstruction
Next Step

Correlate with SBO workup; CT to identify transition point.

9. Gasless abdomen

Concerning

Absence of the normal bowel gas pattern, which can be a subtle but important abnormal finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Little to no visible bowel gas throughout the abdomen, sometimes with a hazy, ground-glass appearance suggesting ascites or fluid-filled bowel.

Differential
  • Complete fluid-filled obstruction (closed-loop)
  • Large-volume ascites
  • Post-emesis/NG decompression (benign if clinically correlated)
Next Step

Correlate clinically; CT if obstruction or ascites is suspected.

10. Colonic ileus / functional distension

Variable

Isolated colonic distension without mechanical obstruction, often reactive to another process.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse colonic gaseous distension with haustra preserved and no discrete transition point; often seen with the rectum also distended (differentiating from mechanical LBO).

Differential
  • Post-operative ileus
  • Medication effect (opioids)
  • Adjacent inflammatory process (appendicitis, pyelonephritis)
  • Electrolyte disturbance
Next Step

Treat underlying cause; conservative management (bowel rest, correct electrolytes).

11. Air-fluid levels, nonspecific

Variable

A general finding on upright/decubitus films that requires further characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Horizontal fluid levels with gas above them within bowel loops; a small number of air-fluid levels of similar height can be a normal variant, while multiple levels of differing heights suggest obstruction.

Differential
  • Normal variant (few, similar-height levels)
  • Small or large bowel obstruction
  • Ileus
  • Gastroenteritis
Next Step

Correlate with the number/pattern of levels and clinical picture; further imaging if obstruction is suspected.

12. Absence of rectal gas

Variable

Lack of visible gas in the rectum on plain film, a supportive but nonspecific sign.

Imaging Characteristics

No gas seen in the expected rectal location on a supine film.

Differential
  • Distal obstruction (mechanical)
  • Normal variant, especially after enema/bowel prep
  • Poor positioning/technique
Next Step

Correlate with other obstructive signs; not diagnostic in isolation.

Calcifications

12 findings

1. Radiopaque renal calculus

Variable

Kidney stone visible on plain film; roughly 60-70% of renal stones are radiopaque, most often calcium-based.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded or branching (staghorn) calcific density overlying the expected course of the kidney/collecting system.

Differential
  • Calcium oxalate/phosphate stone (radiopaque)
  • Uric acid stone (typically radiolucent, not seen on plain film)
  • Overlying bowel content/phlebolith (mimic)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms and location; CT remains the gold standard for full stone characterization.

2. Radiopaque gallstone

Variable

Gallstone visible on plain film; only about 10-15% of gallstones are radiopaque enough to be seen.

Imaging Characteristics

Faceted or laminated calcific density in the right upper quadrant, may show a central lucent nidus.

Differential
  • Cholelithiasis (radiopaque minority)
  • Costal cartilage calcification (mimic, follows rib contour)
Next Step

Ultrasound is the appropriate confirmatory study for suspected biliary disease regardless of plain-film visibility.

3. Appendicolith

Variable

Calcified fecalith within the appendix, visible on plain film in a minority of cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded, laminated calcific density in the right lower quadrant.

Differential
  • Incidental appendicolith without appendicitis
  • Marker of current/impending appendicitis
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam; CT or ultrasound for definitive appendicitis workup.

4. Phleboliths

Benign

Calcified pelvic venous thrombi, extremely common and easily mistaken for distal ureteral stones.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round calcific densities with a lucent center, scattered in the pelvis, typically off the expected course of the ureter.

Differential
  • Distal ureteral stone (differentiate by location relative to ureteral course and clinical correlation)
Next Step

No action needed once recognized as phleboliths.

5. Vascular (aortic wall) calcification

Benign

Atherosclerotic calcification of the abdominal aortic wall, common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear calcific densities paralleling the expected course of the aorta; can help estimate aortic diameter on plain film if walls are well seen.

Differential
  • Normal age-related atherosclerosis
  • May suggest AAA if calcified walls appear widened
Next Step

If aortic width appears increased, dedicated ultrasound or CT for AAA evaluation.

6. Chronic pancreatitis calcifications

Variable

Punctate pancreatic parenchymal calcifications from chronic pancreatic inflammation.

Imaging Characteristics

Scattered, coarse calcific densities in the expected location of the pancreas, often following the course of the pancreatic duct.

Differential
  • Chronic pancreatitis (alcohol-related most common)
  • Hereditary pancreatitis
Next Step

Correlate with history of pancreatitis; no acute action needed for calcifications alone.

7. Calcified uterine fibroid

Benign

Degenerated leiomyoma with characteristic coarse calcification.

Imaging Characteristics

Dense, mottled 'popcorn'-like calcification in the pelvis corresponding to the expected location of the uterus.

Differential
  • Calcified ovarian dermoid (may contain teeth/fat, more lateral)
  • Calcified lymph nodes
Next Step

No action needed; incidental finding correlating with known fibroid history.

8. Calcified mesenteric/abdominal lymph nodes

Benign

Calcified lymph nodes from prior granulomatous infection, common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, discrete, densely calcified foci scattered in the mid-abdomen, often clustered along expected mesenteric or para-aortic nodal chains.

Differential
  • Prior histoplasmosis/tuberculosis (most common)
  • Prior granulomatous disease of other etiology
Next Step

No follow-up needed for classic calcified granulomatous nodes.

9. Adrenal calcification

Benign

Calcification within the adrenal gland, usually from prior hemorrhage or infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear or stippled calcific density in the expected suprarenal location.

Differential
  • Prior adrenal hemorrhage (birth trauma, prior illness)
  • Prior tuberculosis (endemic areas)
  • Rarely, calcified adrenal neoplasm
Next Step

Usually no action needed if stable and asymptomatic; correlate with adrenal function if clinically indicated.

10. Splenic calcifications (granulomas)

Benign

Small calcified foci within the spleen from prior infection, an extremely common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small, discrete, round calcific densities in the left upper quadrant corresponding to the spleen.

Differential
  • Healed histoplasmosis (most common)
  • Prior tuberculosis
Next Step

No follow-up needed.

11. Costal cartilage calcification

Benign

Calcification of the costal cartilages, a common age-related finding that can mimic gallstones or renal stones.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or stippled calcific densities following the expected course of the costal cartilages at the lower rib margins.

Differential
  • Mimic of gallstone or upper renal pole stone
Next Step

Correlate location with rib contour on multiple views to distinguish from true pathologic calcification.

12. Prostatic calcifications

Benign

Calcifications within the prostate gland, common with age and chronic prostatitis/BPH.

Imaging Characteristics

Cluster of small, punctate calcific densities in the midline pelvis at the expected prostate location, below the bladder.

Differential
  • Benign prostatic calcification (most common)
  • Chronic prostatitis
Next Step

No action needed for incidental finding.

Foreign Bodies & Devices

9 findings

1. Ingested radiopaque foreign body

Variable

Swallowed object visible on plain film due to sufficient density.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete, well-defined density with a shape distinct from normal bowel contents, located within the expected course of the GI tract.

Differential
  • Blunt object likely to pass spontaneously
  • Sharp/pointed object (higher perforation risk)
  • Object impacted at a physiologic narrowing
Next Step

Correlate location, shape, and time since ingestion; endoscopy for esophageal or high-risk objects.

2. Button battery

Concerning

Swallowed button battery, a time-critical emergency due to risk of mucosal burn/perforation.

Imaging Characteristics

Round, well-defined density with a characteristic double-density 'halo' or 'step-off' rim on frontal view, distinguishing it from a coin.

Differential
  • Coin (single uniform density, no halo/step-off)
Next Step

Emergent endoscopic removal if in the esophagus; time-critical regardless of location given corrosive injury risk.

3. Retained surgical sponge (radiopaque marker)

Concerning

Surgical sponge inadvertently left after a prior operation, identified by its radiopaque marker strip.

Imaging Characteristics

Characteristic curvilinear or ribbon-like radiopaque marker within the operative field, sometimes surrounded by a soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Correctly accounted-for surgical material
  • Other retained surgical item
Next Step

Surgical consultation for removal; important patient-safety and documentation issue.

4. NG/OG tube malposition

Concerning

Nasogastric or orogastric tube positioned outside the expected gastric location.

Imaging Characteristics

Tube tip coiled in the esophagus, positioned in a bronchus, or looped incorrectly rather than terminating below the diaphragm in the stomach.

Differential
  • Correctly positioned tube (tip in stomach, below diaphragm)
  • Esophageal coiling
  • Bronchial malposition
Next Step

Reposition before use; do not begin feeds/medications through a malpositioned tube.

5. IUD position, normal

Benign

Intrauterine device in expected midline uterine position.

Imaging Characteristics

T- or radiopaque-shaped device centered in the pelvis at the expected location of the uterine cavity.

Differential
  • Normally positioned IUD
Next Step

No action needed if position is as expected and patient asymptomatic.

6. IUD malposition / expulsion

Concerning

Intrauterine device displaced from the endometrial cavity, including perforation into the myometrium/peritoneum or partial expulsion.

Imaging Characteristics

Device positioned outside the expected midline uterine location, rotated, low-lying (partial expulsion), or projecting beyond the expected uterine contour (possible perforation).

Differential
  • Normally positioned IUD
  • Perforated IUD (requires further imaging/removal)
  • Expelled IUD (may no longer be visible)
Next Step

Gynecology referral; ultrasound or CT to localize if perforation is suspected, given risk of bowel/bladder injury.

7. Ureteral stent position

Variable

Indwelling ureteral (double-J) stent, assessed for appropriate curled positioning at both ends.

Imaging Characteristics

Coiled proximal end in the renal pelvis and coiled distal end in the bladder, connected by a tube following the expected ureteral course.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned stent
  • Migrated/malpositioned stent (loss of proximal or distal coil)
Next Step

Urology follow-up for routine stent exchange or evaluation if malpositioned/symptomatic.

8. Surgical clips and staples

Benign

Expected metallic material from prior surgery.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete metallic densities along a surgical bed or staple line.

Differential
  • Normal post-surgical material
Next Step

No action; correlate with surgical history.

9. Peritoneal dialysis catheter position

Variable

Indwelling PD catheter, assessed for expected pelvic tip location.

Imaging Characteristics

Catheter with its tip positioned in the pelvis (true pelvis, below the sacral promontory); malposition shows the tip curled elsewhere in the abdomen.

Differential
  • Appropriately positioned catheter
  • Malpositioned catheter (associated with outflow failure)
Next Step

Correlate with dialysis outflow function; interventional radiology or surgical referral if malpositioned and dysfunctional.

Bones & Pelvis

8 findings

1. Pelvic fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the bony pelvis, from low-energy (osteoporotic) to high-energy trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of the pubic rami, ilium, acetabulum, or sacrum; assess for ring stability by checking for a second break.

Differential
  • Isolated stable fracture (e.g., single pubic ramus)
  • Unstable ring disruption with hemorrhage risk
Next Step

Orthopedic consultation; CT for further characterization and to assess for associated hemorrhage if high-energy mechanism.

2. Hip fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the proximal femur, common in older adults after a fall.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption at the femoral neck, intertrochanteric, or subtrochanteric region; look for asymmetric external rotation and shortening of the limb.

Differential
  • Femoral neck fracture (higher avascular necrosis risk)
  • Intertrochanteric fracture
  • Occult fracture (normal plain film with high clinical suspicion warrants MRI)
Next Step

Orthopedic consultation; MRI if plain film is negative but clinical suspicion remains high.

3. Hip dislocation

Concerning

Displacement of the femoral head from the acetabulum, most often posterior after trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Femoral head displaced from the acetabular socket, typically appearing smaller and more superolateral in posterior dislocation.

Differential
  • Posterior dislocation (most common, often with posterior wall acetabular fracture)
  • Anterior dislocation (less common)
Next Step

Orthopedic emergency; prompt reduction to reduce avascular necrosis risk.

4. Sacral fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the sacrum, from trauma or insufficiency in osteoporotic bone.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertical lucent line through the sacral ala, sometimes subtle and better characterized on CT; insufficiency fractures may show a horizontal component.

Differential
  • Traumatic fracture
  • Insufficiency fracture (osteoporosis, pelvic radiation history)
Next Step

CT for confirmation given plain-film sensitivity is limited; orthopedic/spine consultation.

5. Degenerative changes, lumbosacral spine/hips

Benign

Common age-related osteoarthritic change incidentally seen on abdominal/pelvic films.

Imaging Characteristics

Osteophytes, joint space narrowing, and subchondral sclerosis at the lumbosacral spine, sacroiliac joints, or hips.

Differential
  • Normal aging
  • Inflammatory arthropathy if erosive and bilateral in a younger patient
Next Step

No acute action; correlate with symptoms.

6. Sacroiliitis

Variable

Inflammation of the sacroiliac joints, seen with inflammatory or infectious causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Joint space narrowing, subchondral erosions, or sclerosis at the sacroiliac joints, often bilateral and symmetric in inflammatory spondyloarthropathy.

Differential
  • Ankylosing spondylitis/spondyloarthropathy (bilateral, symmetric)
  • Infectious sacroiliitis (usually unilateral)
  • Degenerative change (asymmetric, older patient)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral for inflammatory pattern; further imaging (MRI) for early/equivocal disease.

7. Lytic or sclerotic osseous lesion, pelvis

Concerning

Focal bone lesion identified on plain film, requiring further characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of decreased (lytic) or increased (sclerotic) bone density within the pelvis or proximal femur.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common in adults)
  • Multiple myeloma
  • Primary bone tumor
  • Paget disease
  • Bone island (benign sclerotic)
Next Step

Correlate with malignancy history; CT/MRI and oncology referral if indeterminate or symptomatic.

8. Vertebral compression fracture (lumbar spine on abdominal film)

Variable

Loss of vertebral body height seen incidentally on an abdominal series.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped or biconcave loss of height of a visualized lumbar vertebral body.

Differential
  • Osteoporotic compression fracture
  • Pathologic fracture (metastasis, myeloma)
  • Traumatic fracture
Next Step

Correlate with trauma history and malignancy risk; dedicated spine imaging (CT/MRI) if acute or pathologic features suspected.

Soft Tissue & Miscellaneous

5 findings

1. Loss of psoas shadow

Variable

Obscuration of the normally visible psoas muscle margin, a nonspecific but classic sign of adjacent pathology.

Imaging Characteristics

Asymmetric or bilateral loss of the sharp psoas muscle outline that is normally visible on a well-penetrated supine film.

Differential
  • Retroperitoneal process (hematoma, abscess, inflammation)
  • Poor technique/patient body habitus (common benign cause)
Next Step

Correlate clinically; cross-sectional imaging if a retroperitoneal process is suspected.

2. Pneumatosis intestinalis (subtle plain-film sign)

Variable

Gas within the bowel wall, occasionally visible on plain film though CT is far more sensitive.

Imaging Characteristics

Faint, curvilinear lucency paralleling the bowel wall, easy to miss on plain film.

Differential
  • Benign causes (COPD, steroids, post-endoscopy)
  • Bowel ischemia (concerning, especially with clinical toxicity)
Next Step

Low threshold for CT given plain-film insensitivity for this finding; correlate with lactate and exam.

3. Situs anomaly (visceral malposition)

Benign

Abnormal left-right organ positioning noted incidentally, such as an unusual gastric bubble or liver location.

Imaging Characteristics

Gastric air bubble or hepatic shadow on the unexpected side of the abdomen.

Differential
  • Situs inversus (usually incidental)
  • Situs ambiguus/heterotaxy (warrants cardiac correlation)
Next Step

Usually incidental if isolated; further workup only if clinical suspicion for heterotaxy syndrome.

4. Abdominal wall hernia (plain film)

Variable

Bowel loop identified outside the expected abdominal wall contour.

Imaging Characteristics

Gas-filled bowel loop projecting beyond the normal abdominal wall margin, most often in the inguinal or umbilical region.

Differential
  • Reducible hernia (incidental)
  • Obstructed/incarcerated hernia (if associated with obstructive gas pattern)
Next Step

Correlate with exam; surgical referral, more urgent if obstructive signs are present.

5. Enlarged bowel gas shadow suggesting organomegaly

Variable

Displacement of bowel gas by an enlarged solid organ, an indirect and limited plain-film sign.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel loops displaced away from an expected organ location (e.g., stomach and splenic flexure displaced medially/inferiorly by splenomegaly).

Differential
  • Splenomegaly
  • Hepatomegaly
  • Large intra-abdominal mass
Next Step

Cross-sectional imaging (ultrasound or CT) for definitive organ assessment; plain film is not reliable for this purpose.

Liver

12 findings

1. Hepatic cyst, simple

Benign

Common benign fluid-filled liver lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, markedly T2-hyperintense, T1-hypointense lesion with sharp margins and no enhancement or diffusion restriction.

Differential
  • Biliary hamartoma (if multiple, tiny)
  • Polycystic liver disease
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic simple cyst.

2. Hepatic hemangioma

Benign

Most common benign liver tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

T2 markedly hyperintense ('light bulb' bright), T1 hypointense, with peripheral discontinuous nodular enhancement and progressive centripetal fill-in on delayed phases.

Differential
  • Hepatocellular carcinoma (atypical enhancement pattern)
  • Hypervascular metastasis
  • Focal nodular hyperplasia
Next Step

Classic small hemangiomas need no further workup; atypical enhancement warrants dedicated liver MRI review.

3. Focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH)

Benign

Benign hyperplastic liver lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Near-isointense to liver on T1/T2 with a central T2-hyperintense scar; homogeneous arterial hyperenhancement, isointensity on portal venous phase, and uptake of hepatobiliary contrast on delayed phase (distinguishing it from adenoma/HCC).

Differential
  • Hepatic adenoma (no hepatobiliary uptake)
  • Fibrolamellar HCC (scar typically non-enhancing, no hepatobiliary uptake)
Next Step

Hepatobiliary-phase MRI is often diagnostic; no treatment needed for classic FNH.

4. Hepatic adenoma

Variable

Benign liver tumor associated with oral contraceptives/anabolic steroids, with bleeding and rare malignant transformation risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous signal (may contain fat or hemorrhage), arterial hyperenhancement without hepatobiliary contrast uptake, and washout can mimic HCC.

Differential
  • FNH
  • Well-differentiated HCC
  • Hypervascular metastasis
Next Step

Subtype characterization (inflammatory, HNF1α-mutated, beta-catenin-activated) guides risk; resection considered for lesions >5 cm or in males.

5. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), LI-RADS

Concerning

Primary liver malignancy, typically arising in a cirrhotic liver.

Imaging Characteristics

Arterial phase hyperenhancement with portal venous/delayed washout and capsule appearance (LR-5 criteria); diffusion restriction and T2 mild hyperintensity are supportive features.

Differential
  • Dysplastic nodule (LR-3/4, lacks full washout+capsule)
  • Hemangioma
  • Hypervascular metastasis
Next Step

Hepatology/oncology referral; LI-RADS categorization guides need for biopsy vs. treatment.

6. Hepatic metastases

Concerning

Secondary malignant liver deposits, the most common hepatic malignancy overall.

Imaging Characteristics

Variable signal; classic 'target sign' with restricted diffusion centrally and T2 hyperintense rim; hypervascular primaries (neuroendocrine, renal, thyroid) show arterial hyperenhancement.

Differential
  • Multifocal HCC
  • Abscesses
  • Biliary hamartomas
Next Step

Correlate with known primary; oncology referral for staging.

7. Hepatic steatosis (in/out-of-phase signal drop)

Variable

Fat accumulation within hepatocytes, quantifiable with chemical shift imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal loss on out-of-phase compared to in-phase gradient-echo images, diffuse or geographic; proton density fat fraction sequences can quantify percentage.

Differential
  • Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
  • Alcohol-related liver disease
  • Focal fat/focal sparing (geographic, non-mass-like, no vessel displacement)
Next Step

Correlate with metabolic risk factors; hepatology referral if significant fibrosis suspected.

8. Hepatic iron overload (hemochromatosis)

Concerning

Excess iron deposition in the liver, hereditary or secondary (transfusional).

Imaging Characteristics

Progressive signal loss on T2*/gradient-echo sequences with increasing echo time; R2* mapping quantifies liver iron concentration.

Differential
  • Hereditary hemochromatosis
  • Secondary/transfusional iron overload
Next Step

Hepatology referral; correlate with ferritin/transferrin saturation and consider genetic testing.

9. Cirrhosis, MRI features

Concerning

Chronic liver disease with fibrosis and architectural distortion.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular hepatic contour, caudate/lateral segment hypertrophy with right lobe atrophy, regenerative/dysplastic nodules, and signs of portal hypertension (varices, splenomegaly, ascites).

Differential
  • Various etiologies (viral, alcohol, NASH, autoimmune)
Next Step

HCC surveillance per guidelines; hepatology referral.

10. Pyogenic hepatic abscess

Concerning

Bacterial liver infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense collection with central diffusion restriction and surrounding edema; may show a 'cluster sign' of adjacent smaller abscesses.

Differential
  • Necrotic metastasis/HCC
  • Amebic abscess
  • Infected cyst
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics; search for biliary/portal source.

11. Budd-Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein thrombosis)

Concerning

Obstruction of hepatic venous outflow, from thrombosis or web.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-visualization or narrowing of hepatic veins, caudate lobe hypertrophy (preserved separate venous drainage), and a heterogeneous, mottled 'nutmeg' enhancement pattern.

Differential
  • Hypercoagulable states
  • Myeloproliferative disorders
  • Membranous webs (more common in some regions)
Next Step

Hepatology/hematology referral; anticoagulation and evaluation for underlying hypercoagulable disorder.

12. Biliary hamartomas (von Meyenburg complexes)

Benign

Benign clusters of malformed bile ducts, incidental and numerous.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple tiny (<1.5 cm), T2-hyperintense, non-enhancing cystic foci scattered throughout the liver without communication with the biliary tree.

Differential
  • Simple hepatic cysts (larger, fewer)
  • Miliary metastases (would show enhancement/diffusion restriction)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for classic appearance.

Biliary Tree / MRCP

8 findings

1. Choledocholithiasis (MRCP)

Concerning

Gallstone within the common bile duct, well demonstrated on heavily T2-weighted MRCP sequences.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded, low-signal filling defect within the T2-hyperintense bile duct lumen.

Differential
  • Sludge/debris (less well-defined)
  • Ampullary or bile duct tumor
Next Step

ERCP for stone extraction; correlate with liver enzymes.

2. Biliary ductal dilation, cause identified

Concerning

Widened bile ducts with an identifiable obstructing lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic ducts proximal to a discrete stone, stricture, or mass.

Differential
  • Choledocholithiasis
  • Pancreatic head mass
  • Cholangiocarcinoma
  • Benign post-surgical stricture
Next Step

Characterize the level and cause; ERCP or surgical referral as indicated.

3. Cholangiocarcinoma (including Klatskin tumor)

Concerning

Malignancy of the bile duct epithelium.

Imaging Characteristics

Infiltrative stricture with upstream ductal dilation; intrahepatic mass-forming type shows delayed peripheral enhancement with capsular retraction, hilar (Klatskin) type shows confluent hepatic duct involvement.

Differential
  • Benign stricture
  • Hepatic metastasis (mass-forming type)
  • IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis
Next Step

Hepatobiliary surgery/oncology referral; MRCP for staging.

4. Primary sclerosing cholangitis

Concerning

Chronic cholestatic disease causing multifocal biliary strictures, associated with inflammatory bowel disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Multifocal strictures alternating with mild dilations producing a 'beaded' appearance of the intrahepatic and/or extrahepatic ducts on MRCP.

Differential
  • IgG4-related sclerosing cholangitis
  • Secondary sclerosing cholangitis (prior surgery/ischemia)
Next Step

GI/hepatology referral; increased cholangiocarcinoma and colorectal cancer surveillance needs.

5. Pancreas divisum

Benign

Congenital failure of fusion of the dorsal and ventral pancreatic ducts, the most common pancreatic congenital variant.

Imaging Characteristics

Dorsal duct (of Santorini) draining independently through the minor papilla, crossing anterior to the common bile duct, best seen on MRCP.

Differential
  • Normal fused duct anatomy
Next Step

Usually incidental; can be a contributing factor in recurrent pancreatitis in a subset of patients.

6. Choledochal cyst

Variable

Congenital cystic dilation of the bile duct, with malignant potential if untreated.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform or saccular dilation of the extrahepatic (or intrahepatic, in Caroli disease) bile duct; classified by Todani type.

Differential
  • Simple biliary ductal dilation from obstruction
  • Caroli disease (intrahepatic type)
Next Step

Surgical referral given malignant transformation risk with long-standing disease.

7. Biloma

Variable

Encapsulated bile collection outside the biliary tree, from surgical or traumatic bile duct injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense fluid collection outside the expected ductal anatomy, often near a surgical bed.

Differential
  • Post-surgical/traumatic bile leak
  • Infected biloma (if rim-enhancing/complex)
Next Step

IR drainage and identification of the leak source; correlate with recent hepatobiliary surgery/trauma.

8. Caroli disease

Variable

Congenital saccular dilation of the intrahepatic bile ducts.

Imaging Characteristics

Multifocal cystic intrahepatic ductal dilations with the characteristic 'central dot sign' (a portal vein radicle surrounded by dilated duct on enhanced imaging).

Differential
  • Multiple hepatic cysts (do not communicate with ducts)
  • Recurrent pyogenic cholangitis
Next Step

GI/hepatology referral; associated with cholangitis risk and, in Caroli syndrome, congenital hepatic fibrosis.

Pancreas

8 findings

1. Acute pancreatitis, MRI

Concerning

Pancreatic inflammation, assessed on MRI when CT is contraindicated or for better fluid/necrosis characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Pancreatic enlargement with T2-hyperintense peripancreatic fluid/edema; necrosis appears as non-enhancing parenchyma, well shown on contrast-enhanced sequences.

Differential
  • Interstitial edematous pancreatitis
  • Necrotizing pancreatitis
Next Step

Supportive care; identify etiology (gallstones, alcohol, triglycerides).

2. Pancreatic pseudocyst

Variable

Encapsulated peripancreatic fluid collection, a late complication of pancreatitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Round/oval, uniformly T2-hyperintense fluid collection with a well-defined, non-epithelialized wall, lacking internal solid debris.

Differential
  • Walled-off necrosis (contains solid debris, more heterogeneous)
  • Cystic pancreatic neoplasm
Next Step

Observation if asymptomatic; drainage if infected or symptomatic.

3. Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma

Concerning

Most common pancreatic malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-hypointense, hypoenhancing mass on the pancreatic parenchymal phase relative to normal pancreas, often with upstream pancreatic and biliary ductal dilation ('double duct sign') and vascular encasement.

Differential
  • Focal (mass-forming) pancreatitis
  • Autoimmune pancreatitis
  • Neuroendocrine tumor (hyperenhancing, key differentiator)
Next Step

Oncology/surgery referral for resectability staging.

4. Intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm (IPMN)

Variable

Mucin-producing cystic pancreatic tumor, well characterized by MRI/MRCP.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic, T2-hyperintense lesion communicating with the pancreatic duct, main-duct (higher risk) or branch-duct (lower risk) type; worrisome features include mural nodules and main duct dilation.

Differential
  • Side-branch IPMN (lower risk)
  • Main-duct IPMN (higher risk)
  • Mucinous cystic neoplasm (no ductal communication)
  • Serous cystadenoma
Next Step

Surveillance vs. resection per current consensus guidelines based on size and worrisome/high-risk features.

5. Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor

Variable

Tumor of pancreatic islet cells, functional or non-functional.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense mass with avid arterial phase hyperenhancement, distinguishing it from the hypovascular ductal adenocarcinoma; may restrict diffusion.

Differential
  • Hypervascular metastasis to pancreas
  • Accessory spleen (if at the tail)
  • Solid pseudopapillary neoplasm
Next Step

Endocrine/oncology referral; correlate with functional hormone symptoms.

6. Autoimmune pancreatitis

Variable

IgG4-related or idiopathic inflammatory pancreatic disease that can mimic malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse ('sausage-shaped') pancreatic enlargement with a T2-hypointense peripheral rim ('capsule-like rim sign') and loss of normal lobulation; may be focal and mimic a mass.

Differential
  • Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (key differential, especially with focal presentation)
  • Chronic pancreatitis
Next Step

Correlate with IgG4 levels and other organ involvement; steroid-responsive, and biopsy if malignancy cannot be excluded.

7. Groove pancreatitis

Variable

Focal chronic pancreatitis affecting the groove between the pancreatic head, duodenum, and common bile duct.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, T2-variable soft tissue in the pancreaticoduodenal groove, often with duodenal wall thickening and cystic change; can closely mimic pancreatic head carcinoma.

Differential
  • Pancreatic head adenocarcinoma (key differential)
  • Duodenal pathology
Next Step

Correlate with alcohol use history; multidisciplinary review given overlap with malignancy on imaging.

8. Serous cystadenoma

Benign

Benign pancreatic cystic neoplasm composed of numerous small microcysts.

Imaging Characteristics

'Microcystic' or 'honeycomb' cluster of numerous small cysts, classically with a central stellate scar that may calcify.

Differential
  • IPMN
  • Mucinous cystic neoplasm
Next Step

Typically observed if classic appearance and asymptomatic; low malignant potential.

Kidneys & Adrenal

10 findings

1. Renal cell carcinoma, MRI characterization

Concerning

Primary renal malignancy, often further characterized by MRI when CT is indeterminate or contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid, enhancing renal mass; MRI is useful for detecting subtle enhancement, assessing venous invasion (renal vein/IVC), and characterizing lesions in patients who cannot receive iodinated contrast.

Differential
  • Oncocytoma (benign, can mimic RCC)
  • Angiomyolipoma without visible fat
  • Complex cyst
Next Step

Urology/oncology referral for staging and surgical planning.

2. Angiomyolipoma, fat-poor (chemical shift)

Variable

AML variant lacking enough macroscopic fat for confident CT diagnosis, better characterized by MRI chemical shift imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal drop on opposed-phase compared to in-phase imaging indicating microscopic fat, though this overlaps somewhat with clear cell RCC (which can also show intravoxel fat).

Differential
  • Fat-poor AML
  • Clear cell RCC (can show overlapping signal drop)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary review; biopsy or resection if diagnosis remains uncertain.

3. Complex renal cyst, Bosniak MRI criteria

Variable

Cystic renal lesion assessed for malignant potential using MRI-adapted Bosniak criteria.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of septa thickness/number, wall thickening, and enhancement (subtracted imaging improves detection of subtle enhancement compared to CT).

Differential
  • Bosniak II (benign, minimal complexity)
  • Bosniak III (indeterminate, ~50% malignant)
  • Bosniak IV (predominantly malignant, enhancing solid components)
Next Step

Bosniak III-IV warrants urologic referral for resection or biopsy consideration.

4. Adrenal adenoma, chemical shift MRI

Benign

Benign adrenal nodule characterized using in-phase/opposed-phase imaging to detect intracellular lipid.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal drop on opposed-phase compared to in-phase images due to intracellular lipid content, diagnostic of a lipid-rich adenoma.

Differential
  • Lipid-poor adenoma (no signal drop, indeterminate)
  • Metastasis (no signal drop)
  • Pheochromocytoma (no signal drop, T2 bright)
Next Step

No further workup needed for a classic adenoma with signal drop.

5. Pheochromocytoma, MRI

Concerning

Catecholamine-secreting adrenal medullary tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Classic 'light bulb' bright T2 signal, no signal drop on chemical shift imaging, and avid enhancement.

Differential
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma
  • Lipid-poor adenoma
  • Adrenal metastasis
Next Step

Biochemical testing (plasma/urine metanephrines) before biopsy given hypertensive crisis risk.

6. Adrenal metastasis, MRI

Concerning

Secondary malignant adrenal deposit.

Imaging Characteristics

No chemical shift signal drop, variable T2 signal (often intermediate-to-high), and heterogeneous enhancement, particularly relevant in a patient with known extra-adrenal malignancy.

Differential
  • Pheochromocytoma
  • Lipid-poor adenoma
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma
Next Step

Correlate with known primary malignancy; biopsy if it will change staging/management.

7. Renal vein thrombosis, MRI

Concerning

Occlusion of the renal vein, from hypercoagulability, nephrotic syndrome, or tumor thrombus.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the renal vein on contrast-enhanced sequences; tumor thrombus (from RCC) shows internal enhancement and vessel expansion, unlike bland thrombus.

Differential
  • Bland thrombus (hypercoagulable state, nephrotic syndrome, dehydration)
  • Tumor thrombus from RCC
Next Step

Vascular/nephrology consultation; anticoagulation for bland thrombus.

8. Pyelonephritis, MRI

Concerning

Renal parenchymal infection, occasionally characterized by MRI when CT is contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped or striated areas of decreased enhancement radiating from the papilla to cortex, with perinephric T2-hyperintense stranding.

Differential
  • Renal infarct (sharper margins)
  • Lymphoma infiltration (rare)
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to culture; assess for abscess if not improving.

9. Medullary sponge kidney

Benign

Congenital dilation of the medullary/papillary collecting ducts, associated with stone formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic dilation of the collecting ducts within the renal pyramids, often with a 'paintbrush' appearance on excretory-phase imaging; frequently associated with medullary nephrocalcinosis.

Differential
  • Normal variant medullary changes
  • Nephrocalcinosis from other causes
Next Step

Usually incidental; correlate with recurrent stone disease history.

10. Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease

Concerning

Hereditary disorder causing progressive replacement of renal parenchyma by numerous cysts.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral, massively enlarged kidneys replaced by innumerable cysts of varying signal intensity (some hemorrhagic/proteinaceous, appearing T1 bright); often with associated hepatic cysts.

Differential
  • Acquired cystic kidney disease (dialysis-related, kidneys usually small, not enlarged)
  • Multiple simple cysts (fewer, normal-sized kidney)
Next Step

Nephrology referral; screening for intracranial aneurysms in appropriate clinical context.

Uterus & Endometrium

10 findings

1. Uterine fibroid (leiomyoma), MRI characterization

Benign

Common benign smooth muscle tumor, MRI is the modality of choice for mapping prior to fibroid-directed therapy.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, T2-hypointense mass (relative to myometrium) distorting the uterine contour; degenerating fibroids show variable, heterogeneous signal.

Differential
  • Adenomyosis (ill-defined, diffuse)
  • Leiomyosarcoma (rare; large size, rapid growth, and high-signal necrotic areas raise concern)
Next Step

MRI mapping (FIGO type, size, location) guides candidacy for uterine artery embolization or myomectomy.

2. Adenomyosis

Benign

Ectopic endometrial tissue within the myometrium, a common cause of dysmenorrhea/menorrhagia.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or focal thickening of the junctional zone (>12 mm) with ill-defined margins and scattered tiny T2-hyperintense foci (ectopic endometrial glands).

Differential
  • Uterine fibroid (well-circumscribed, unlike adenomyosis)
  • Normal junctional zone variant
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; gynecology referral for management options.

3. Endometrial cancer, MRI staging

Concerning

Endometrial malignancy, with MRI used primarily for local staging (depth of myometrial invasion).

Imaging Characteristics

Endometrial mass with assessment of the depth of myometrial invasion (less than vs. greater than 50%) and cervical stromal involvement, both of which affect FIGO stage.

Differential
  • Endometrial polyp
  • Endometrial hyperplasia
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral for staging and surgical planning.

4. Endometrial polyp

Benign

Focal benign endometrial overgrowth.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-intermediate signal mass within the endometrial cavity, often with a visible feeding vessel on enhanced imaging.

Differential
  • Submucosal fibroid (typically more T2-hypointense)
  • Focal endometrial hyperplasia
Next Step

Gynecology referral for hysteroscopic resection if symptomatic.

5. Müllerian duct anomaly (septate/bicornuate uterus)

Benign

Congenital uterine malformation from abnormal Müllerian duct fusion/resorption.

Imaging Characteristics

Septate uterus: normal external fundal contour with a T2-hypointense fibrous (or intermediate muscular) septum dividing the cavity. Bicornuate uterus: external fundal cleft with two divergent horns.

Differential
  • Septate uterus (normal external contour, treatable hysteroscopically)
  • Bicornuate uterus (external fundal cleft, distinguishing feature)
  • Arcuate uterus (minimal fundal indentation, usually not clinically significant)
Next Step

Correlate with reproductive history; gynecology referral for symptomatic anomalies.

6. Cervical cancer, MRI staging

Concerning

Cervical malignancy, with MRI used to assess local extent including parametrial invasion.

Imaging Characteristics

Cervical stromal mass, often T2-hyperintense relative to the normally hypointense cervical stroma; assessment of parametrial invasion (loss of the hypointense stromal ring) is critical for staging.

Differential
  • Cervical polyp/benign lesion
  • Nabothian cyst (benign, incidental)
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral for staging.

7. Asherman syndrome (intrauterine adhesions)

Variable

Intrauterine synechiae, often following instrumentation, causing infertility or menstrual abnormalities.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, low-signal bands crossing the endometrial cavity, disrupting the normal endometrial stripe.

Differential
  • Post-procedural adhesions
  • Endometrial scarring from infection
Next Step

Gynecology referral; hysteroscopy for diagnosis and lysis of adhesions.

8. IUD position, MRI

Benign

Intrauterine device visualized incidentally on pelvic MRI (appears as a signal void).

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or T-shaped signal void within the endometrial cavity in expected midline position.

Differential
  • Normally positioned IUD
  • Malpositioned/perforated IUD (signal void outside expected cavity)
Next Step

No action if normally positioned; gynecology referral if malposition is identified.

9. Endometrial hyperplasia

Variable

Thickened, proliferative endometrium, a precursor lesion for endometrial cancer in some cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely thickened endometrial stripe with preserved junctional zone, without a discrete focal mass (helping distinguish from cancer, though overlap exists).

Differential
  • Normal proliferative endometrium (phase-dependent)
  • Endometrial polyp
  • Endometrial cancer
Next Step

Gynecology referral for endometrial sampling, especially in postmenopausal bleeding.

10. Leiomyosarcoma

Concerning

Rare malignant smooth muscle tumor of the uterus, difficult to distinguish from a benign fibroid preoperatively.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, rapidly growing mass with ill-defined margins, heterogeneous high T2 signal from necrosis/hemorrhage, and restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Degenerating/atypical fibroid (overlapping features, key diagnostic challenge)
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral, particularly with rapid growth or atypical features; avoid morcellation if suspected.

Ovaries & Adnexa

8 findings

1. Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid)

Benign

Common benign germ cell tumor containing multiple tissue types.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic mass with a fat-containing component (signal drop on fat-suppressed sequences) and often a mural (Rokitansky) nodule; chemical shift artifact at the fat-fluid interface is characteristic.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic cyst (no fat suppression signal drop)
  • Immature teratoma (concerning, less fat-predominant, more solid)
Next Step

Gynecology referral for surgical removal given torsion risk, particularly for larger lesions.

2. Endometrioma

Variable

Ovarian cyst containing old blood from endometriosis.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-hyperintense, T2-hypointense ('shading') cystic lesion, often with a thick wall and sometimes multiple/bilateral.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic cyst (typically resolves on follow-up, endometrioma persists)
Next Step

Gynecology referral for symptom management; malignant transformation is rare but increases with long-standing disease.

3. Hemorrhagic ovarian cyst

Benign

Cyst containing blood products from ovulation or corpus luteum bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Variable T1/T2 signal depending on blood product age, often with a lace-like internal reticular pattern and no enhancing solid component.

Differential
  • Endometrioma (persists on follow-up)
  • Hemorrhage into a neoplasm (less common)
Next Step

Usually resolves; follow-up ultrasound to confirm resolution if uncertain.

4. Ovarian cystadenoma (serous/mucinous)

Benign

Common benign ovarian epithelial tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Serous: unilocular or few septations, simple fluid signal. Mucinous: multilocular with varying signal intensity between locules reflecting differing mucin content.

Differential
  • Cystadenocarcinoma (solid components/septal nodularity raise concern)
  • Simple functional cyst
Next Step

Gynecology referral for management; features of complexity warrant oncology involvement.

5. Ovarian malignancy, suspicious features

Concerning

Adnexal mass with imaging characteristics concerning for malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid components, thick irregular septations, papillary projections with diffusion restriction and early enhancement, often with ascites or peritoneal implants.

Differential
  • Borderline ovarian tumor
  • Primary ovarian carcinoma
  • Metastasis to ovary (Krukenberg tumor)
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral; CA-125 and multidisciplinary staging workup.

6. Ovarian torsion, MRI

Concerning

Twisting of the ovary on its vascular pedicle.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, edematous ovary with peripherally displaced follicles, often with an underlying predisposing mass (commonly a dermoid); a twisted vascular pedicle may be visible.

Differential
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
  • Hemorrhagic cyst
Next Step

Gynecologic surgical emergency; ultrasound with Doppler remains first-line when torsion is clinically suspected.

7. Hydrosalpinx

Benign

Fluid-distended, obstructed fallopian tube, often from prior pelvic infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Tubular, T2-hyperintense adnexal structure with incomplete septations ('cogwheel' or 'beads on a string' sign from folded mucosal folds), distinct from an ovarian cystic mass.

Differential
  • Ovarian cystadenoma (round rather than tubular)
  • Paratubal cyst
Next Step

Gynecology referral, particularly relevant to fertility evaluation.

8. Polycystic ovarian morphology

Benign

Imaging pattern associated with (but not diagnostic alone of) polycystic ovary syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged ovaries with numerous small (2-9 mm) peripherally arranged follicles ('string of pearls') and increased central stroma.

Differential
  • Normal multifollicular ovaries (fewer, less peripherally arranged follicles)
  • PCOS (requires clinical/biochemical correlation, not imaging alone)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical and laboratory criteria (Rotterdam criteria); endocrinology/gynecology referral.

Prostate & Male Pelvis

8 findings

1. PI-RADS lesion, peripheral zone

Concerning

Suspicious prostate lesion in the peripheral zone on multiparametric MRI.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, round/oval, markedly hypointense lesion on T2 with corresponding restricted diffusion (low ADC, high b-value signal); PI-RADS category assigned primarily by DWI in this zone.

Differential
  • Prostatitis (can mimic focal T2 hypointensity)
  • Post-biopsy hemorrhage (correlate with recent biopsy history)
  • Benign stromal hyperplasia nodule (transition zone, different zone)
Next Step

PI-RADS ≥3 warrants urology referral for consideration of targeted biopsy.

2. PI-RADS lesion, transition zone

Concerning

Suspicious prostate lesion in the transition zone, scored primarily on T2 morphology.

Imaging Characteristics

Lenticular or non-circumscribed, homogeneously hypointense ('erased charcoal sign') lesion distinct from the typical nodular BPH pattern; DWI is a secondary sequence in this zone.

Differential
  • Benign prostatic hyperplasia nodule (well-circumscribed, encapsulated)
  • Stromal BPH nodule with atypical features
Next Step

PI-RADS ≥3 warrants urology referral for targeted biopsy consideration.

3. Benign prostatic hyperplasia, zonal enlargement

Benign

Common age-related enlargement of the transition zone.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged transition zone with well-circumscribed, encapsulated nodules of mixed glandular/stromal signal, compressing the peripheral zone.

Differential
  • Normal transition zone
  • Transition zone cancer (less well-circumscribed)
Next Step

Correlate with PSA and urinary symptoms; no oncologic workup needed for classic BPH nodules.

4. Prostatitis

Variable

Prostate gland inflammation, which can mimic malignancy on imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or wedge-shaped T2 hypointensity, often without the focal round morphology or marked diffusion restriction of cancer; may improve on follow-up after treatment.

Differential
  • Prostate cancer (key differential, imaging overlap is common)
  • Post-biopsy change
Next Step

Correlate with clinical symptoms/urinalysis; consider follow-up MRI after treatment if diagnosis uncertain.

5. Extraprostatic extension / seminal vesicle invasion

Concerning

Local spread of prostate cancer beyond the gland capsule.

Imaging Characteristics

Capsular bulge, irregularity, or frank tumor signal extending through the capsule; seminal vesicle invasion shows abnormal low T2 signal replacing normal seminal vesicle architecture.

Differential
  • Organ-confined disease (no capsular/seminal vesicle involvement)
Next Step

Affects staging and treatment planning; urologic oncology referral.

6. Prostate cancer, local recurrence post-treatment

Concerning

Recurrent tumor after radical prostatectomy or radiation therapy.

Imaging Characteristics

Post-prostatectomy: nodular soft tissue at the vesicourethral anastomosis with restricted diffusion and early enhancement. Post-radiation: focal T2 hypointensity with restricted diffusion in a gland that is otherwise diffusely low-signal from treatment effect.

Differential
  • Post-treatment fibrosis/granulation tissue (no diffusion restriction/enhancement)
Next Step

Correlate with rising PSA; urologic oncology referral.

7. Penile Peyronie plaque

Benign

Fibrous plaque within the tunica albuginea causing penile curvature.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, low-signal thickening of the tunica albuginea, most often dorsal.

Differential
  • Normal tunical variant
Next Step

Urology referral if causing symptomatic curvature/pain affecting function.

8. Testicular mass, MRI characterization

Concerning

Testicular lesion further characterized by MRI when ultrasound is indeterminate.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid, enhancing intratesticular mass; MRI adds value in equivocal ultrasound cases by assessing enhancement and signal characteristics, though ultrasound remains first-line.

Differential
  • Germ cell tumor (most common in young men)
  • Lymphoma/leukemia infiltration
  • Benign lesion (rare, e.g., epidermoid cyst with characteristic onion-skin appearance)
Next Step

Urology referral; tumor markers (AFP, beta-hCG, LDH) and likely orchiectomy for definitive diagnosis of a solid mass.

Rectum & Anus

6 findings

1. Rectal cancer, T-staging (mesorectal fascia)

Concerning

Rectal malignancy, with MRI as the primary modality for local staging.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of tumor depth through the rectal wall layers and, critically, the distance from the tumor (or a suspicious node/tumor deposit) to the mesorectal fascia (circumferential resection margin).

Differential
  • Benign rectal polyp/adenoma
  • Inflammatory rectal wall thickening
Next Step

Colorectal surgery/oncology referral; MRI staging guides neoadjuvant therapy decisions.

2. Anal fistula, Crohn-related (Parks classification)

Concerning

Abnormal tract connecting the anal canal to the perianal skin, common in Crohn disease.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense/STIR-bright tract traversing the anal sphincter complex, classified by its relationship to the sphincters (intersphincteric, transsphincteric, suprasphincteric, extrasphincteric).

Differential
  • Simple cryptoglandular fistula
  • Crohn-associated complex fistula
Next Step

Colorectal surgery/GI referral; classification guides surgical vs. medical (biologic) management.

3. Perianal/perirectal abscess

Concerning

Localized perianal infection, often associated with a fistula.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense fluid collection in the perianal or ischiorectal space.

Differential
  • Simple abscess
  • Abscess associated with an underlying fistula tract
Next Step

Surgical drainage; identify any associated fistula for definitive management.

4. Anal cancer

Concerning

Malignancy of the anal canal, often HPV-associated.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential or focal mass involving the anal canal with variable T2 signal and restricted diffusion; assess sphincter involvement and nodal spread.

Differential
  • Anal fissure/hemorrhoid (benign, no discrete mass)
  • Anal condyloma
Next Step

Oncology referral; MRI staging to guide chemoradiation planning.

5. Rectal cancer, nodal staging

Concerning

Assessment of mesorectal and lateral pelvic lymph nodes for metastatic involvement in rectal cancer.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodes assessed by size, irregular border, and heterogeneous internal signal rather than size threshold alone, given the limitations of size-based criteria.

Differential
  • Reactive/benign lymph node
  • Metastatic lymph node
Next Step

Affects staging and neoadjuvant treatment planning; colorectal oncology referral.

6. Post-treatment rectal fibrosis vs. recurrence

Variable

Distinguishing treatment-related change from tumor recurrence after chemoradiation for rectal cancer.

Imaging Characteristics

Fibrosis: low T2 signal, no diffusion restriction. Recurrence: intermediate-to-high T2 signal with restricted diffusion and early enhancement.

Differential
  • Post-treatment fibrosis (benign)
  • Local tumor recurrence
Next Step

Serial MRI and correlation with tumor markers/endoscopy; oncology referral if recurrence suspected.

Bowel / MR Enterography

6 findings

1. Active Crohn disease (MR enterography)

Concerning

Active transmural small bowel inflammation, assessed with dedicated MR enterography protocols.

Imaging Characteristics

Bowel wall thickening with mural hyperenhancement and stratification, restricted diffusion, and engorged vasa recta ('comb sign'); mesenteric fat proliferation ('creeping fat') is often present.

Differential
  • Infectious enteritis
  • Ischemia
  • Lymphoma
Next Step

GI consultation for disease activity assessment and treatment escalation.

2. Fibrostenotic Crohn stricture

Concerning

Chronic, fibrotic narrowing of the bowel from long-standing Crohn disease, distinct from active inflammatory narrowing.

Imaging Characteristics

Luminal narrowing with wall thickening but relatively less enhancement/diffusion restriction than active disease, often with upstream bowel dilation.

Differential
  • Active inflammatory stricture (more enhancement/edema)
  • Malignant stricture (rare, consider especially with long-standing disease)
Next Step

GI/surgical referral; distinguishing fibrotic from inflammatory stricture guides medical vs. surgical/endoscopic management.

3. Enteric fistula (Crohn-related)

Concerning

Abnormal communication between bowel loops or bowel and adjacent structures (bladder, vagina, skin), a penetrating Crohn complication.

Imaging Characteristics

Tract of T2-hyperintense signal connecting bowel to an adjacent structure, often with surrounding inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Enteroenteric fistula
  • Enterovesical/enterovaginal fistula
  • Enterocutaneous fistula
Next Step

Surgical/GI referral for multidisciplinary management.

4. Bowel-related abscess (Crohn-related)

Concerning

Localized abscess from penetrating bowel disease, most often Crohn.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense fluid collection adjacent to diseased bowel, sometimes containing gas.

Differential
  • Diverticular abscess
  • Post-surgical abscess
Next Step

IR drainage and antibiotics; GI/surgical referral for underlying disease management.

5. Mesenteric comb sign

Concerning

Engorged, prominent vasa recta perpendicular to the bowel wall, a marker of active inflammatory bowel disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Series of closely spaced, linear enhancing vessels extending from the mesenteric border toward an inflamed bowel segment, resembling the teeth of a comb.

Differential
  • Active Crohn disease (most classic association)
  • Other active inflammatory bowel processes
Next Step

Correlate with overall disease activity assessment; GI referral.

6. Chronic fibrotic vs. active inflammatory bowel wall (Crohn)

Variable

Distinguishing reversible inflammation from irreversible fibrosis in Crohn disease, important for treatment selection.

Imaging Characteristics

Active: high T2 signal, strong mural enhancement, diffusion restriction. Chronic/fibrotic: lower T2 signal, milder enhancement, bowel wall thinning/narrowing without significant edema.

Differential
  • Predominantly active inflammatory disease (favors medical therapy)
  • Predominantly fibrotic disease (favors endoscopic/surgical therapy)
Next Step

GI/surgical multidisciplinary discussion to guide management strategy.

Vascular & Retroperitoneum

4 findings

1. Abdominal aortic aneurysm, MRA

Concerning

Aortic dilation ≥3 cm assessed by MR angiography, an alternative when iodinated contrast is contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter ≥3 cm with assessment of mural thrombus and involvement of visceral/iliac vessels.

Differential
  • Ectasia (mild diffuse dilation <3 cm)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral per size/growth-based repair thresholds.

2. Mesenteric ischemia, MRI/MRA

Concerning

Bowel ischemia from mesenteric vascular compromise, assessed by MRA when CT is contraindicated.

Imaging Characteristics

Stenosis or occlusion of the SMA/celiac axis on MRA, with bowel wall changes (thinning acutely, thickening later) on corresponding sequences.

Differential
  • Arterial embolism/thrombosis
  • Venous thrombosis
  • Nonocclusive ischemia (low-flow states)
Next Step

Time-critical; surgical/IR consultation.

3. Portal vein thrombosis, MRI

Concerning

Clot within the portal venous system.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the portal vein on contrast-enhanced sequences; bland thrombus does not enhance, while tumor thrombus (from HCC) shows internal enhancement.

Differential
  • Bland thrombus (cirrhosis, hypercoagulable state)
  • Tumor thrombus (HCC)
  • Pylephlebitis (septic thrombophlebitis)
Next Step

Anticoagulation for bland thrombus; treat underlying source if septic.

4. Retroperitoneal fibrosis, MRI

Concerning

Fibro-inflammatory process encasing retroperitoneal structures, often the ureters and great vessels.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue rind around the infrarenal aorta, variable T2 signal depending on disease activity (higher signal in active/inflammatory phase, lower in chronic fibrotic phase), medially deviating the ureters.

Differential
  • IgG4-related disease
  • Malignancy-related desmoplastic reaction (tends to displace rather than encase structures)
Next Step

Urology/rheumatology referral; biopsy may be needed to exclude malignancy.

Liver & Biliary (RUQ)

16 findings

1. Cholelithiasis

Variable

Gallstones within the gallbladder lumen, the primary indication for RUQ ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic, mobile, dependent structure(s) within the gallbladder lumen with posterior acoustic shadowing.

Differential
  • Symptomatic vs. asymptomatic cholelithiasis
  • Gallbladder polyp (non-mobile, non-shadowing)
  • Adenomyomatosis
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; surgical referral if symptomatic.

2. Acute cholecystitis

Concerning

Gallbladder inflammation, most often from cystic duct obstruction by a stone.

Imaging Characteristics

Gallbladder wall thickening (>3 mm), pericholecystic fluid, gallbladder distension, and a positive sonographic Murphy sign (maximal tenderness over the visualized gallbladder).

Differential
  • Gallbladder wall thickening from other causes (hepatitis, hypoalbuminemia, heart failure)
  • Adenomyomatosis
Next Step

Surgical consultation for cholecystectomy; percutaneous cholecystostomy if poor surgical candidate.

3. Gallbladder polyp

Variable

Focal projection from the gallbladder wall into the lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-mobile, non-shadowing echogenic focus attached to the gallbladder wall.

Differential
  • Cholesterol polyp (most common, benign)
  • Adenomyomatous polyp
  • True adenoma/early carcinoma (larger size raises concern)
Next Step

Surveillance ultrasound based on size (generally larger polyps or growth over time warrant cholecystectomy).

4. Adenomyomatosis

Benign

Benign hyperplastic gallbladder wall condition with cholesterol-laden Rokitansky-Aschoff sinuses.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse gallbladder wall thickening with characteristic echogenic foci and 'comet-tail' reverberation artifacts from cholesterol crystals within the wall sinuses.

Differential
  • Acute cholecystitis
  • Gallbladder carcinoma (rare, more irregular/mass-like)
Next Step

No treatment needed for classic asymptomatic adenomyomatosis; cholecystectomy if symptomatic.

5. Choledocholithiasis

Concerning

Gallstone within the common bile duct.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic, shadowing focus within a dilated common bile duct.

Differential
  • Sludge/debris (non-shadowing)
  • Distal ductal stricture/mass
Next Step

MRCP or ERCP for confirmation and stone extraction.

6. Biliary ductal dilation

Concerning

Widening of the common bile duct and/or intrahepatic ducts.

Imaging Characteristics

Common bile duct diameter >6-8 mm (age- and post-cholecystectomy-adjusted), intrahepatic ducts visible extending toward the liver periphery when dilated.

Differential
  • Choledocholithiasis
  • Pancreatic head mass
  • Cholangiocarcinoma
  • Benign stricture
Next Step

MRCP to determine the level and cause of obstruction.

7. Hepatic steatosis, grading

Variable

Fat accumulation within the liver, graded semi-quantitatively by ultrasound appearance.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely increased hepatic echogenicity ('bright liver') relative to the renal cortex, with poor visualization of the diaphragm and portal vein walls in more severe grades (increasing posterior beam attenuation).

Differential
  • Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease
  • Alcohol-related liver disease
Next Step

Correlate with metabolic risk factors and liver enzymes; elastography or biopsy if fibrosis is a concern.

8. Cirrhosis, ultrasound features

Concerning

Chronic liver disease with fibrosis and architectural distortion.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular, irregular liver surface contour, coarsened/heterogeneous parenchymal echotexture, and signs of portal hypertension (splenomegaly, ascites, portal vein enlargement/reversed flow).

Differential
  • Various etiologies of chronic liver disease
Next Step

HCC surveillance per guidelines; hepatology referral.

9. Hepatic hemangioma, ultrasound

Benign

Most common benign liver tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, homogeneously hyperechoic lesion, typically without significant internal vascularity on color Doppler for classic small lesions.

Differential
  • Focal fatty sparing/infiltration
  • Hypervascular metastasis (atypical features)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic small hyperechoic hemangioma in a patient without malignancy history.

10. Hepatic cyst, simple

Benign

Common benign fluid-filled liver lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, well-circumscribed lesion with a thin/imperceptible wall, posterior acoustic enhancement, and no internal vascularity.

Differential
  • Biliary hamartoma (multiple, tiny)
  • Polycystic liver disease
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic simple cyst.

11. Hepatic abscess

Concerning

Bacterial or amebic liver infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, often hypoechoic collection with internal debris/septations and increased through-transmission; may show internal gas.

Differential
  • Necrotic metastasis/HCC
  • Infected cyst
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics; blood cultures.

12. Hepatocellular carcinoma, ultrasound

Concerning

Primary liver malignancy, often initially detected on surveillance ultrasound in cirrhotic patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Variable echogenicity mass (small lesions often hypoechoic, larger lesions heterogeneous with a mosaic pattern); vascular invasion (portal vein) should be assessed.

Differential
  • Regenerative/dysplastic nodule
  • Hemangioma
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI with LI-RADS categorization; hepatology/oncology referral.

13. Portal vein thrombosis, Doppler

Concerning

Clot within the portal venous system.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic material within the portal vein lumen with absent or reduced flow on color/spectral Doppler; cavernous transformation (collateral vessels) suggests chronic thrombosis.

Differential
  • Bland thrombus (cirrhosis, hypercoagulable state)
  • Tumor thrombus (pulsatile arterial-type flow within the thrombus on Doppler)
Next Step

Anticoagulation for bland thrombus; further characterization (CT/MRI) if tumor thrombus suspected.

14. Ascites

Variable

Free intraperitoneal fluid, readily detected by ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic (simple) or echogenic/septated (complex) fluid outlining bowel loops and in dependent recesses (Morison pouch, paracolic gutters, pelvis).

Differential
  • Cirrhosis/portal hypertension
  • Heart failure
  • Malignancy/carcinomatosis
  • Infection
Next Step

Correlate with underlying disease; diagnostic paracentesis if new onset or infection suspected.

15. Gallbladder sludge

Variable

Thickened, echogenic bile without discrete stones, often from stasis.

Imaging Characteristics

Low-level, non-shadowing echoes layering dependently within the gallbladder that shift slowly with patient position ('sludge ball' if organized).

Differential
  • Prolonged fasting/TPN-related stasis (benign)
  • Precursor to stone formation
  • Can mimic tumor if it fails to move with positioning (repeat scan to confirm mobility)
Next Step

Usually no action if asymptomatic; correlate with symptoms and consider follow-up if persistent.

16. Gallbladder wall thickening, nonspecific

Variable

Diffuse gallbladder wall thickening without other signs of acute cholecystitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Wall thickening without pericholecystic fluid or a positive sonographic Murphy sign; often more pronounced when the gallbladder is underdistended.

Differential
  • Hypoalbuminemia
  • Heart failure/volume overload
  • Hepatitis
  • Ascites
  • Underdistended gallbladder (pseudothickening)
Next Step

Correlate with albumin, liver function, and volume status rather than assuming cholecystitis.

Kidneys & Bladder

14 findings

1. Hydronephrosis, grading

Concerning

Dilation of the renal collecting system, graded by severity.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic splitting of the central renal sinus echo complex; graded mild (minimal calyceal dilation) to severe (marked calyceal/pelvic dilation with parenchymal thinning).

Differential
  • Obstructing stone
  • Extrinsic compression (mass, retroperitoneal fibrosis)
  • Vesicoureteral reflux
  • Overdistended bladder (transient, resolves after voiding)
Next Step

Identify the level and cause of obstruction; urology referral, especially urgent if signs of infection.

2. Nephrolithiasis, ultrasound

Variable

Kidney stone identified on renal ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic focus within the renal collecting system with posterior acoustic shadowing; the 'twinkle artifact' on color Doppler increases detection sensitivity for small stones.

Differential
  • Non-obstructing stone (incidental)
  • Obstructing stone with hydronephrosis
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms and degree of obstruction; CT for full characterization if needed.

3. Simple renal cyst

Benign

Extremely common benign fluid-filled renal lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, well-circumscribed, thin-walled lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement and no internal vascularity.

Differential
  • Complex cyst (septations, wall thickening, internal echoes)
  • Hydronephrosis (connected to the collecting system, cysts are not)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic simple cyst.

4. Complex renal cyst, ultrasound

Variable

Cystic renal lesion with septations, wall thickening, or internal echoes.

Imaging Characteristics

Internal septations, mural nodularity, or low-level internal echoes distinguishing it from a simple cyst.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic/infected cyst
  • Cystic renal cell carcinoma
Next Step

CT or MRI with Bosniak classification for further characterization.

5. Renal mass, solid

Concerning

Solid renal lesion identified on ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid, vascularized mass distinct from surrounding parenchyma on grayscale and color Doppler imaging.

Differential
  • Renal cell carcinoma
  • Angiomyolipoma (classically hyperechoic, though overlap exists)
  • Oncocytoma
Next Step

CT or MRI for further characterization; urology referral.

6. Pyelonephritis, ultrasound

Concerning

Renal parenchymal infection; ultrasound has limited sensitivity compared to CT.

Imaging Characteristics

May show renal enlargement and heterogeneous echotexture, though ultrasound is often normal in uncomplicated pyelonephritis.

Differential
  • Normal kidney (does not exclude pyelonephritis)
  • Renal abscess (if a discrete complex collection is seen)
Next Step

Clinical diagnosis primarily; CT if complications (abscess, obstruction) are suspected or the patient is not improving.

7. Renal/perinephric abscess, ultrasound

Concerning

Localized infected collection within or around the kidney.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, hypoechoic collection with internal debris, possibly with increased through-transmission.

Differential
  • Pyelonephritis without abscess
  • Infected renal cyst
Next Step

IR drainage for larger abscesses plus antibiotics; urology involvement.

8. Medical renal disease (increased cortical echogenicity)

Variable

Nonspecific ultrasound pattern reflecting parenchymal renal disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely increased renal cortical echogenicity relative to the liver/spleen, with or without loss of normal corticomedullary differentiation.

Differential
  • Chronic kidney disease (various etiologies)
  • Acute glomerulonephritis
  • Normal variant in some patients
Next Step

Correlate with renal function labs; nephrology referral if abnormal function.

9. Duplicated collecting system

Benign

Common congenital variant with two separate renal pelves from a single kidney.

Imaging Characteristics

Two distinct central echo complexes within a single renal outline, sometimes with a thin cortical bridge separating the upper and lower moieties.

Differential
  • Normal variant, usually incidental
Next Step

No action needed unless associated with obstruction or reflux.

10. Ureterocele

Variable

Cystic dilation of the distal ureter as it enters the bladder, often associated with a duplicated system.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin-walled, round, fluid-filled structure within the bladder lumen near the ureterovesical junction, sometimes showing a 'jet' of urine on color Doppler.

Differential
  • Simple ureterocele (often incidental)
  • Ureterocele causing obstruction of the associated moiety
Next Step

Urology referral, particularly if associated with hydronephrosis or recurrent UTI.

11. Bladder wall thickening

Variable

Diffuse or focal thickening of the bladder wall.

Imaging Characteristics

Wall thickness >3-5 mm (distention-dependent); diffuse thickening is often benign/reactive, while focal thickening is more concerning for a mass.

Differential
  • Underdistension (pseudothickening)
  • Chronic outlet obstruction/neurogenic bladder
  • Chronic cystitis
  • Bladder wall mass (if focal)
Next Step

Repeat with adequate distension if the only finding; cystoscopy referral if focal or persistent.

12. Bladder mass

Concerning

Focal, wall-based lesion projecting into the bladder lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Fixed, wall-based soft-tissue mass, often with internal vascularity on color Doppler, distinguishing it from a mobile, avascular clot.

Differential
  • Blood clot (mobile, avascular, changes with position)
  • Urothelial carcinoma
  • Benign polyp
Next Step

Urology referral for cystoscopy and biopsy.

13. Elevated post-void residual

Variable

Retained urine volume after voiding, assessed by bladder ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Bladder volume calculated from measured dimensions after the patient has voided; elevated values (commonly >100-150 mL, context-dependent) suggest incomplete emptying.

Differential
  • Bladder outlet obstruction (BPH, stricture)
  • Neurogenic bladder
  • Medication effect (anticholinergics)
Next Step

Correlate with urinary symptoms; urology referral if persistently elevated.

14. Bladder calculus, ultrasound

Variable

Stone within the bladder lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic, shadowing, mobile/dependent structure within the bladder that repositions with patient movement.

Differential
  • Calcified bladder tumor (fixed, wall-based, non-mobile)
Next Step

Urology referral, particularly to address underlying outlet obstruction.

Pancreas & Spleen

6 findings

1. Acute pancreatitis, ultrasound

Concerning

Pancreatic inflammation; ultrasound views are often limited by overlying bowel gas.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse pancreatic enlargement with decreased echogenicity from edema, and peripancreatic fluid when views allow adequate visualization.

Differential
  • Limited/nondiagnostic study due to bowel gas (common limitation)
  • Necrotizing pancreatitis (better assessed by CT)
Next Step

CT remains the primary modality for full pancreatitis assessment and complication detection when ultrasound views are limited.

2. Pancreatic pseudocyst, ultrasound

Variable

Encapsulated peripancreatic fluid collection, a late complication of pancreatitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic to complex fluid collection with a well-defined wall in the expected peripancreatic location.

Differential
  • Walled-off necrosis (more internal debris)
  • Cystic neoplasm
Next Step

Follow size on serial imaging; drainage if symptomatic or infected.

3. Splenomegaly

Variable

Enlarged spleen, a nonspecific finding with a broad differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Splenic length exceeding approximately 12-13 cm on longitudinal measurement.

Differential
  • Portal hypertension/cirrhosis
  • Hematologic malignancy
  • Infection
  • Congestive states
Next Step

Correlate with CBC and liver disease history; avoid contact sports if significantly enlarged.

4. Splenic laceration / free fluid (FAST)

Concerning

Traumatic splenic injury, assessed in the FAST examination via the perisplenic view.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular splenic contour or heterogeneous parenchyma with adjacent free fluid in the splenorenal recess; ultrasound is more sensitive for free fluid than for grading the laceration itself.

Differential
  • Splenic laceration/contusion
  • Isolated free fluid without visible organ injury
Next Step

CT for definitive injury grading; surgical consultation guided by hemodynamic status.

5. Splenic infarct, ultrasound

Variable

Area of ischemic splenic tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Peripheral, wedge-shaped hypoechoic area extending to the capsule, with absent flow on color Doppler within the affected region.

Differential
  • Atrial fibrillation-related embolism
  • Hypercoagulable states
  • Pancreatitis-related splenic vein thrombosis
Next Step

Correlate with cardiac rhythm and coagulation workup.

6. Splenic cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Fluid-filled splenic lesion, congenital or post-traumatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, well-circumscribed lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement, sometimes with peripheral calcification (more typical of post-traumatic pseudocysts).

Differential
  • Splenic abscess (if complex/irregular wall)
  • Lymphangioma
Next Step

No follow-up typically needed for a simple cyst.

Aorta & Vascular Doppler

6 findings

1. Abdominal aortic aneurysm, screening ultrasound

Concerning

Focal aortic dilation ≥3 cm, the primary indication for AAA screening ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter ≥3 cm measured outer-wall to outer-wall in transverse and longitudinal planes; assess for mural thrombus.

Differential
  • Ectasia (mild diffuse dilation <3 cm)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for size-based surveillance or repair per current guidelines.

2. Aortic mural thrombus

Variable

Thrombus lining the wall of an aneurysmal or atherosclerotic aorta.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic material lining the aortic wall within the lumen, most often in the setting of an aneurysm.

Differential
  • Mural thrombus within a AAA (common, usually not independently concerning)
  • Source of distal embolization (less common)
Next Step

Correlate with aneurysm surveillance; no independent action needed for typical mural thrombus.

3. Portal vein Doppler abnormality

Concerning

Abnormal direction or velocity of portal venous flow.

Imaging Characteristics

Reversed (hepatofugal) flow or reduced/absent flow velocity, both suggesting portal hypertension or thrombosis.

Differential
  • Portal hypertension (reversed flow)
  • Portal vein thrombosis (absent flow)
Next Step

Hepatology referral; correlate with cirrhosis workup.

4. IVC assessment (collapsibility/thrombus)

Variable

Evaluation of the inferior vena cava for volume status estimation or thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Collapsibility index used for volume status assessment; echogenic filling defect with absent flow indicates thrombus.

Differential
  • Volume status assessment (collapsibility)
  • IVC thrombosis (filling defect)
  • Extrinsic compression (mass effect)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical volume status; anticoagulation and further workup if thrombus identified.

5. Renal artery Doppler abnormality (resistive index)

Variable

Elevated or asymmetric renal resistive index, a marker of parenchymal disease or obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Elevated resistive index (generally >0.7-0.8) suggesting parenchymal disease, or a parvus-tardus waveform distal to a renal artery stenosis.

Differential
  • Renal artery stenosis (parvus-tardus pattern)
  • Acute obstruction (elevated RI)
  • Chronic medical renal disease (elevated RI)
Next Step

Correlate with renal function and blood pressure; nephrology/vascular referral.

6. Hepatic vein Doppler abnormality (Budd-Chiari)

Concerning

Abnormal hepatic venous outflow, suggesting Budd-Chiari syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent, reversed, or monophasic (loss of normal triphasic) flow in the hepatic veins on Doppler.

Differential
  • Hepatic vein thrombosis
  • Extrinsic compression of hepatic veins
Next Step

Hepatology/hematology referral for anticoagulation and hypercoagulable workup.

Appendix

5 findings

1. Acute appendicitis, graded compression ultrasound

Concerning

Appendiceal inflammation, evaluated with graded compression technique, especially in children and pregnant patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible, dilated appendix (>6-7 mm outer diameter) in a blind-ending tubular structure, often with increased periappendiceal echogenicity (fat stranding equivalent) and hyperemia on color Doppler.

Differential
  • Mesenteric adenitis
  • Epiploic appendagitis
  • Ovarian pathology (right-sided, in females)
Next Step

Surgical consultation; antibiotics-first vs. appendectomy per shared decision-making.

2. Appendicolith, ultrasound

Variable

Calcified fecalith within the appendiceal lumen.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing within the appendiceal lumen.

Differential
  • Incidental appendicolith without appendicitis
  • Marker of current/impending appendicitis
Next Step

Correlate with appendiceal diameter and surrounding inflammatory change.

3. Periappendiceal fluid / abscess

Concerning

Complication of appendicitis with a localized fluid or abscess collection.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex fluid collection adjacent to the appendix, which may not be separately visualized if perforated.

Differential
  • Perforated appendicitis with abscess
  • Perforated appendicitis with phlegmon
Next Step

IR drainage plus antibiotics with interval appendectomy, or emergent surgery depending on presentation.

4. Normal appendix, ultrasound

Benign

Compressible appendix within normal size limits, or nonvisualization.

Imaging Characteristics

Appendix, when identified, measures ≤6 mm and is fully compressible with graded pressure; nonvisualization is common and does not reliably exclude appendicitis.

Differential
  • True normal appendix
  • Appendicitis with a nonvisualized/atypically located appendix
Next Step

Correlate with clinical probability; consider CT or clinical observation if suspicion remains despite a nondiagnostic ultrasound.

5. Perforated appendicitis, ultrasound signs

Concerning

Appendiceal rupture, suggested by secondary ultrasound signs.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal, continuous echogenic submucosal layer, a discrete periappendiceal or pelvic fluid collection, and increased surrounding echogenic fat.

Differential
  • Uncomplicated appendicitis (intact wall layers)
Next Step

Surgical consultation; further imaging (CT) if the extent of perforation needs clarification.

Female Pelvis

18 findings

1. Simple ovarian cyst

Benign

Common physiologic or benign ovarian cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Round, anechoic, thin-walled lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement and no internal vascularity or septations.

Differential
  • Functional cyst (follicular, corpus luteum)
  • Benign cystadenoma
Next Step

Most resolve spontaneously; follow-up ultrasound for larger cysts (generally >5 cm) per size-based guidelines.

2. Hemorrhagic ovarian cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Cyst containing blood products, typically from ovulation or corpus luteum bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Internal lace-like reticular pattern or a retracting clot (avascular on color Doppler, distinguishing it from a solid mural nodule), with a normal-appearing cyst wall.

Differential
  • Endometrioma (persists on follow-up)
  • Hemorrhage into a neoplasm (rare)
Next Step

Follow-up ultrasound in 6-8 weeks to confirm resolution.

3. Complex/suspicious adnexal mass

Concerning

Adnexal lesion with features raising concern for malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid components, thick irregular septations, papillary projections with internal vascularity on color Doppler, or associated ascites.

Differential
  • Ovarian malignancy (primary or metastatic)
  • Endometrioma
  • Mature/immature teratoma
Next Step

Gynecologic oncology referral; CA-125 as part of the workup.

4. Ovarian torsion, ultrasound with Doppler

Concerning

Twisting of the ovary on its vascular pedicle, a surgical emergency for ovarian salvage.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, edematous ovary with peripherally displaced follicles; absent or markedly reduced venous and/or arterial flow on Doppler (though normal flow does not fully exclude intermittent torsion), and a 'whirlpool sign' of the twisted pedicle.

Differential
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
  • Hemorrhagic cyst
  • Ovarian mass without torsion
Next Step

Gynecologic surgical emergency regardless of Doppler flow if clinical suspicion is high.

5. Ectopic pregnancy

Concerning

Pregnancy implanted outside the endometrial cavity, most often in the fallopian tube.

Imaging Characteristics

Adnexal mass or 'ring' structure separate from the ovary, sometimes with a yolk sac/embryo, in a patient with a positive hCG and empty uterine cavity; free fluid raises concern for rupture.

Differential
  • Tubal ectopic (most common)
  • Corpus luteum cyst (can mimic, but ipsilateral to the ovary)
  • Interstitial/cornual ectopic (higher rupture risk)
Next Step

Gynecologic emergency if ruptured or unstable; serial hCG and gynecology referral for management.

6. Intrauterine pregnancy, viability assessment

Variable

Confirmed intrauterine gestation, assessed for viability by size and cardiac activity milestones.

Imaging Characteristics

Gestational sac, yolk sac, and embryo with cardiac activity assessed against expected size/gestational age milestones.

Differential
  • Viable IUP
  • Early pregnancy loss (no cardiac activity when expected by size)
  • Pregnancy of unknown location (if no sac visualized despite positive hCG)
Next Step

Correlate with hCG trend and gestational age; obstetric follow-up.

7. Uterine fibroid, ultrasound

Benign

Common benign smooth muscle tumor of the uterus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, hypoechoic (relative to myometrium) mass distorting the uterine contour, often with posterior acoustic shadowing from calcification.

Differential
  • Adenomyosis (less well-defined, diffuse)
  • Uterine sarcoma (rare)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; MRI for surgical planning if fibroid-directed therapy is considered.

8. Adenomyosis, ultrasound

Benign

Ectopic endometrial tissue within the myometrium.

Imaging Characteristics

Globular, asymmetric uterine enlargement with heterogeneous myometrial echotexture, myometrial cysts, and indistinct endometrial-myometrial border.

Differential
  • Uterine fibroid (well-circumscribed, unlike adenomyosis)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; gynecology referral for management options.

9. Endometrial thickness, postmenopausal bleeding

Variable

Endometrial stripe measurement, a key triage tool in postmenopausal bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Endometrial thickness measured on sagittal view; a thin endometrium (commonly ≤4 mm) has a high negative predictive value for endometrial cancer in postmenopausal bleeding.

Differential
  • Atrophic endometrium (thin, benign)
  • Endometrial hyperplasia or polyp (thickened)
  • Endometrial cancer (thickened, especially with irregular vascularity)
Next Step

Endometrial biopsy for thickened or irregular endometrium in the setting of postmenopausal bleeding.

10. Endometrial polyp, ultrasound

Benign

Focal benign endometrial overgrowth.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, echogenic endometrial lesion, often with a single feeding vessel visible on color Doppler; saline infusion sonohysterography improves detection.

Differential
  • Submucosal fibroid
  • Focal endometrial hyperplasia
Next Step

Gynecology referral for hysteroscopic resection if symptomatic.

11. Tubo-ovarian abscess

Concerning

Complication of pelvic inflammatory disease with a complex inflammatory adnexal mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, thick-walled, multiloculated adnexal collection, often bilateral, with internal debris and surrounding hyperemia.

Differential
  • Ovarian torsion
  • Ruptured ovarian cyst
  • Appendiceal abscess (if right-sided)
Next Step

IV antibiotics; IR or surgical drainage for large or non-responding abscesses.

12. Free fluid, cul-de-sac

Variable

Fluid in the pelvic cul-de-sac (pouch of Douglas), a common and often physiologic finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic (simple) or echogenic (complex/hemorrhagic) fluid in the posterior cul-de-sac.

Differential
  • Physiologic (mid-cycle)
  • Ruptured ovarian cyst
  • Ectopic pregnancy (correlate hCG)
  • Pelvic inflammatory process
Next Step

Correlate with hCG and clinical context; usually no isolated action needed for small physiologic amounts.

13. Mature cystic teratoma (dermoid), ultrasound

Benign

Common benign germ cell tumor containing multiple tissue types.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous mass with a hyperechoic component (often with posterior shadowing from fat/hair, the 'tip of the iceberg' sign) and sometimes a fat-fluid level.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic cyst
  • Other complex adnexal mass
Next Step

Gynecology referral for surgical removal given torsion risk.

14. Hydrosalpinx, ultrasound

Benign

Fluid-distended, obstructed fallopian tube.

Imaging Characteristics

Elongated, tubular, anechoic adnexal structure with incomplete septations from folded mucosal folds ('cogwheel sign' on cross-section), distinct from an ovarian cyst.

Differential
  • Ovarian cystadenoma (round rather than tubular)
  • Paratubal cyst
Next Step

Gynecology referral, particularly relevant to fertility evaluation.

15. Endometrioma, ultrasound

Variable

Ovarian cyst containing old blood from endometriosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, low-level ('ground-glass') internal echoes within a cyst, often with a thick wall; may be bilateral.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic cyst (resolves on follow-up, endometrioma persists)
Next Step

Gynecology referral for symptom management and fertility counseling if relevant.

16. Polycystic ovarian morphology, ultrasound

Benign

Imaging pattern associated with (but not diagnostic alone of) polycystic ovary syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged ovarian volume with numerous small (2-9 mm) peripheral follicles and increased stromal echogenicity.

Differential
  • Normal multifollicular ovaries
  • PCOS (requires clinical/biochemical correlation)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical and laboratory criteria; endocrinology/gynecology referral.

17. Nabothian cyst

Benign

Benign mucus-retention cyst of the cervix.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, anechoic, well-circumscribed cyst within the cervical stroma.

Differential
  • Incidental finding, no malignant potential
Next Step

No action needed.

18. Retained products of conception

Concerning

Retained placental or fetal tissue after delivery, miscarriage, or termination.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened, heterogeneous endometrial echo complex, often with increased vascularity on color Doppler ('molar-like' hypervascular pattern in some cases).

Differential
  • Normal postpartum/post-abortion endometrium (thinner, less vascular)
  • Retained products with associated hemorrhage/infection
Next Step

Gynecology referral; correlate with bleeding severity and infection signs, may require evacuation.

Male Pelvis / Scrotum

10 findings

1. Testicular torsion

Concerning

Twisting of the testicle on its spermatic cord, a time-critical surgical emergency for testicular salvage.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent or markedly reduced intratesticular flow on color Doppler compared to the contralateral side, often with an enlarged, heterogeneous testis; a 'whirlpool sign' of the twisted cord may be seen.

Differential
  • Epididymitis/orchitis (increased, not absent, flow)
  • Torsion-detorsion (may show hyperemia after spontaneous detorsion)
Next Step

Urologic surgical emergency; do not delay surgical consultation for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

2. Epididymitis

Variable

Inflammation of the epididymis, often infectious.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, hypoechoic (or heterogeneous) epididymis with markedly increased flow on color Doppler compared to the contralateral side.

Differential
  • Bacterial epididymitis (often sexually transmitted or from UTI-causing organisms)
  • Epididymo-orchitis (if testicular involvement present)
Next Step

Antibiotics targeted to likely organisms based on age/risk factors; urology follow-up if not improving.

3. Orchitis

Variable

Inflammation of the testicle, often extending from epididymitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, heterogeneous, hypoechoic testis with increased flow on color Doppler.

Differential
  • Epididymo-orchitis (most common)
  • Viral orchitis (e.g., mumps)
Next Step

Treat underlying cause; supportive care and urology follow-up.

4. Hydrocele

Benign

Fluid collection within the tunica vaginalis surrounding the testicle.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic fluid surrounding the testis within the scrotal sac, without internal septations or solid components in a simple hydrocele.

Differential
  • Simple hydrocele (idiopathic or reactive)
  • Complex hydrocele (septated, suggests infection/hemorrhage)
Next Step

No action needed for a simple, asymptomatic hydrocele; urology referral if large/symptomatic.

5. Varicocele

Variable

Dilated pampiniform venous plexus, most common on the left side.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple anechoic, tubular, serpiginous structures superior to and surrounding the testis, measuring >2-3 mm, with augmented flow on color Doppler during Valsalva.

Differential
  • Idiopathic varicocele (most common, left-sided)
  • Secondary varicocele (right-sided or acute-onset, raises concern for retroperitoneal mass causing venous compression)
Next Step

Urology referral if associated with infertility or pain; right-sided or acute varicocele warrants evaluation for a retroperitoneal cause.

6. Testicular mass, ultrasound

Concerning

Solid or complex intratesticular lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, often hypoechoic, vascularized intratesticular lesion distinct from normal parenchyma.

Differential
  • Germ cell tumor (most common in young men)
  • Lymphoma/leukemia infiltration (bilateral, older patients)
  • Benign lesion (rare, e.g., epidermoid cyst)
Next Step

Urology referral; tumor markers (AFP, beta-hCG, LDH) and likely orchiectomy for a solid intratesticular mass.

7. Hematocele

Variable

Blood collection within the tunica vaginalis, usually from trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex fluid collection around the testis with internal septations/debris, distinguishing it from a simple hydrocele.

Differential
  • Traumatic hematocele
  • Post-surgical/post-procedural hematocele
Next Step

Correlate with trauma history; assess testicular integrity for associated rupture.

8. Fournier gangrene

Concerning

Necrotizing fasciitis of the perineal/scrotal soft tissue, a surgical emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Subcutaneous gas (echogenic foci with 'dirty' posterior shadowing/reverberation artifact) within the scrotal wall soft tissue, with wall thickening.

Differential
  • Simple scrotal cellulitis (no gas)
  • Fournier gangrene (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics; do not delay for further imaging if clinically suspected.

9. Torsion of the appendix testis

Benign

Torsion of a small vestigial appendage on the testis, a common mimic of testicular torsion in prepubertal boys.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, hyperechoic nodule (the twisted appendage) near the upper pole of the testis, often with a reactive hydrocele and normal testicular flow ('blue dot sign' may be seen clinically).

Differential
  • Testicular torsion (key differential, distinguished by preserved testicular flow)
  • Epididymitis
Next Step

Conservative management (analgesia); self-limited, resolving over 1-2 weeks.

10. Spermatocele

Benign

Benign cystic dilation of the epididymal duct containing sperm.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or low-level echo cyst located in the head of the epididymis, superior/posterior to the testis.

Differential
  • Epididymal cyst (similar appearance, no sperm content distinguishable by imaging)
Next Step

No action needed unless symptomatic.

Concerning

Anechoic (or hypoechoic if hemorrhagic) stripe of fluid between the liver and right kidney.

Free fluid in the left upper quadrant view of FAST, around the spleen and left kidney.

Anechoic or hypoechoic fluid in the splenorenal recess or around the spleen tip, a common site for early accumulation with splenic injury.

  • Hemoperitoneum from splenic injury
  • Preexisting ascites

Correlate with hemodynamic status; CT for definitive injury grading if stable.

3. Pelvic free fluid (FAST)

Concerning

Free fluid in the pelvis, the most dependent recess in a supine patient and often the first place fluid accumulates.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or hypoechoic fluid in the retrovesical (male) or rectouterine/pouch of Douglas (female) space.

Differential
  • Hemoperitoneum (trauma)
  • Physiologic fluid (in females, correlate with cycle)
Next Step

Correlate with hemodynamic status and other FAST views; CT if stable for further characterization.

4. Pericardial effusion (subxiphoid FAST view)

Concerning

Fluid within the pericardial sac, assessed via the subxiphoid cardiac view in eFAST.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic (or complex if hemorrhagic) stripe of fluid surrounding the heart within the pericardial sac; assess for signs of tamponade (right ventricular diastolic collapse).

Differential
  • Traumatic hemopericardium
  • Preexisting pericardial effusion
Next Step

Emergent cardiothoracic surgery/cardiology consultation, especially with tamponade physiology.

5. Absent lung sliding (pneumothorax, eFAST)

Concerning

Loss of the normal pleural sliding motion, suggesting pneumothorax.

Imaging Characteristics

Absence of the normal to-and-fro sliding of the visceral against parietal pleura on M-mode ('barcode' or 'stratosphere' sign instead of the normal 'seashore' sign); a 'lung point' confirms pneumothorax if identified.

Differential
  • Pneumothorax
  • Prior pleurodesis/adhesions (can also cause absent sliding without pneumothorax)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical status; chest decompression if tension physiology present.

6. Hemothorax (thoracic FAST view)

Concerning

Blood within the pleural space, detected via the thoracic views in eFAST.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic or complex fluid collection above the diaphragm within the pleural space, often best seen just cephalad to the diaphragm in the same view used to assess for subdiaphragmatic free fluid.

Differential
  • Traumatic hemothorax
  • Preexisting pleural effusion
Next Step

Chest tube placement per trauma protocol if significant volume or hemodynamically relevant.

Pediatric-Specific

5 findings

1. Pyloric stenosis

Concerning

Hypertrophy of the pyloric muscle causing gastric outlet obstruction in infants, classically presenting around 3-6 weeks of age.

Imaging Characteristics

Elongated pyloric channel (length >14-17 mm) with thickened muscle wall (>3-4 mm), often described as the 'cervix sign' or 'donut sign' on cross-section, with observed failure of gastric contents to pass.

Differential
  • Pylorospasm (transient, normal muscle thickness)
  • Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis
Next Step

Pediatric surgery referral for pyloromyotomy; correct electrolyte abnormalities (hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis) before surgery.

2. Intussusception, pediatric

Concerning

Telescoping of one bowel segment into another, most common in infants/toddlers, classically ileocolic.

Imaging Characteristics

'Target' or 'donut sign' on transverse view with concentric alternating echogenic/hypoechoic rings, and a 'pseudokidney sign' on longitudinal view.

Differential
  • Transient small-bowel intussusception (often incidental, short-segment, no lead point)
  • Ileocolic intussusception (requires reduction)
Next Step

Air or contrast enema reduction (also therapeutic); surgical consultation if reduction fails or signs of perforation/necrosis are present.

3. Meckel diverticulum

Variable

Congenital outpouching of the distal ileum, the most common congenital GI anomaly, classically causing painless rectal bleeding in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Blind-ending, tubular structure in the right lower quadrant, best identified when complicated by inflammation (diverticulitis) or as a lead point for intussusception; often difficult to identify when uncomplicated.

Differential
  • Normal bowel loop (uncomplicated Meckel is often not visualized)
  • Meckel diverticulitis (mimics appendicitis)
  • Lead point for intussusception
Next Step

Correlate with clinical presentation (painless GI bleeding classic); technetium-99m pertechnetate scan ('Meckel scan') for definitive diagnosis if suspected.

4. Mesenteric adenitis

Benign

Benign, self-limited enlargement of mesenteric lymph nodes, often following a viral illness, a common mimic of appendicitis in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Cluster of enlarged (>5-8 mm short axis) but normally shaped, oval mesenteric lymph nodes, typically in the right lower quadrant, with a normal appendix.

Differential
  • Appendicitis (key differential, requires visualizing a normal, compressible appendix to favor adenitis)
  • Crohn disease
Next Step

Supportive care; self-limited, resolving over 1-2 weeks.

5. Ovarian torsion, pediatric

Concerning

Twisting of the ovary in a child/adolescent, often associated with an underlying ovarian mass or cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, edematous ovary with peripherally displaced follicles and absent or reduced flow on Doppler; underlying cyst/mass is a common predisposing factor.

Differential
  • Ovarian cyst without torsion
  • Tubo-ovarian abscess
Next Step

Gynecologic/pediatric surgical emergency for ovarian salvage.

Soft Tissue & Miscellaneous

4 findings

1. Abdominal wall hernia, dynamic ultrasound

Variable

Protrusion of abdominal contents through a fascial defect, assessed dynamically with Valsalva.

Imaging Characteristics

Fat or bowel protruding through a fascial defect, which enlarges or becomes more apparent with increased intra-abdominal pressure (Valsalva/standing).

Differential
  • Reducible hernia (contents return with relaxation/reclining)
  • Incarcerated hernia (fixed, non-reducible, higher concern)
Next Step

Surgical referral; more urgent if non-reducible or associated with pain/obstructive symptoms.

2. Rectus sheath hematoma, ultrasound

Variable

Bleeding within the rectus abdominis muscle sheath.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform, hypoechoic to complex collection within the rectus muscle, often unilateral, that does not cross the midline (limited by the linea alba).

Differential
  • Anticoagulation-related (most common)
  • Post-traumatic/post-procedural
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; correlate with anticoagulation status.

3. Subcutaneous abscess / cellulitis

Concerning

Soft-tissue infection, with ultrasound distinguishing simple cellulitis from a drainable abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Cellulitis: thickened, hyperechoic subcutaneous tissue with interspersed fluid tracking between fat lobules ('cobblestoning'), without a discrete collection. Abscess: discrete, complex, hypoechoic-to-anechoic fluid collection, often with internal debris and increased through-transmission.

Differential
  • Simple cellulitis (no drainable collection)
  • Abscess (drainable)
Next Step

Incision and drainage for a discrete abscess; antibiotics alone for cellulitis without a drainable collection.

4. Lymphadenopathy, ultrasound features

Variable

Enlarged lymph nodes assessed for benign versus concerning features.

Imaging Characteristics

Benign: oval shape, preserved echogenic fatty hilum, symmetric cortical thickness. Concerning: rounded shape, loss of fatty hilum, asymmetric or markedly thickened cortex, and increased peripheral (rather than hilar) vascularity.

Differential
  • Reactive lymphadenopathy (benign features)
  • Lymphoma/metastatic lymphadenopathy (concerning features)
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; biopsy for nodes with concerning features or in the setting of known/suspected malignancy.

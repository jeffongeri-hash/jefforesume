Foundations · Enteric Nervous System

The Gut's Own Nervous System

Before the region-by-region tour: the architecture that makes the GI tract capable of running its own show, with the brain more as a supervisor than a puppeteer.

01The "Second Brain"

The enteric nervous system (ENS) contains roughly 400–600 million neurons — comparable to the spinal cord — organized into two interconnected plexuses embedded in the gut wall itself. Critically, the ENS can generate coordinated motility and secretory reflexes entirely independent of CNS input — a denervated segment of bowel (as in a transplant) still contracts and coordinates peristalsis on its own. The CNS (via vagal and sympathetic input) modulates and fine-tunes this activity rather than originating it — most gut reflexes are locally generated and locally executed.

Myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus

Between the longitudinal and circular muscle layers. Primarily controls motility — the layer whose neurotransmitters (covered region-by-region in this atlas) directly drive smooth muscle contraction and relaxation.

Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus

Within the submucosa. Primarily controls secretion and local blood flow — senses luminal contents and adjusts epithelial secretory activity accordingly.

02Three Modes of GI Signaling

Every molecule in this atlas fits one of three signaling categories — the same endocrine/paracrine/autocrine framework from general physiology, but with a GI-specific twist: many "hormones" covered here also moonlight as neurotransmitters released directly from enteric nerve terminals, a dual identity that's uncommon outside the gut.

ModeDefinitionGI example
EndocrineReleased from specialized mucosal cells into blood, acts on distant targetsSecretin (S cells → pancreas)
ParacrineReleased locally, diffuses to adjacent cells without entering systemic circulationSomatostatin (D cells → adjacent parietal/G cells)
NeurocrineReleased from enteric nerve terminals, acts as a classic synaptic neurotransmitterVIP, nitric oxide, substance P, acetylcholine
The dual-identity molecules
Several molecules in this atlas are released both ways depending on the cell of origin — somatostatin acts paracrine from mucosal D cells but is also a neurocrine transmitter from enteric neurons; serotonin is overwhelmingly paracrine/endocrine from enterochromaffin cells (Section 4) but a small enteric-neuronal pool exists too. This dual identity is a recurring theme, not an exception.

03The Migrating Motor Complex — Baseline Housekeeping

Between meals, the GI tract runs a recurring ~90–120 minute cycle called the migrating motor complex (MMC), driven primarily by motilin (covered in Section 4) — a "housekeeping" wave that sweeps undigested debris and bacteria distally, preventing small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. This baseline pattern is completely interrupted by feeding (which switches the gut into a fed, motilin-suppressed state) — the fasting/fed state switch is one of the cleanest examples in this atlas of one signal wholly reorganizing GI behavior.

04How to Use This Atlas

Organized by anatomic region — esophagus, stomach, small intestine (split across two sections given the density of signaling molecules), and colon — with each region's hormones and neurotransmitters covered together as they arise, including full disease correlations, genetic syndromes, and drug targets. Cross-references to earlier sections use plain section names since this file is self-contained.

Region 1 · Esophagus

Esophagus — Neural Control & LES Regulation

The one region in this atlas with essentially no endocrine hormone story — motility here is almost entirely a neurocrine affair, which makes it the cleanest place to introduce the excitatory/inhibitory neurotransmitter pairing that recurs throughout the rest of the tract.

PeristalsisTwo Kinds, One Neural Program

Primary peristalsis

Triggered by swallowing, centrally programmed via the swallowing center (medulla) and carried out through vagal efferents to the myenteric plexus — a coordinated wave that requires CNS initiation, unlike most of the reflexes elsewhere in this atlas.

Secondary peristalsis

Triggered locally by esophageal distension (a food bolus that didn't clear, refluxed material) — entirely an intrinsic myenteric plexus reflex, no swallow or CNS initiation required. This is the "second brain" in action exactly as described in the Foundational Principles section.

The LESAn Excitatory/Inhibitory Push-Pull

The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) is tonically contracted at rest (maintaining the barrier against reflux) and must relax on cue with each swallow — a job requiring two opposing neurotransmitter systems acting on the same smooth muscle.

NeurotransmitterEffect on LESSource
Acetylcholine (ACh)Contraction (maintains resting tone)Excitatory myenteric motor neurons, vagal cholinergic input
Nitric oxide (NO)Relaxation — the dominant inhibitory signalInhibitory myenteric neurons ("NANC" — non-adrenergic, non-cholinergic transmission)
VIPRelaxation, co-released alongside NO from the same inhibitory neuronsSame inhibitory myenteric neuron population

Swallowing triggers a precisely timed receptive relaxation — inhibitory (NO/VIP) neurons fire just ahead of the peristaltic wave's arrival, dropping LES tone so the bolus can pass, followed by a rebound contraction that restores the reflux barrier.

Disease Correlations

AchalasiaLoss of inhibitory (NO/VIP) myenteric neurons
Acquired, likely autoimmune-mediated (idiopathic) or Chagas disease (secondary)

Selective degeneration of the inhibitory neuron population — the excitatory (ACh) side is relatively preserved. Net result: the LES cannot relax on swallowing (unopposed cholinergic tone), and peristalsis in the esophageal body is lost or becomes uncoordinated/spastic. This is a clean, direct demonstration of what happens when only one side of the push-pull pair fails — not a global neuropathy, but a selective one. Presents with dysphagia to both solids and liquids, regurgitation, and a characteristic "bird's beak" tapering of the distal esophagus on barium swallow.

Chagas Disease (Esophageal Involvement)Trypanosoma cruzi infection
Acquired, endemic to Latin America

Direct parasitic destruction of myenteric plexus neurons (both inhibitory and excitatory, more diffusely than idiopathic achalasia) produces a secondary achalasia-like picture — same functional endpoint (failed LES relaxation, dysmotility) via a distinct, infectious mechanism rather than the idiopathic/autoimmune process.

Diffuse Esophageal SpasmDysregulated (not absent) inhibitory neurotransmission
Idiopathic

Uncoordinated, simultaneous (non-peristaltic) contractions — thought to reflect a partial or patchy defect in inhibitory neurotransmission timing rather than achalasia's more complete loss, producing chest pain and dysphagia with a characteristic "corkscrew esophagus" appearance on barium swallow.

Region 2 · Stomach

Stomach — Gastrin, Histamine, Somatostatin, ACh & Ghrelin

Four signals converge on one cell — the parietal cell — to drive acid secretion, and nearly every acid-suppressing drug class works by interrupting one specific point in that convergence.

All three receptors converge on activating the parietal cell's apical H⁺/K⁺-ATPase, the final common pathway for acid secretion — but they're not simply additive. Histamine potentiates the effect of both gastrin and ACh (a permissive, multiplicative relationship rather than three independent inputs), which is exactly why H2 blockers are disproportionately effective at reducing total acid output despite blocking only one of the three receptors.

Gastrin's most important action is actually indirect — it primarily stimulates enterochromaffin-like (ECL) cells to release histamine, which then drives the bulk of acid secretion; gastrin's direct CCK2-receptor effect on the parietal cell itself is comparatively minor. Gastrin is negative-feedback inhibited by luminal acid itself (antral pH <3 suppresses further G-cell release) and by somatostatin (below) — a dual brake system.

SomatostatinThe Universal Brake

D cells (scattered throughout the gastric mucosa) release somatostatin in response to luminal acid — it paracrine-inhibits G cells (↓ gastrin), ECL cells (↓ histamine), and has some direct inhibitory effect on the parietal cell itself. This is the same broad inhibitory signaling molecule introduced in the pancreatic islet context elsewhere in physiology — here redeployed as the stomach's primary self-limiting acid brake, engaged precisely when acid has already done its job.

GhrelinThe Hunger Signal, Gastric-Sourced

Secreted by X/A-like (P/D1) cells in the gastric fundus, rising before meals and falling sharply after eating. Acts on the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (activating NPY/AgRP neurons) as the body's principal orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) signal, and directly on the pituitary's growth hormone secretagogue receptor to stimulate GH release. Sleeve gastrectomy removes the ghrelin-producing fundus directly — circulating ghrelin drops substantially and durably post-op, now understood as a major, independent contributor to the procedure's appetite-suppressing effect, distinct from and additive to simple mechanical volume restriction.

Disease Correlations

Zollinger-Ellison SyndromeGastrin-secreting tumor (gastrinoma) — often duodenal/pancreatic
Sporadic or MEN1-associated (~25%)

Autonomous gastrin secretion, unresponsive to the normal acid-feedback brake — massively elevated acid output causes severe, often multiple or refractory peptic ulcers, frequently in atypical locations (distal duodenum, jejunum). Secretory diarrhea is common (acid inactivates pancreatic lipase and directly injures small bowel mucosa). Diagnosed by fasting serum gastrin (markedly elevated) with a secretin stimulation test — paradoxically, gastrin rises further after secretin in ZES (secretin normally suppresses gastrin — see the Small Intestine Hormones section — but gastrinoma cells express a secretin receptor that stimulates rather than inhibits gastrin release, an aberrant response that's diagnostic).

Atrophic Gastritis / Pernicious AnemiaAutoimmune destruction of parietal cells (anti-parietal cell, anti-intrinsic factor antibodies)
Autoimmune, often clusters with other autoimmune endocrinopathies

Parietal cell loss removes both acid secretion (hypochlorhydria/achlorhydria) and intrinsic factor production (→ B12 malabsorption, megaloblastic anemia). Loss of the acid-mediated negative feedback on G cells causes secondary hypergastrinemia — sometimes striking — and can drive G-cell hyperplasia or, rarely, gastric neuroendocrine (carcinoid-type) tumors from chronic trophic gastrin stimulation of ECL cells. Worth distinguishing from ZES: both cause hypergastrinemia, but atrophic gastritis has high gastric pH (hypochlorhydria) while ZES has pathologically low pH (acid hypersecretion) — checking gastric pH alongside gastrin resolves the ambiguity immediately.

H. pylori InfectionBacterial colonization of gastric mucosa
Acquired, infectious

Antral-predominant infection reduces local D-cell somatostatin (removing the gastrin brake) → hypergastrinemia → increased acid output → duodenal ulcer risk. Corpus-predominant infection instead damages parietal cells directly, tending toward hypochlorhydria and gastric (rather than duodenal) ulcer/cancer risk — the same organism producing near-opposite acid phenotypes depending on which mucosal region is predominantly colonized.

Region 3 · Small Intestine — Hormones

CCK, Secretin, GIP, GLP-1, GLP-2 & Motilin

The classical endocrine cells of the small bowel coordinate pancreaticobiliary output with the meal actually arriving, run the incretin effect, and — through GLP-2 — even rebuild the gut itself.

A genuinely elegant coordination problem solved by one hormone: fat/protein arriving in the duodenum needs bile (for fat emulsification) and pancreatic enzymes (for digestion) delivered simultaneously, and needs the sphincter guarding their shared exit open at the same moment — CCK does all three at once. Also slows gastric emptying (giving pancreatic/biliary secretions time to work) and contributes to meal-related satiety via vagal afferent signaling to the brainstem.

Triggered specifically by acid, not by nutrients — a direct sensor for the exact problem it solves: gastric acid entering the duodenum needs neutralizing before pancreatic enzymes (which require a near-neutral pH to function) can work. Secretin also directly inhibits gastrin release from the stomach — a direct negative-feedback partnership with the Stomach section's acid-secretion story, and the basis of the paradoxical gastrin rise after secretin stimulation testing in Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (Stomach section) — a genuinely useful diagnostic inversion precisely because it's the opposite of the normal physiologic response.

GIPGlucose-Dependent Insulinotropic Polypeptide

Released from duodenal/jejunal K cells in response to nutrient arrival, especially fat and glucose. Along with GLP-1 below, GIP mediates the incretin effect — oral glucose provoking substantially more insulin secretion than IV glucose at matched blood levels — by stimulating glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells (the glucose-dependence is the key safety feature distinguishing incretin-based therapy from agents like sulfonylureas). GIP has a comparatively larger role in fat handling than GLP-1, and the incretin effect is substantially blunted in type 2 diabetes — the physiologic rationale behind GIP/GLP-1 dual-agonist therapy.

GLP-1Satiety & Glucose-Dependent Insulin Release

Released from distal ileum/colon L cells — cleaved from proglucagon by PC1/3 (the same precursor gene expressed differently by tissue: pancreatic alpha cells use PC2 to make glucagon instead — a clean example of one gene, two entirely different hormones, depending purely on which prohormone convertase the tissue expresses). Glucose-dependent insulin secretion, glucagon suppression, delayed gastric emptying, and central satiety signaling. Extremely short native half-life (~2 minutes, rapidly degraded by DPP-4) — the entire pharmaceutical GLP-1 receptor agonist class (semaglutide, liraglutide, and others) is built around engineering resistance to this exact degradation pathway for extended dosing intervals.

GLP-2The Intestine's Own Growth Factor

Co-secreted with GLP-1 from the same distal ileal/colonic L cells (same proglucagon precursor, same PC1/3 processing), but with an entirely different job: GLP-2 is intestinotrophic — it stimulates crypt cell proliferation, inhibits enterocyte apoptosis, increases villus height, enhances intestinal barrier function, and increases intestinal blood flow. Acts through a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed on subepithelial myofibroblasts and enteric neurons/enteroendocrine cells (not directly on enterocytes) — an indirect, paracrine-relayed growth signal. Native half-life is only ~7 minutes (DPP-4 degraded, same enzyme as GLP-1).

Teduglutide — a hormone turned directly into a drug
Teduglutide, a DPP-4-resistant GLP-2 analog, is FDA-approved for short bowel syndrome with intestinal failure — it promotes mucosal growth and absorptive capacity in the remaining bowel after major resection, meaningfully reducing parenteral nutrition dependence in clinical trials. It's one of the clearest examples in GI physiology of a naturally occurring intestinotrophic signal being converted directly into a disease-modifying (not just symptom-modifying) therapy — newer longer-acting analogs (apraglutide, weekly dosing) are following the same GLP-2-mimicry strategy.

MotilinThe Interdigestive Housekeeper

Released from duodenal M cells specifically during the fasting state — motilin drives phase III of the migrating motor complex (Foundational Principles section), the strong, sweeping contractions that clear residual debris and bacteria between meals. Feeding suppresses motilin release, switching the gut out of housekeeping mode.

Erythromycin's off-label prokinetic use
Erythromycin is a direct motilin receptor agonist — an antibiotic side effect turned therapeutic tool, used off-label at sub-antimicrobial doses to promote gastric emptying in gastroparesis and to clear the stomach before urgent endoscopy in upper GI bleeding. Tachyphylaxis (receptor downregulation with continued use) limits its long-term utility as a prokinetic.

Genetic/Acquired Correlations

VIPomaSee Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section for the full mechanism

Cross-referenced here because it's frequently confused with a classical hormone-secreting tumor — VIP is neurocrine, not endocrine, in its normal physiologic role, covered in depth in the next section.

Short Bowel SyndromeAcquired — extensive surgical resection

Loss of the distal ileum specifically impairs endogenous GLP-1, GLP-2, and PYY production (all predominantly distal L-cell products — see the Colon section for PYY) — part of why the location of resection, not just the length of remaining bowel, determines functional severity. Loss of terminal ileum also independently causes B12 and bile acid malabsorption, unrelated to the hormonal deficit but compounding the same segment's importance.

Region 3 (cont.) · Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters

Serotonin, VIP, Substance P, Enkephalins & Nitric Oxide

This is where the "second brain" from the Foundational Principles section actually does its signaling — and where 90% of the body's serotonin quietly lives, doing a job that has nothing to do with mood.

Serotonin (5-HT)The GI Tract's Main Signaling Molecule

~90% of the body's total serotonin is synthesized and stored in the gut — overwhelmingly by enterochromaffin cells scattered throughout the mucosa (a paracrine/endocrine source, not enteric neurons), synthesized from dietary tryptophan via tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH1, the gut-specific isoform — distinct from the CNS isoform TPH2, which is why gut and brain serotonin pools are largely independent).

Excess serotonin is cleared by the serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT) on enterocytes — the same transporter targeted by SSRIs systemically, which is part of the mechanistic explanation for SSRIs' common GI side effects (nausea, altered bowel habits) independent of any CNS action.

VIPVasoactive Intestinal Peptide

Released from secretomotor enteric neurons (the same inhibitory neuron population introduced at the LES in the Esophagus section — VIP's job is consistent throughout the tract: relaxation and secretion). In the small intestine specifically: stimulates intestinal and pancreatic fluid/electrolyte secretion, relaxes intestinal and vascular smooth muscle, and inhibits gastric acid secretion.

VIPoma (Verner-Morrison Syndrome / WDHA Syndrome)VIP-secreting pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor
Sporadic, occasionally MEN1-associated

Autonomous VIP secretion at pathologic levels drives massive, secretory (persists with fasting, unlike most osmotic diarrheas) watery diarrhea — the WDHA acronym: Watery Diarrhea, Hypokalemia (from massive stool potassium loss), Achlorhydria (VIP's inhibitory effect on gastric acid secretion, at pathologic levels, essentially shuts acid production down). Fluid losses can be severe enough to cause life-threatening dehydration and metabolic acidosis. Diagnosed by markedly elevated serum VIP; treated with octreotide (somatostatin analog — exploiting the universal inhibitory brake introduced in the Stomach section) as a bridge to surgical resection.

Substance PThe Excitatory Counterpart

A tachykinin released from excitatory enteric motor neurons — drives smooth muscle contraction (the excitatory partner to VIP/NO's inhibitory relaxation signal) and is a key peripheral and central mediator of visceral pain/nociception. Also contributes to the neurogenic inflammation component of some GI conditions and to carcinoid syndrome's flushing (co-released alongside serotonin from midgut neuroendocrine tumors — see the Colon section for the full carcinoid picture).

EnkephalinsThe Brake on Motility

Endogenous opioid peptides released from enteric neurons, acting on μ-opioid receptors expressed throughout the enteric nervous system — net effect is inhibition of both motility (reduced peristalsis, increased sphincter tone) and secretion. This endogenous system is exactly what exogenous opioids hijack:

Opioid-induced constipation, mechanistically
Systemic opioids (for pain) and the endogenous enkephalin system both converge on the same enteric μ-opioid receptors — reduced motility, increased fluid absorption time, and increased sphincter tone together produce opioid-induced constipation, a direct, predictable, dose-dependent peripheral GI effect rather than an idiosyncratic side effect. Loperamide exploits this same receptor deliberately as an antidiarrheal — a peripherally restricted opioid agonist (doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier at normal doses) that slows transit without central opioid effects. Peripherally-acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (methylnaltrexone, naloxegol) reverse opioid-induced constipation specifically by blocking these same peripheral gut receptors while sparing central analgesia — a clean, receptor-level solution to a receptor-level problem.

Nitric OxideThe Universal Inhibitory Signal, Revisited

As introduced at the LES (Esophagus section), NO is released from inhibitory ("NANC") enteric motor neurons throughout the gut and mediates smooth muscle relaxation — in the small intestine, this includes the receptive relaxation that allows a bolus to move forward without triggering simultaneous contraction ahead of and behind it (the coordinated relaxation-then-contraction sequence that makes peristalsis directional rather than just diffuse squeezing).

Disease CorrelationIrritable Bowel Syndrome

Serotonin signaling dysregulation is one of several implicated mechanisms in IBS — altered enterochromaffin cell density/serotonin availability, tryptophan metabolism shifts (with emerging research interest in the gut microbiome's role in modulating local serotonergic signaling), and genome-wide association data implicating serotonergic pathway genes have all been described. This remains an active, evolving research area rather than a single settled mechanism — current evidence supports serotonin signaling as a meaningful contributor to IBS's motility and visceral hypersensitivity features (part of why 5-HT3 antagonists and 5-HT4 agonists have genuine, if partial, efficacy in IBS-D and IBS-C respectively), without serotonin dysregulation fully explaining the syndrome on its own.

Region 4 · Colon

Colon — PYY, the Ileal Brake & the Congenital Failure of Inhibitory Signaling

The closing section brings back nearly every signal introduced earlier in this atlas — and ends with the single clearest congenital demonstration of what happens when inhibitory enteric neurons never develop at all.

PYY release rises in proportion to the caloric/fat load reaching the distal gut — a direct physiologic signal that "enough has been eaten to satisfy what's coming," which is why it's called the ileal brake: unabsorbed fat reaching the distal ileum triggers PYY (and GLP-1) release that slows gastric emptying and upper-gut transit, giving the distal gut more time to catch up. Like ghrelin's fundus removal in sleeve gastrectomy (Stomach section), PYY levels rise substantially after bariatric bypass surgery — nutrients reaching the distal gut faster than normal — contributing independently to that procedure's appetite-suppressing effect.

Serotonin & Colonic Motility

The same enterochromaffin-cell serotonin system introduced in the Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section drives colonic peristaltic and secretory reflexes via the identical 5-HT3/5-HT4 mechanism — mechanical stimulation of the colonic mucosa (stool bolus) triggers 5-HT release, activating the peristaltic reflex forward. This is the same system 5-HT4 agonist prokinetics (prucalopride) and 5-HT3 antagonists act on, now specifically relevant to colonic transit time and chronic constipation/diarrhea management.

Disease Correlations

Hirschsprung DiseaseRET proto-oncogene (most common), also EDNRB and others
Variable — often sporadic, familial clustering with RET mutations; associated with Down syndrome

Failed neural crest cell migration during development leaves a distal colonic segment (rectosigmoid most commonly, variable length) with no enteric neurons at all — not a selective loss of inhibitory neurons as in achalasia (Esophagus section), but complete aganglionosis. Without any neurons — inhibitory or excitatory — the aganglionic segment cannot relax and remains in a state of tonic, uncoordinated contraction, functioning as a mechanical obstruction. Proximal bowel dilates against this fixed downstream obstruction (the mechanism behind the "transition zone" seen radiographically). Presents classically as failure to pass meconium within 48 hours of birth; diagnosed by rectal suction biopsy showing absent ganglion cells, treated surgically by resecting the aganglionic segment.

Why this is the cleanest teaching case in the whole atlas
Achalasia (Esophagus section) shows what happens when only the inhibitory neurons are selectively lost — unopposed excitatory tone, failure to relax. Hirschsprung's shows what happens when no neurons exist at all — the muscle defaults to tonic contraction with nothing modulating it in either direction. Comparing these two directly is the single best way to internalize why the excitatory/inhibitory neurotransmitter balance introduced at the very start of this atlas (Esophagus section) is a genuine push-pull system, not just a convenient teaching framework.
Carcinoid SyndromeSerotonin-secreting neuroendocrine tumor, classically midgut-derived
Sporadic, occasionally MEN1-associated

Autonomous enterochromaffin-cell-derived tumor secretes serotonin (plus tachykinins including substance P, and sometimes histamine/prostaglandins) directly into circulation — classically only causing systemic symptoms once hepatic metastases develop, since the liver normally clears portal-vein serotonin from a primary gut tumor before it reaches systemic circulation (a primary tumor draining directly into systemic circulation, e.g., a bronchial carcinoid, can cause the syndrome without liver metastases, bypassing this first-pass clearance entirely). Presents with flushing, secretory diarrhea (persists with fasting, distinguishing it from typical IBS diarrhea, which occurs postprandially and not during sleep), bronchospasm, and — with long-standing right-heart serotonin exposure — carcinoid heart disease (tricuspid/pulmonic valve fibrosis).

Pellagra — the tryptophan-diversion complication
Because tryptophan is the shared precursor for both serotonin and niacin (vitamin B3) synthesis, a tumor diverting large amounts of tryptophan toward serotonin production can produce a secondary niacin deficiency — clinically manifesting as pellagra (the "3 D's": dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia) in a meaningful minority of carcinoid syndrome patients. This is a genuinely useful, easily overlooked diagnosis-within-a-diagnosis: a carcinoid patient with new confusion or a photosensitive dermatitis warrants niacin repletion consideration, not just escalation of carcinoid-directed therapy.

Diagnosed via urinary 5-HIAA (the serotonin breakdown metabolite); managed with somatostatin analogs (octreotide/lanreotide — exploiting the same universal inhibitory brake as VIPoma treatment above) to control both hormonal symptoms and, in some cases, tumor growth.

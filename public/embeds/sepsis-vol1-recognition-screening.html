Sepsis Recognition & Screening

SIRS, qSOFA, and SOFA — what each tool actually catches, what it misses, and why a single vital sign should never carry the diagnosis. Anchored to the Surviving Sepsis Campaign International Guidelines, with every point-of-care sensitivity/specificity figure sourced to its original cohort.

01 Guideline Currency & Scope

⚠ Read before relying on this volume

The Surviving Sepsis Campaign published an entirely new international guideline in 2026 — Prescott HC, Antonelli M, Alhazzani W, et al. Surviving Sepsis Campaign: International Guidelines for Management of Sepsis and Septic Shock 2026. Crit Care Med 2026;54:725–812 (epub Mar 23, 2026) — superseding the 2021 version that most current pocket references and app-based calculators are still built on. This document contains 129 statements; of the 39 statements revisited from 2021, 7 are unchanged and 29 keep the 2021 recommendation but with revised wording or a new clarifying remark. The remainder are genuinely new.

This volume was built with real-time verification against the 2026 executive summary and primary literature — not from training memory, which predates this release. Where I am citing the 2026 document directly, it's flagged SSC 2026. Where content is unchanged, longstanding physiology or Sepsis-3 definitional framework, it's flagged STABLE. Full-text access to all 129 statements was not verified line-by-line — for any specific numbered recommendation you plan to defend in a chart or M&M, pull the primary source (Crit Care Med 2026;54:725–812) directly.

On "IDSA guidelines" for sepsis: IDSA does not publish an independent sepsis management guideline. The master document is the Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SCCM/ESICM-led, multi-society consensus). IDSA endorsed the 2021 SSC guidelines — notably, after declining to endorse the 2016 revision over concerns about antibiotic stewardship and the balance between speed-of-antibiotics and antimicrobial overuse. That tension — IDSA's stewardship lens versus SSC's time-to-antibiotic mandates — is real and gets its own callout in Volume 3 (Empiric Antibiotics). For this volume (recognition/screening), the relevant anchor is Sepsis-3 (Singer et al., JAMA 2016) as operationalized by SSC 2021/2026.

02 Sepsis-3 Definitions STABLE

The Third International Consensus Definitions (Sepsis-3, Singer et al., JAMA 2016) remain the conceptual backbone carried forward into SSC 2021 and 2026. Two terms matter operationally:

Sepsis

Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.

Operationalized as: suspected or confirmed infection + an acute increase in SOFA score ≥ 2 points from baseline (baseline assumed 0 if no known preexisting organ dysfunction).

Septic Shock

A subset of sepsis with profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities associated with a greater risk of mortality.

Clinically: sepsis + vasopressor requirement to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg + lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. In-hospital mortality in the derivation cohorts exceeded 40%.

Why "severe sepsis" disappeared

Sepsis-2 (2001) required SIRS ≥2 + infection + organ dysfunction to reach "severe sepsis." Sepsis-3 retired that tier entirely: sepsis now is what used to be called severe sepsis, and SIRS was demoted from a diagnostic requirement to an optional screening aid. If you trained before ~2017 or still hear "severe sepsis" on rounds, that terminology is deprecated — it will not appear in current SSC-based order sets or CMS SEP-1-adjacent quality metrics as a distinct category.

Practical tension you'll feel at the bedside

Sepsis-3's SOFA-based definition is precise for research and mortality-risk stratification but requires labs (creatinine, bilirubin, platelets, PaO2/FiO2) that aren't back at triage. That's the entire reason SIRS and qSOFA persist clinically — they're what you have before the SOFA-relevant data returns. Section 3 onward is about the tradeoffs each screening tool makes to fill that gap.

03 SIRS Criteria

Bone et al., 1992 ACCP/SCCM consensus. ≥2 of 4 criteria = SIRS positive. It was never designed to diagnose sepsis specifically — it was designed to flag physiologic stress broadly, sepsis included.

Temperature
>38.0°C or <36.0°C
Core or equivalent. Note: many septic elderly and immunocompromised patients never mount fever — see box below.
Heart rate
>90 bpm
The single most confounded criterion in the set. Full breakdown in Section 4.
Respiratory rate
>20/min or PaCO₂ <32 mmHg
Often the earliest abnormal vital sign and the most under-documented by triage staff.
White blood cell count
>12,000 or <4,000/mm³, or >10% bands
The original 1992 criterion includes the band/immature-neutrophil clause — most EHR-embedded SIRS calculators drop it because differentials with band counts are inconsistently resulted, which quietly inflates measured SIRS-negative rates in database studies (see NEJM 2015;373:879 correspondence, Section 8).

Bottom line for chart defensibility

SIRS is high-sensitivity, low-specificity — it is built to over-trigger. That's a feature for a screening tool (you don't want to silently walk past sepsis), but it means a SIRS-positive patient is not thereby "septic" — pancreatitis, trauma, post-op state, PE, and simple dehydration all trigger SIRS routinely. Conversely, do not chart "SIRS-negative, sepsis excluded" as a stand-alone rule-out — in the ED/undifferentiated population, roughly a quarter to a third of patients who go on to need ICU-level care or die at 48h were SIRS-negative on the numbers above.

Fever absence is not sepsis absence

Elderly patients (blunted febrile response, up to ~20–30% present afebrile with serious bacterial infection in several geriatric cohorts), immunocompromised/neutropenic patients, patients on antipyretics or chronic NSAIDs, and patients with CKD/ESRD (impaired thermoregulation) can all be profoundly septic while normothermic or hypothermic. Hypothermic sepsis (<36°C) is itself an independent marker of higher mortality — it is not a reassuring finding.

04 The HR>90 Problem

You asked specifically how to think about a patient who's tachycardic "from pain or something" — this is worth its own section because heart rate is simultaneously the most sensitive-feeling vital sign at triage and the least specific SIRS criterion in practice. There isn't a single published "sensitivity of HR>90 for sepsis when confounded by pain" statistic — that's a clinical-reasoning problem, not a validated instrument — so what follows is the physiology and the differential, framed explicitly as clinical reasoning rather than a cited trial finding.

Causes tachycardia — mimics sepsis (false positive)

Acute pain — sympathetic surge from nociception alone can push HR well past 90–100; typically improves within 15–30 min of adequate analgesia if pain was the sole driver.

Anxiety / agitation / panic — catecholamine-driven, usually resolves with reassurance or brief observation.

Non-septic hypovolemia — GI losses, poor PO intake, diuretic overuse.

New arrhythmia — afib/aflutter with RVR, SVT — check the rhythm, not just the rate.

Non-infectious fever — drug fever, thyroid storm, heat illness, malignant hyperthermia, serotonin syndrome/NMS.

Physiologic states — pregnancy (baseline HR runs 10–20 bpm above non-pregnant norms by the 3rd trimester — see Volume 5), pediatric age-based norms, deconditioning.

Stimulants / withdrawal — sympathomimetics, caffeine, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, albuterol/beta-agonist nebs just given in the ED.

PE — tachycardia without fever; keep on the differential when SIRS-positive but source-negative.

Anemia — compensatory tachycardia, especially acute GI bleed.

Masks tachycardia — false reassurance (false negative)

Beta-blockers — the single most important one to actively check the med list for. A septic patient on metoprolol may sit at HR 78–85 despite hemodynamic compromise; the chronotropic response is blunted, not absent — look for a relative tachycardia (20+ bpm above their documented baseline) instead of an absolute threshold.

Non-dihydropyridine CCBs (diltiazem, verapamil) — same principle.

Chronotropic incompetence — elderly patients, sinus node dysfunction, permanent pacemakers programmed to a fixed or narrow-range rate.

High-level spinal cord injury — impaired sympathetic outflow.

Hypothyroidism — blunts the expected tachycardic response.

Highly conditioned athletes — high resting vagal tone; their "90" may already represent a significant relative rise from a baseline of 50–55.

Practical approach

1. Trend it, don't snapshot it. A single triage HR of 96 in a patient rating pain 8/10 is low-yield in isolation. Recheck after analgesia — persistent tachycardia despite adequate pain control and fluids is far more concerning than an isolated number.

2. Anchor to the patient's own baseline, not the population threshold, whenever prior vitals are available in the chart — especially for beta-blocked or elderly patients.

3. Never let HR carry the decision alone. SIRS requires ≥2 criteria for a reason; combine with RR, temperature trend, WBC, mentation, and lactate before committing to (or excluding) a sepsis workup.

4. Document the confounder explicitly if you're deliberately not pursuing a sepsis workup despite a positive criterion — "HR 104, improved to 78 after 1000mg IV acetaminophen and pain control, no other SIRS criteria, reassuring against sepsis" is a defensible chart note; a silent SIRS-positive vital sign with no documented reasoning is not.

05 qSOFA

Introduced alongside Sepsis-3 (Seymour et al., JAMA 2016) as a bedside prompt — deliberately not a diagnostic tool — to flag infected patients at higher risk of a poor outcome outside the ICU, where full SOFA labs aren't readily available.

Respiratory rate
≥ 22/min
Systolic BP
≤ 100 mmHg
Altered mentation
GCS < 15

≥2 of 3 = qSOFA positive — associated with higher risk of mortality/prolonged ICU stay in the derivation and multiple validation cohorts.

SSC explicitly discourages qSOFA as a stand-alone screen

Across the ED/prehospital validation literature, qSOFA's specificity is consistently strong but its sensitivity is consistently the weakest of the three major tools — in some ED triage cohorts as low as ~30% for predicting ICU admission. SSC 2021 guidance (carried into 2026) is to not rely on qSOFA alone; use it alongside SIRS, NEWS/MEWS, and lactate rather than as a replacement screening gate. A qSOFA-negative patient can absolutely still be septic — treat a negative qSOFA as "does not rule out," not "ruled out."

07 NEWS2 & Other Early Warning Tools

SSC guidance (2021, carried into 2026) lists NEWS/NEWS2 and MEWS as acceptable adjuncts alongside or instead of SIRS/qSOFA for hospital-wide early warning, particularly because NEWS2 is already embedded in many EHR vitals-based alerting systems in the UK/NHS and increasingly in US systems.

ParameterScored range
Respiratory rate0–3 points across bands from ≤8 to ≥25/min
SpO₂ (Scale 1 & Scale 2 for hypercapnic COPD)0–3 points
Supplemental O₂0 or 2 points
Temperature0–3 points, both hyper- and hypothermia scored
Systolic BP0–3 points across bands
Heart rate0–3 points across bands
Level of consciousness / new confusion0 or 3 points

A meta-analysis comparing qSOFA, SIRS, and NEWS for mortality prediction in suspected sepsis (Fernando et al.-type pooled analyses; see references) consistently finds the same pattern across tools: no single score achieves both high sensitivity and high specificity — each trades one for the other, which is the unifying lesson of this entire volume.

09 Bedside Algorithm

STEP 1 — Suspected infection (clinical gestalt, source symptoms, or incidental finding) — this is the trigger. No score replaces suspicion.
↓
STEP 2 — Calculate SIRS and qSOFA in parallel, not sequentially. Check the med list for beta-blockers/CCBs and the chart for the patient's own vital sign baseline before interpreting either.
↓
STEP 3 — Any positive screen (SIRS ≥2 or qSOFA ≥2 or strong clinical gestalt despite a negative score) → send lactate, blood cultures per Volume 2 thresholds, CBC with differential, BMP/LFTs for SOFA-relevant data, and begin the source workup.
↓
STEP 4 — Isolated single abnormal vital sign (e.g., HR>90 alone) with a plausible non-infectious explanation → treat the confounder (analgesia, anxiolysis, volume for non-septic hypovolemia), then recheck vitals before deciding to stand down. Document the reasoning either way.
↓
STEP 5 — Labs return → calculate true SOFA delta from baseline. Infection + SOFA rise ≥2 = sepsis by definition → proceed to Volume 3 (antibiotics/source control) and Volume 4 (hemodynamics) on the SSC 1-hour bundle clock.

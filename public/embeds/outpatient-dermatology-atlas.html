Full-Time Practice · Outpatient Dermatology

Outpatient Dermatology Atlas

Work through these five steps in order for any new rash in clinic. Each step narrows the differential and tells you what to do next — treat, biopsy, or escalate.

1

1. Acute or Chronic?

How long has this been present?

Acute (days, new onset)

Think: drug reaction, contact dermatitis, viral exanthem, urticaria, acute infection — go to Step 2 with an acute lens.

Chronic/recurrent (weeks-months-years)

Think: psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, lichen planus, chronic contact dermatitis, cutaneous lupus — go to Step 2 with a chronic lens.

2

2. Primary Morphology — what does the individual lesion actually look like?

Papulosquamous (scaly plaques)? Vesiculobullous (fluid-filled)? Urticarial (wheals, migratory, <24h per lesion)? Purpuric (non-blanching)? Pustular?

Papulosquamous

Psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, seborrheic dermatitis, tinea, lichen planus, secondary syphilis, pityriasis rosea.

Vesiculobullous

HSV/VZV, contact dermatitis (allergic, can vesiculate), bullous pemphigoid, dyshidrotic eczema — if mucosal + skin sloughing, treat as an emergency (SJS/TEN) and don't manage in routine clinic.

Urticarial (each lesion resolves <24h, migrates)

Acute or chronic urticaria, urticarial drug reaction — ask about a new medication in the last 1-6 weeks specifically.

Purpuric / non-blanching

STOP — this changes the entire workup. Vasculitis, thrombocytopenia, coagulopathy, or a severe infectious process need same-day evaluation, not routine derm follow-up; blanch-test every concerning rash.

Pustular

Acne, folliculitis, pustular psoriasis, acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP, drug-related) — ask about recent new medication if widespread and febrile.

3

3. Distribution Pattern

Flexural vs extensor? Photodistributed (face/dorsal hands/V of neck, sparing under chin/behind ears)? Dermatomal? Follicular? Palms/soles?

Flexural (antecubital/popliteal fossae)

Classic atopic dermatitis distribution in older children/adults.

Extensor surfaces (elbows, knees, scalp)

Classic psoriasis distribution.

Photodistributed, sparing shaded areas

Photosensitive drug eruption or cutaneous lupus — ask about new medications and sun exposure timing.

Strictly unilateral, dermatomal, stops at midline

Herpes zoster — even without classic vesicles yet, dermatomal pain/burning preceding a rash is highly suggestive.

Palms and soles specifically involved

Secondary syphilis, hand-foot-mouth disease (children), some drug reactions, dyshidrotic eczema — a rash that classically SPARES palms/soles (like most viral exanthems) becomes notable when it doesn't.

4

4. Associated Symptoms & Systemic Signs

Itch vs. pain vs. burning? Fever? Mucosal involvement? Joint pain? Recent new medication (check the 1-6 week window even for drugs started a while ago)?

Itch-dominant, well-appearing

Most inflammatory dermatoses (eczema, psoriasis, urticaria, contact dermatitis) — proceed with routine outpatient workup.

Pain out of proportion to what you see, or rapidly progressive

Consider necrotizing soft tissue infection or a severe drug reaction — this is a same-day-escalation red flag, not a routine follow-up.

Fever + rash + mucosal involvement (eyes/mouth/genitals)

Red flag for SJS/TEN, DRESS, or Kawasaki disease (children) — refer/escalate urgently, do not manage as routine dermatitis.

New joint pain, photosensitivity, or oral ulcers alongside the rash

Consider a connective tissue disease workup (lupus, dermatomyositis) rather than treating as isolated dermatitis.

5

5. Decide: Treat Empirically, Biopsy, or Refer/Escalate

Based on the above, does this fit a classic, low-risk pattern; does the diagnosis remain unclear after morphology + distribution; or are there red flags present?

Classic pattern, no red flags

Treat empirically per the relevant condition card in this atlas; schedule routine follow-up to confirm response.

Diagnosis unclear, or atypical/persistent despite first-line treatment

Punch or shave biopsy (see Procedural Dermatology tab) rather than continuing to guess — a non-diagnostic empiric treatment trial that fails is a common, avoidable delay.

Any red flag from Steps 2-4 (purpura, mucosal involvement, pain out of proportion, systemic symptoms)

Escalate same-day — do not schedule routine follow-up for these.

A reusable framework for charting any skin finding — use this alongside the condition-specific documentation language on each card. Consistent structure here speeds chart review, protects against under-documentation, and supports accurate coding.

HPI Elements to Capture for Any Rash

Onset &amp; Duration

When did it start? Sudden or gradual? Constant or intermittent/relapsing-remitting?

Evolution

Has it spread, changed color, changed shape, or resolved and recurred? Photos at each visit are the single best objective evolution record.

Associated Symptoms

Pruritus vs. pain vs. burning vs. numbness — and specifically ask about systemic symptoms (fever, joint pain, mucosal involvement) since their presence/absence changes the differential and urgency.

Prior Treatment &amp; Response

What has been tried (OTC, prescription, home remedies), for how long, and what was the response? A treatment that "didn't work" after 3 days of a regimen that takes 6-8 weeks isn't a true treatment failure — document duration of prior use, not just what was used.

Exposure History

New products, medications (including start date relative to rash onset — check the 1-6 week drug-reaction window), occupational/hobby exposures, travel, sick contacts, sun exposure pattern.

Relevant PMH/FHx

Atopic history (asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema), autoimmune disease, family history of skin cancer or the condition in question, immunosuppression status.

Morphology Checklist

Primary Lesion Type

Macule, patch, papule, plaque, nodule, vesicle, bulla, pustule, wheal, or ulcer — name the primary lesion before describing anything secondary.

Secondary Changes

Scale, crust, excoriation, lichenification, atrophy, scarring — these often tell you about CHRONICITY and patient behavior (scratching) as much as the primary disease process.

Color

Erythematous, violaceous, hyperpigmented, hypopigmented, skin-colored — note any color that doesn't blanch with pressure (a critical distinguishing detail from an inflammatory erythema).

Size &amp; Measurement

Measure in cm/mm rather than using only relative terms ("quarter-sized") — precise measurement at each visit is what actually lets you and any future reader assess growth/response objectively.

Configuration

Annular (ring-shaped), linear, grouped/herpetiform, target-shaped, reticulated (net-like) — configuration is often more diagnostically specific than color or primary lesion type alone.

Distribution

Localized vs. generalized; symmetric vs. asymmetric; specific pattern (flexural, extensor, photodistributed, dermatomal, acral) — always state distribution explicitly rather than relying on an attached photo to convey it.

Coding Notes

Atopic dermatitis

L20.9 (unspecified) vs. more specific L20.8x codes when a specific pattern is documented — specificity in the note supports specificity in coding.

Psoriasis

L40.0 (plaque) is the default; L40.5x psoriatic arthropathy codes require documenting joint findings explicitly — don't let a real PsA diagnosis go uncoded because the joint exam wasn't documented.

Actinic keratosis vs. SCC

L57.0 (AK) vs. C44.x (SCC) — the biopsy result, not clinical impression alone, should generally drive which code is used once tissue diagnosis is available; document pending-biopsy status clearly in the interim.

Melanocytic lesion biopsies

Document the clinical differential (including melanoma) BEFORE the biopsy result returns — this supports medical necessity for the procedure and protects against a payer questioning why a biopsy was performed on a lesion later read as benign.

Template

General template: "[Duration]-[onset pattern] [primary lesion type] with [secondary changes], [color], distributed in a [pattern] distribution over the [body sites], [associated symptoms]. [Pertinent negatives]. [Exposure/trigger history if relevant]."

01

Psoriasis (Plaque-Type)

Morphology / Appearance

Well-demarcated, erythematous plaques with thick, silvery-white scale; Auspitz sign (pinpoint bleeding when scale is removed) supports the diagnosis clinically.

Distribution

Classic extensor distribution — elbows, knees, scalp, lower back, and the intergluteal cleft; nail pitting/onycholysis and joint symptoms (psoriatic arthritis) should be actively screened for at every visit.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

An IMMUNE-MEDIATED disease, not primarily an infectious or purely genetic skin barrier problem — dendritic cells and T-cells (especially the IL-23/IL-17 axis) drive keratinocyte hyperproliferation, shortening the normal ~28-day epidermal turnover cycle to as little as 3-5 days; this accelerated, incomplete maturation is what produces the thick, silvery scale (retained nuclei in the stratum corneum, parakeratosis). Koebner phenomenon (new plaques at sites of trauma) reflects the same IL-17-driven inflammatory cascade being triggered locally.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis; biopsy reserved for atypical presentations or when the diagnosis is genuinely unclear. Screen for psoriatic arthritis symptoms (joint pain/stiffness, dactylitis) at every visit since missed PsA has real joint-damage consequences.

Management

Topical corticosteroids ± vitamin D analogs (calcipotriene) for limited disease; phototherapy (narrowband UVB) for more widespread disease; biologic therapy (IL-17, IL-23, or TNF-alpha inhibitors) for moderate-severe disease or when topical/phototherapy fails — biologic selection is increasingly guided by comorbid psoriatic arthritis, since some agents treat both effectively.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Topical therapy: reassess at 4 weeks (some visible improvement expected by then, though full plaque clearance can take 8-12 weeks). Phototherapy: typically 2-3x/week for 8-12 weeks to assess response. Biologics: primary response typically assessed at 12-16 weeks per the specific agent's trial data — don't judge biologic failure before this window.

Red Flags

Erythrodermic psoriasis (>75-90% body surface area involvement) or generalized pustular psoriasis are dermatologic emergencies requiring same-day evaluation, not routine follow-up.

Documentation Language

"Chronic, well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery-white scale over bilateral elbows/knees/scalp, [X]% BSA involvement, +/- nail pitting/onycholysis, denies/reports joint pain or morning stiffness (screen for PsA). Auspitz sign [present/not assessed]."

Clinical Pearl

Always screen for psoriatic arthritis at every visit, even years into a patient's psoriasis diagnosis — PsA can develop well after the skin disease and is frequently under-recognized until joint damage is already present.

02

Atopic Dermatitis (Eczema)

Morphology / Appearance

Poorly-demarcated, erythematous, scaly, often excoriated patches/plaques; chronic lesions show lichenification (thickened, leathery skin with accentuated skin lines from chronic scratching).

Distribution

Age-dependent: infants — face and extensor surfaces; older children/adults — classic flexural distribution (antecubital and popliteal fossae, neck, wrists, ankles).

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

A combined skin-barrier-defect and immune-dysregulation disease — filaggrin gene mutations (present in a meaningful subset of patients) impair the stratum corneum's structural integrity and lipid barrier, increasing transepidermal water loss and allergen/irritant penetration; this barrier defect then permits a Th2-skewed immune response (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31 — IL-31 specifically drives the intense itch) that perpetuates the inflammatory cycle. The "itch-scratch cycle" is a real pathophysiologic feedback loop, not just a behavioral descriptor — mechanical scratching itself releases additional inflammatory mediators.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis in the vast majority of cases; consider patch testing if a superimposed allergic contact dermatitis is suspected (worsening despite good routine management, or an atypical distribution suggesting an external trigger).

Management

Emollients/moisturizers as the foundation for ALL severity levels (not just an adjunct); topical corticosteroids for flares, stepped by potency to the affected body site (low-potency for face/skin folds, higher-potency for thicker-skinned areas); topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) as steroid-sparing options, especially for facial/eyelid involvement; systemic options (dupilumab, JAK inhibitors) for moderate-severe disease refractory to topical management.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Emollients: continuous, indefinite use as maintenance — not just during flares. Topical corticosteroids: typically 1-2 weeks for an acute flare, then taper/discontinue and resume emollient-only maintenance; reassess sooner if no improvement in 1 week. Systemic agents (dupilumab, JAK inhibitors): initial response often visible by 4 weeks, fuller effect by 16 weeks.

Red Flags

Eczema herpeticum (widespread, punched-out vesicles/erosions, often with fever, on a background of atopic dermatitis) is a dermatologic emergency requiring same-day antiviral treatment — don't mistake it for a simple flare.

Documentation Language

"[Infant: facial/extensor] or [Older child/adult: flexural] erythematous, scaly, excoriated patches with [lichenification if chronic]. [X]% BSA. Sleep disruption [present/absent] — a validated marker of disease severity beyond visual BSA alone. No vesiculation/punched-out erosions concerning for eczema herpeticum."

Clinical Pearl

"Soak and smear" (bathing followed immediately by moisturizer application while skin is still damp) meaningfully improves barrier function and reduces flare frequency — this practical counseling point is often more impactful for day-to-day control than escalating medication alone.

03

Acne Vulgaris

Morphology / Appearance

Comedones (open/closed), inflammatory papules/pustules, and — in more severe disease — nodules/cysts that can scar; graded mild/moderate/severe based on lesion type/count and scarring risk.

Distribution

Face, chest, and back — areas with the highest density of sebaceous follicles; distribution/pattern can hint at an underlying driver (jawline/chin predominance in adult women raises consideration of a hormonal component).

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Four converging pathophysiologic factors: (1) follicular hyperkeratinization (abnormal desquamation plugging the follicle), (2) increased sebum production (androgen-driven), (3) Cutibacterium acnes proliferation within the anaerobic, lipid-rich plugged follicle, and (4) the resulting inflammatory response to C. acnes and its byproducts — modern understanding frames inflammation as present even in comedonal ("non-inflammatory") lesions at a subclinical level, not as a separate later stage.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis; consider a hormonal workup (PCOS screening — irregular menses, hirsutism, elevated androgens) in adult women with jawline-predominant acne, especially with other suggestive symptoms.

Management

Topical retinoids are the foundation of most regimens (comedolytic + anti-inflammatory); benzoyl peroxide added for its antibacterial effect and to reduce resistance when combined with topical/oral antibiotics; topical or oral antibiotics for inflammatory disease (time-limited courses given resistance concerns); combined oral contraceptives or spironolactone for hormonally-driven adult female acne; isotretinoin for severe/scarring/treatment-refractory acne, with mandatory pregnancy prevention program compliance (iPLEDGE in the US) given its teratogenicity.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Topical therapy: minimum 6-8 weeks before judging efficacy — set this expectation explicitly at the visit it's prescribed. Oral antibiotics: reassess at 3-4 months maximum per AAD guidance, then de-escalate. Isotretinoin: full course typically 4-6 months (weight-based cumulative dosing), with monthly monitoring visits.

Red Flags

Acne fulminans (sudden, severe, ulcerative acne with systemic symptoms — fever, joint pain) is rare but requires urgent escalation, often with systemic corticosteroids alongside isotretinoin initiation under specialist guidance.

Documentation Language

"[Comedonal/inflammatory/nodulocystic] acne, IGA grade [1-4], distributed over [face/chest/back], [X] non-inflammatory and [X] inflammatory lesions, [presence/absence] of scarring or psychosocial impact reported."

Clinical Pearl

Set expectations explicitly that most acne regimens take 6-8 weeks to show meaningful improvement and often cause an initial "purging" period with retinoids — patients who don't know this frequently discontinue an effective regimen prematurely.

04

Rosacea

Morphology / Appearance

Centrofacial erythema, telangiectasias, and inflammatory papules/pustules — classically WITHOUT comedones (a key distinguishing feature from acne, despite superficial similarity); phymatous change (rhinophyma) represents a more advanced, sebaceous-gland-driven subtype.

Distribution

Centrofacial — cheeks, nose, chin, forehead; periorbital sparing is common and can help distinguish it from other facial dermatoses.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Neurovascular dysregulation (abnormal vasodilation/flushing response), innate immune system overactivation (elevated cathelicidin peptide activity), and a proposed role for Demodex mite density and cutaneous microbiome alterations — unlike acne, comedone formation is not part of the core pathophysiology, which is why comedolytic-focused acne treatments don't address rosacea's actual disease process.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis; ask about trigger exposures (sun, heat, spicy food, alcohol, stress) since trigger avoidance is a foundational management component alongside medication.

Management

Topical metronidazole, azelaic acid, or ivermectin for the inflammatory papulopustular component; topical brimonidine or oxymetazoline for background erythema/flushing specifically (these are vasoconstrictors, treating a different aspect of the disease than the anti-inflammatory agents); oral doxycycline (at anti-inflammatory, sub-antimicrobial dosing) for more significant inflammatory disease; laser/light-based therapy for persistent telangiectasias that don't respond to topical treatment.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Topical therapy: reassess inflammatory component at 8-12 weeks. Oral doxycycline (anti-inflammatory dosing): typically a 12-16 week course, then reassess for maintenance need. Background erythema agents (brimonidine/oxymetazoline): effect is same-day but temporary (hours), not a cumulative disease-modifying treatment — set this expectation distinctly from the anti-inflammatory agents.

Red Flags

Ocular rosacea (dry, irritated, red eyes with lid margin involvement) is common and under-recognized — ask about eye symptoms specifically, since untreated ocular rosacea can threaten vision in severe cases and needs ophthalmology co-management.

Documentation Language

"Centrofacial erythema and telangiectasias with [X] inflammatory papules/pustules, no comedones noted. [Phymatous changes present/absent]. Ocular symptoms (dryness, irritation, lid margin findings) [screened, present/absent]. Trigger history: [sun/heat/alcohol/spicy food/stress]."

Clinical Pearl

Distinguishing rosacea from acne matters clinically because benzoyl peroxide and some acne regimens can actually WORSEN rosacea's inflammatory component — don't reflexively apply an acne-style regimen to a centrofacial eruption without confirming the diagnosis first.

05

Seborrheic Dermatitis

Morphology / Appearance

Erythematous patches with greasy, yellowish scale — distinct from psoriasis's drier, more silvery scale, though the two can coexist and overlap ("sebopsoriasis").

Distribution

Areas with high sebaceous gland density — scalp, eyebrows, nasolabial folds, ears, and central chest; infantile seborrheic dermatitis ("cradle cap") is a distinct, generally self-limited entity in the pediatric population.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Driven by an inflammatory response to Malassezia yeast (a normal skin commensal) metabolizing sebum lipids into irritating free fatty acids, in genetically/immunologically susceptible individuals — this is why antifungal agents targeting Malassezia are effective even though the yeast itself is not considered a true "infection" in the conventional sense.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis; persistent, severe, or treatment-refractory seborrheic dermatitis in an adult should prompt consideration of HIV testing, since severe seborrheic dermatitis can be a presenting sign of underlying immunosuppression.

Management

Antifungal shampoos/creams (ketoconazole, selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione) targeting the Malassezia yeast implicated in its pathogenesis; low-potency topical corticosteroids for more inflammatory flares, used intermittently rather than continuously given facial/scalp steroid-atrophy risk with prolonged use.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Antifungal shampoo/topical: response typically within 2-4 weeks; this is a chronic-relapsing condition requiring intermittent (not necessarily continuous) maintenance treatment indefinitely, not a one-time cure.

Red Flags

No acute emergency association, but disproportionately severe/refractory disease is the red flag described above (immunosuppression workup).

Documentation Language

"Erythematous patches with greasy yellowish scale over scalp/eyebrows/nasolabial folds/ears. [Severity/extent]. [If refractory/severe in an adult: HIV risk factors and testing discussed]."

Clinical Pearl

Cradle cap in infants is generally reassuring and self-limited, resolving over months without aggressive treatment — gentle emollients and mineral oil to soften scale before washing is usually sufficient, and overly aggressive treatment causes more parental anxiety than the condition warrants.

06

Allergic vs. Irritant Contact Dermatitis

Morphology / Appearance

Allergic: well-demarcated, often vesicular/weepy, intensely pruritic patches, classically with sharp geometric borders or streaking that hints at an external cause (a plant brushing against skin, a piece of jewelry). Irritant: more diffuse, often burning more than itching, with less sharp demarcation.

Distribution

Directly correlates with the site of allergen/irritant contact — a shape that doesn't match a known dermatosis pattern (a rectangle, a streak, a ring matching a piece of jewelry) is itself a diagnostic clue.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Allergic: a true type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity reaction — requires prior sensitization to the specific allergen, with re-exposure triggering a T-cell-mediated inflammatory cascade 24-72 hours later (the delay is why patients often don't connect the rash to the causative exposure). Irritant: direct chemical/physical damage to the skin barrier without an immune sensitization step — can occur on FIRST exposure in anyone given sufficient concentration/duration of contact, unlike allergic contact dermatitis.

Workup

Patch testing is the gold-standard diagnostic tool for allergic contact dermatitis when the culprit allergen isn't obvious from history — a detailed exposure history (new products, occupational exposures, hobbies) is the essential first step before reflexively ordering patch testing.

Management

Identify and avoid the causative agent (the single most important intervention — no medication substitutes for allergen avoidance); topical corticosteroids for the inflammatory component; cool compresses and barrier creams for irritant contact dermatitis specifically, where the goal is restoring skin barrier function rather than suppressing an allergic response.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Allergen/irritant avoidance: improvement typically begins within days once the causative exposure stops; topical corticosteroid course typically 1-2 weeks. Patch testing results (if performed) take 48-96 hours (readings at 48h and 72-96h) to finalize.

Red Flags

Widespread or facial/periorbital involvement with significant swelling can mimic angioedema or a more serious process — assess airway/systemic symptoms if the reaction is severe or rapidly progressive.

Documentation Language

"[Geometric/linear/well-demarcated] [vesicular/weeping or diffuse/dry] dermatitis corresponding to [specific contact site/pattern]. Exposure history: [new product/plant/occupational exposure] within [timeframe]. Patch testing [performed/recommended/not indicated]."

Clinical Pearl

Poison ivy/oak/sumac (urushiol-induced allergic contact dermatitis) classically shows LINEAR streaking where the plant brushed against skin — this pattern is nearly diagnostic on its own even without a clear history of plant exposure.

Lichen Planus

Morphology / Appearance

Classic "6 Ps": Purple, Polygonal, Planar (flat-topped), Pruritic Papules and Plaques; fine white lines (Wickham's striae) are visible on the surface of lesions with close inspection, and oral involvement (white, lacy patches on the buccal mucosa) is common and should be actively examined for.

Distribution

Flexor surfaces of wrists/forearms, ankles, and lower back are classic; the Koebner phenomenon (new lesions arising at sites of skin trauma/scratching) is characteristic.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

A T-cell-mediated autoimmune process directly targeting basal keratinocytes (a "lichenoid" pattern of dense band-like lymphocytic infiltrate at the dermal-epidermal junction on histology) — the specific triggering antigen is often unknown, though hepatitis C, certain medications (drug-induced lichenoid reactions), and dental restoration materials (in oral lichen planus specifically) are recognized associations in a subset of patients.

Workup

Often a clinical diagnosis given the classic morphology, but biopsy is frequently pursued given overlap with other papulosquamous conditions and to confirm before starting more aggressive treatment; screen for hepatitis C, which has a recognized association with lichen planus.

Management

Topical corticosteroids (often higher-potency, given the disease's relative steroid-resistance compared to eczema) for cutaneous disease; intralesional corticosteroid injection for thick, localized plaques; oral corticosteroids or other systemic immunomodulators for widespread or refractory disease; oral lichen planus specifically requires closer surveillance given a small but real increased risk of oral squamous cell carcinoma with chronic erosive disease.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Topical/intralesional corticosteroids: reassess at 4-6 weeks; cutaneous lichen planus often resolves spontaneously over 1-2 years even with treatment, though oral erosive disease frequently requires longer-term, sometimes indefinite maintenance therapy given its chronicity and malignant-transformation surveillance need.

Red Flags

Erosive oral or genital lichen planus causing significant pain/dysfunction, or any suspicious oral lesion showing induration/ulceration in chronic disease, warrants biopsy to exclude malignant transformation.

Documentation Language

"Violaceous, flat-topped, polygonal papules/plaques with Wickham's striae over [flexor wrists/ankles/lower back]. Oral mucosa: [lacy white patches present/absent, erosive changes present/absent]. Koebner phenomenon [present/absent]. Hepatitis C screening [performed/pending/not indicated]."

Clinical Pearl

Actively examine the oral mucosa in any patient with a new lichen planus diagnosis — oral involvement is common, often asymptomatic early on, and its presence/absence changes both the differential consideration (hepatitis C screening) and long-term surveillance needs.

08

Bullous Pemphigoid

Morphology / Appearance

Tense, fluid-filled bullae on an erythematous or urticarial-appearing base — the bullae are TENSE (don't rupture easily) in contrast to more fragile blistering conditions, and Nikolsky's sign is classically NEGATIVE (distinguishing it from pemphigus vulgaris, where it's positive).

Distribution

Trunk and extremities, often with a preceding urticarial/eczematous prodrome for weeks before frank bullae appear; predominantly affects OLDER adults (typically >60), unlike pemphigus vulgaris which can affect a younger population.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

An autoimmune process with IgG autoantibodies targeting BP180 (type XVII collagen) and BP230, hemidesmosomal proteins that anchor the epidermis to the basement membrane — antibody binding activates complement and recruits inflammatory cells (notably eosinophils) that damage the dermal-epidermal junction, causing the SUBEPIDERMAL split responsible for tense (not fragile) bullae, distinct from pemphigus vulgaris's intraepidermal split.

Workup

Biopsy with direct immunofluorescence is required for definitive diagnosis, showing a characteristic linear band of IgG/C3 deposition at the dermal-epidermal junction — this is not a diagnosis to manage purely clinically given how much the treatment differs from other blistering conditions.

Management

Topical or systemic corticosteroids depending on extent of disease; steroid-sparing immunosuppressants (doxycycline, dapsone, or more potent immunosuppressants) for widespread or steroid-refractory disease; the biologic dupilumab has emerging evidence as an effective, better-tolerated option, an important development given how poorly older adults with bullous pemphigoid often tolerate prolonged systemic corticosteroids.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Systemic corticosteroids: initial control typically within 1-2 weeks, then a prolonged taper over months guided by clinical response; steroid-sparing agents (dapsone, doxycycline, dupilumab) are typically continued long-term (many months to years) given the disease's chronic, relapsing course in older adults.

Red Flags

Widespread bullae with significant body surface area involvement in an elderly patient carries real morbidity/mortality risk (infection, fluid losses) — this population often has more comorbidities and less physiologic reserve than younger blistering-disease patients, making the practical management challenges different even when the disease itself is present.

Documentation Language

"Tense bullae on erythematous/urticarial base over trunk/extremities, Nikolsky sign negative. [X]% BSA involvement. Biopsy with DIF [performed/pending] showing [linear IgG/C3 at DEJ if available]. Preceding pruritic/urticarial prodrome duration: [X weeks]."

Clinical Pearl

A NEW bullous eruption in an older adult should prompt dermatology referral for biopsy with direct immunofluorescence rather than empiric treatment — bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris, and drug-induced blistering conditions require different management, and getting this wrong has real consequences.

01

Actinic Keratosis

Morphology / Appearance

Rough, scaly, erythematous papules/patches, often easier to feel than see ("sandpaper" texture) — considered a precursor lesion on a continuum toward squamous cell carcinoma rather than a cancer itself.

Distribution

Chronically sun-exposed areas — face, ears, scalp (in bald men), dorsal hands/forearms; multiple lesions are common given shared cumulative UV exposure across the affected area.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Cumulative UV-induced DNA damage (particularly TP53 mutations) in epidermal keratinocytes produces a clone of atypical, dysplastic cells confined to the epidermis — AK exists on a histologic and biologic continuum with SCC in situ and invasive SCC, which is why it's classified as a precursor lesion rather than a distinct, unrelated entity.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis; biopsy if a lesion is indurated, tender, rapidly growing, ulcerated, or otherwise atypical for a simple AK — these features suggest possible progression to invasive SCC.

Management

Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen) for isolated/few lesions is the most common in-office treatment; field therapy (topical 5-fluorouracil, imiquimod, or photodynamic therapy) for widespread actinic damage/multiple subclinical lesions, treating the whole affected field rather than each lesion individually.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Cryotherapy: single treatment often sufficient per lesion, healing over 1-2 weeks; reassess treated site at that interval. Field therapy (5-FU, imiquimod): typically 2-4 week application courses depending on agent and tolerability, with expected significant irritation/erythema during treatment that resolves after completion — counsel on this expected reaction explicitly to prevent premature discontinuation.

Red Flags

A lesion that becomes tender, indurated, or rapidly enlarges should raise concern for transformation to invasive SCC and prompt biopsy rather than continued cryotherapy.

Documentation Language

"Rough, erythematous, scaly [papule/patch] on [chronically sun-exposed site], [size]. [Tender/indurated/ulcerated — if present, biopsy performed given concern for progression to invasive SCC]. [Number of similar lesions in the surrounding field]."

Clinical Pearl

AKs are simultaneously extremely common and a genuine SCC risk marker — the actual rate of any individual AK progressing to invasive SCC is low, but the presence of multiple AKs identifies a patient population worth closer periodic skin cancer surveillance overall, not just treatment of the visible lesions.

02

Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

Morphology / Appearance

Classic nodular BCC: a pearly, translucent papule/nodule with rolled borders and often visible telangiectasias, sometimes with central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"); superficial BCC presents as a more subtle, scaly, erythematous patch that can mimic eczema/psoriasis if not examined closely.

Distribution

Overwhelmingly on sun-exposed skin, especially the head and neck — the most common malignancy overall, reflecting cumulative UV exposure as the dominant driver.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Driven predominantly by UV-induced mutations activating the Hedgehog signaling pathway (most commonly via PTCH1 loss-of-function or SMO activating mutations) — this specific pathway dependency is exactly why hedgehog-pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib) are effective in advanced disease, a direct mechanism-to-treatment link.

Workup

Shave or punch biopsy to confirm diagnosis and histologic subtype before definitive treatment — subtype (nodular vs. superficial vs. more aggressive infiltrative/morpheaform) meaningfully changes both risk assessment and treatment choice.

Management

Surgical excision with margins, or Mohs micrographic surgery for high-risk locations (face, especially around the eyes/nose/ears/lips) or aggressive histologic subtypes, given Mohs's superior margin control and tissue-sparing advantage in cosmetically/functionally sensitive areas. Electrodesiccation and curettage is an option for low-risk, superficial BCC in non-critical locations. Topical imiquimod or 5-FU can be used for superficial BCC when surgery isn't preferred/feasible. Advanced/metastatic (rare) BCC has hedgehog-pathway-inhibitor targeted therapy (vismodegib, sonidegib) as a systemic option.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Surgical excision/Mohs: single definitive procedure, with suture removal/wound check typically at 1-2 weeks. Topical therapy (imiquimod/5-FU for superficial BCC): typically 6 weeks of application with reassessment 4-8 weeks after completion to confirm clearance. Recurrence surveillance: skin checks every 6-12 months indefinitely given elevated risk of a second skin cancer.

Red Flags

BCC essentially never metastasizes, but LOCAL tissue destruction can be extensive and disfiguring if neglected/untreated for a long time, especially around the eyes/nose — this is the actual risk to communicate, not death from metastasis.

Documentation Language

"[Pearly/translucent papule with rolled borders and telangiectasias, +/- central ulceration] or [erythematous, scaly patch] on [sun-exposed site], [size]. Biopsy: [pending/confirms subtype: nodular/superficial/infiltrative/morpheaform]. Growth history: [duration, rate of change]."

Clinical Pearl

A patient hearing "basal cell carcinoma" often assumes the same mortality risk as melanoma — explicitly reassuring that BCC's danger is local tissue destruction rather than metastatic spread (while still emphasizing the real need for prompt treatment) improves both understanding and treatment adherence.

03

Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

Morphology / Appearance

Firm, often tender, hyperkeratotic or ulcerated papule/nodule/plaque — more likely to be tender than BCC, and grows more quickly on average; can arise de novo or from a pre-existing actinic keratosis.

Distribution

Sun-exposed skin similarly to BCC, but with a meaningfully higher risk of metastasis than BCC, especially for high-risk features (large size, poor differentiation, perineural invasion, immunosuppressed patients, or lip/ear location).

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

UV-induced TP53 and other mutations accumulate in keratinocytes, with a documented stepwise progression from actinic keratosis through SCC in situ (Bowen's disease) to invasive SCC in many (though not all) cases — chronic immunosuppression (transplant recipients) dramatically accelerates this progression by impairing immune surveillance of these accumulating mutant clones.

Workup

Biopsy (shave or punch) to confirm diagnosis and assess histologic risk features (differentiation grade, depth of invasion, perineural invasion) — these features directly drive whether sentinel lymph node evaluation or more aggressive surgical margins are needed.

Management

Surgical excision with margins guided by risk stratification, or Mohs surgery for high-risk features/locations, similar logic to BCC but with lower threshold given SCC's metastatic potential; regional lymph node evaluation/imaging for high-risk tumors; systemic therapy (immunotherapy — cemiplimab, pembrolizumab) for advanced/metastatic/unresectable disease, a significant recent advance for a population that previously had limited systemic options.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Surgical excision/Mohs: definitive single procedure. High-risk tumors: nodal imaging/evaluation typically arranged within 2-4 weeks of diagnosis. Post-treatment surveillance: every 3-6 months for the first 2 years (highest recurrence risk window), then extending to annual thereafter.

Red Flags

SCC arising on the lip, ear, or in a chronically immunosuppressed patient (transplant recipients specifically carry a markedly elevated SCC risk and more aggressive disease behavior) should be treated with a lower threshold for aggressive management and closer surveillance.

Documentation Language

"Firm, [tender/non-tender], [hyperkeratotic/ulcerated] papule/nodule on [site], [size]. Biopsy: [differentiation grade, depth of invasion, perineural invasion status if available]. Immunosuppression history: [transplant/other, duration]. Regional lymph node exam: [findings]."

Clinical Pearl

Solid organ transplant recipients have a dramatically elevated SCC risk (up to 100-fold in some populations) and their SCCs behave more aggressively — this population needs more frequent skin surveillance than general dermatology patients, and this should be built into their routine post-transplant care, not left to patient-initiated visits.

04

Melanoma

Morphology / Appearance

Follow the ABCDE rule for initial screening (Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Color variation, Diameter >6mm, Evolution/change) — but remember nodular melanoma often does NOT fit this pattern (see the Cancer Deep Dives Vol. II atlas) and instead presents as a rapidly growing, often symmetric, uniformly-colored nodule.

Distribution

Can occur anywhere, including non-sun-exposed sites (acral lentiginous subtype on palms/soles/nail beds, especially important to recognize in patients with darker skin where this is the predominant subtype) — see the Cancer Deep Dives Vol. II atlas for the full subtype breakdown.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

UV-induced mutations (classically BRAF V600E in roughly 40-50% of cutaneous melanomas, also NRAS, and others) transform melanocytes; unlike most carcinomas, melanoma has a well-recognized capacity for early hematogenous and lymphatic dissemination even from thin primary lesions, which is the biologic basis for sentinel lymph node biopsy thresholds being set at a relatively shallow Breslow depth compared to how deeply other cancers typically need to invade before nodal risk becomes significant.

Workup

Excisional biopsy with narrow margins (1-3mm) is preferred over shave biopsy whenever melanoma is a real consideration — a shave biopsy risks transecting the base of the lesion and compromising accurate Breslow depth measurement, which directly drives staging and treatment decisions downstream.

Management

Wide local excision with margins determined by Breslow depth, ± sentinel lymph node biopsy per NCCN thickness/ulceration thresholds — see the Cancer Classification &amp; Staging and Cancer Deep Dives Vol. II atlases for the full staging and systemic treatment framework for more advanced disease.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Excisional biopsy: results typically in 1-2 weeks. Definitive wide excision (once depth known): scheduled promptly, generally within a few weeks of diagnosis given known biology. Surveillance thereafter follows NCCN stage-based intervals (see the Cancer Classification &amp; Staging Atlas) — typically every 3-12 months depending on stage, for a minimum of several years to a lifetime depending on risk.

Red Flags

Any pigmented lesion under a nail, on a palm/sole, or a rapidly changing/growing lesion regardless of how "typical" or "atypical" it looks by ABCDE criteria warrants a low biopsy threshold — melanoma is the diagnosis you cannot afford to miss in outpatient dermatology.

Documentation Language

"[Asymmetric/irregular border/color-variegated] pigmented lesion, [X]mm diameter, [evolution history if known]. Excisional biopsy performed with [1-3mm] margins. Breslow depth [pending/X mm], ulceration [present/absent]. Dermoscopy: [pigment network pattern, vascular pattern if documented]."

Clinical Pearl

Dermoscopy substantially improves diagnostic accuracy for pigmented lesions beyond naked-eye exam alone (asymmetric pigment network, atypical vascular patterns, blue-white veil, and irregular streaks/pseudopods are concerning dermoscopic features) — building dermoscopic exam into routine full-body skin checks, not just for lesions that already look worrying on gross exam, catches earlier disease.

05

Dermoscopy Fundamentals

Morphology / Appearance

Not a single lesion but a technique: dermoscopy (epiluminescence microscopy) reveals subsurface pigment/vascular structures invisible to the naked eye, substantially improving sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing benign nevi from melanoma and for subtyping non-melanoma skin cancers.

Distribution

Applied to any pigmented or suspicious lesion during a full-body skin exam — building dermoscopic evaluation into routine practice (not reserved only for lesions that already look concerning) catches subtly atypical lesions earlier.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Not applicable in the disease-etiology sense — dermoscopy is a technique, and its diagnostic value comes from revealing subsurface structural correlates of the underlying histology (e.g., an asymmetric pigment network correlates with disorganized melanocyte proliferation) that aren't visible to the naked eye.

Workup

Key patterns to recognize: a regular, symmetric pigment network with even color favors a benign nevus; an ASYMMETRIC pigment network, blue-white veil, irregular streaks/pseudopods at the periphery, and atypical/polymorphous vasculature favor melanoma; arborizing (branching) vessels favor BCC; a "strawberry" pattern with fine white scale favors actinic keratosis/SCC in situ.

Management

Dermoscopy is a diagnostic aid, not a replacement for biopsy when a lesion is genuinely concerning — its main practical value is improving the threshold decision of WHICH lesions to biopsy among many nevi, reducing both missed melanomas and unnecessary biopsies of clearly benign lesions.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Not a treatment — but equivocal lesions followed dermoscopically (rather than biopsied immediately) should have a defined short-interval follow-up (typically 3 months) with repeat photography/dermoscopy to assess for change, not an open-ended "watch it" plan without a specific return date.

Red Flags

Don't let a reassuring dermoscopic pattern override a strong clinical concern (rapid change reported by the patient, a lesion that "doesn't fit" the patient's other nevi — the "ugly duckling" sign) — dermoscopy improves but does not replace clinical judgment and biopsy threshold.

Documentation Language

"Dermoscopic exam of [lesion location]: [pigment network pattern — regular/asymmetric], [vascular pattern], [additional structures: blue-white veil, streaks/pseudopods, blotches]. Overall impression: [benign/equivocal/concerning for melanoma] — biopsy [recommended/deferred with short-interval follow-up]."

Clinical Pearl

The "ugly duckling" sign — a lesion that looks different from the patient's other nevi, even if it doesn't meet strict ABCDE criteria on its own — is one of the most practically useful screening heuristics in a full-body skin exam, especially in patients with many nevi where individually applying ABCDE to every lesion isn't practical.

01

Shave Biopsy

Indication

Superficial or exophytic (raised, protruding) lesions where full-thickness sampling isn't essential — most BCC and SCC evaluations, seborrheic keratosis removal, and superficial nevi where melanoma is NOT a significant concern.

Technique

A blade (or flexible razor blade) is used to shave the lesion off at or just below the skin surface, typically under local anesthesia; hemostasis achieved with aluminum chloride solution or light electrocautery.

Contraindications / Avoid When

AVOID for any lesion where melanoma is a genuine consideration — shave biopsy risks transecting the lesion base and losing accurate Breslow depth measurement, which directly compromises staging (see the Melanoma card above).

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Healing: 1-2 weeks for most sites; results typically return in 3-7 business days depending on lab. Follow-up scheduled to discuss results and any needed further treatment once pathology returns.

Documentation Language

"Shave biopsy performed at [site] under local anesthesia (lidocaine [with/without] epinephrine), hemostasis achieved with [aluminum chloride/electrocautery]. Specimen sent to pathology. Wound care instructions given."

Clinical Pearl

Before performing a shave biopsy on ANY pigmented lesion, explicitly ask yourself "could this be melanoma?" — if there's real uncertainty, default to an excisional or deep punch/saucerization technique instead rather than risking a compromised depth measurement that can't be recovered later.

02

Punch Biopsy

Indication

Inflammatory dermatoses requiring full-thickness dermal sampling (to evaluate architecture and depth of inflammatory infiltrate), and smaller lesions where a cylindrical full-thickness sample is diagnostically useful.

Technique

A circular punch tool (typically 3-6mm) is rotated through the epidermis and dermis (sometimes into subcutaneous fat) to obtain a cylindrical tissue core; the resulting defect is usually closed with one or two sutures for punches ≥4mm.

Contraindications / Avoid When

Less ideal for large or highly heterogeneous lesions where a small punch may not be representative of the whole lesion (sampling error) — consider multiple punches from different areas of a large, variably-appearing lesion, or an incisional/excisional approach instead.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Suture removal: typically 7-10 days depending on body site (face sooner, trunk/extremity later given tension). Results: 3-7 business days for routine histology; DIF processing can take longer given specialized handling requirements.

Documentation Language

"[X]mm punch biopsy performed at [site] under local anesthesia, closed with [X] suture(s) of [type]. Specimen sent for [routine histology and/or direct immunofluorescence]. Suture removal instructions given for [X] days."

Clinical Pearl

For inflammatory dermatoses where the diagnosis is genuinely unclear (the classic "could be several things" papulosquamous eruption), a punch biopsy sent for both routine histology AND direct immunofluorescence (if a blistering/autoimmune process is on the differential) maximizes diagnostic yield from a single procedure.

03

Excisional Biopsy

Indication

Any lesion where melanoma is a real consideration (preferred over shave biopsy specifically to preserve accurate depth measurement), and lesions requiring complete removal for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes (e.g., a lesion that needs to be entirely gone, not just sampled).

Technique

The entire lesion is removed with a narrow margin of surrounding normal skin (typically 1-3mm for a diagnostic excision of a pigmented lesion of concern), extending into subcutaneous fat, followed by layered closure.

Contraindications / Avoid When

Larger lesions in cosmetically sensitive areas may require referral for a more complex closure (flap or graft) rather than routine primary closure — plan the biopsy approach with the eventual definitive treatment/closure in mind, not just the immediate diagnostic need.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Suture removal: typically 7-14 days depending on site/tension. Results: 3-7 business days routine, longer if special staining needed. Definitive re-excision (if melanoma confirmed) planned promptly once depth is known — see the Melanoma card.

Documentation Language

"Excisional biopsy performed at [site] with [1-3mm] margins under local anesthesia, layered closure with [suture type/technique]. Specimen sent for histology including Breslow depth/margin assessment if melanoma is in the differential."

Clinical Pearl

When biopsying a lesion where melanoma is suspected, resist the temptation to take an overly wide margin at the DIAGNOSTIC biopsy stage — a narrow diagnostic margin (1-3mm) is preferred, since a wider therapeutic excision (determined by the eventual Breslow depth) is planned as a separate, second procedure once the diagnosis and depth are confirmed.

04

Cryotherapy (Liquid Nitrogen)

Indication

Actinic keratoses, warts (verrucae), seborrheic keratoses, and some superficial BCCs in low-risk locations — a fast, well-tolerated, no-suture office procedure for appropriately selected superficial lesions.

Technique

Liquid nitrogen is applied (spray or cotton-tip applicator) to freeze the lesion and a small margin of surrounding tissue, typically for one or two freeze-thaw cycles depending on the lesion type and desired depth of destruction.

Contraindications / Avoid When

Avoid for pigmented lesions where melanoma is a consideration (destroys the tissue, preventing any histologic diagnosis) and for lesions in patients with cold-sensitivity conditions (cryoglobulinemia, cold urticaria, Raynaud's) or over superficial nerves (risk of nerve injury with overly aggressive freezing).

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Healing: 1-3 weeks depending on lesion type/depth of freeze. Reassess at that interval; many lesions (warts especially) require repeat treatment every 2-4 weeks over multiple sessions to achieve full clearance.

Documentation Language

"Cryotherapy applied to [lesion/site], [1-2] freeze-thaw cycle(s), [duration of freeze if titrated]. Expected post-treatment blistering/crusting discussed, including counseling on post-inflammatory dyspigmentation risk in darker skin tones."

Clinical Pearl

Counsel patients explicitly about expected post-treatment blistering/crusting and, importantly, the real risk of post-inflammatory hypo- or hyperpigmentation — this is disproportionately more noticeable and more likely to prompt a return visit or complaint in patients with darker skin tones, and setting expectations upfront prevents unnecessary alarm.

05

Curettage &amp; Electrodesiccation (C&amp;E)

Indication

Low-risk, superficial BCC in non-critical anatomic locations, seborrheic keratoses, and some other superficial benign/low-risk lesions where tissue-sparing cosmesis isn't the top priority.

Technique

The lesion is scraped away with a curette (a sharp, spoon-shaped instrument) down to a firm dermal base, followed by electrodesiccation (light electrocautery) of the base to achieve hemostasis and destroy any remaining microscopic tumor extension; often repeated for 2-3 cycles.

Contraindications / Avoid When

AVOID for high-risk BCC subtypes (infiltrative, morpheaform), high-risk anatomic locations (central face, around eyes/nose/ears/lips), or recurrent BCC at a previously treated site — Mohs surgery is preferred in these situations given C&amp;E's less precise margin control compared to Mohs.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Healing: 2-4 weeks with expected scar formation (distinct from excision, where the goal is often a less visible scar) — set this expectation explicitly since C&amp;E scars can look different than patients expect. Follow-up to confirm clearance typically at 4-6 weeks.

Documentation Language

"C&amp;E performed at [site], [X] cycles, hemostasis achieved with electrodesiccation. Specimen [sent for histology/discarded per low-risk lesion protocol]. Wound care instructions given."

Clinical Pearl

C&amp;E leaves NO tissue for histologic margin confirmation the way excision or Mohs does — this is an appropriate trade-off for genuinely low-risk lesions, but it means C&amp;E should only be selected when you're already confident in the diagnosis and risk category from a prior biopsy, not as a first diagnostic step.

06

When to Refer for Mohs Micrographic Surgery

Indication

High-risk BCC/SCC by location (central face, periorbital, perinasal, perioral, periauricular, genitalia, hands/feet), high-risk histologic subtype (infiltrative/morpheaform BCC, poorly differentiated or perineural-invasion-positive SCC), recurrent tumors at a previously treated site, or large tumors where tissue conservation matters significantly for function/cosmesis.

Technique

Not applicable in the same sense — Mohs is performed by a fellowship-trained Mohs surgeon, examining 100% of the surgical margin in real-time during the procedure (versus the small sampling of margins typical with standard excision pathology), allowing immediate re-excision of any positive margin in the same setting.

Contraindications / Avoid When

Not necessary/cost-effective for straightforward, low-risk lesions in non-critical locations where standard excision achieves excellent cure rates with less procedural complexity and cost — reserving Mohs for genuinely high-risk situations (rather than defaulting to it for every skin cancer) is appropriate resource stewardship.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Mohs referral-to-procedure interval varies by practice access, but should not be treated as low-urgency for the high-risk features that trigger referral in the first place — aim for scheduling within a few weeks, sooner for rapidly growing or symptomatic lesions.

Documentation Language

"Referral to Mohs surgery for [BCC/SCC] at [site] given [high-risk feature: location/histologic subtype/recurrence/size]. Biopsy-confirmed diagnosis and pathology report sent with referral."

Clinical Pearl

Knowing WHEN to refer for Mohs (rather than either over-referring low-risk lesions or under-referring high-risk ones) is one of the most practically important judgment calls in a general dermatology practice — the NCCN high-risk feature list above is worth having memorized rather than looked up case-by-case.

01

Androgenetic Alopecia

Morphology / Appearance

Progressive, patterned hair thinning — bitemporal recession and vertex thinning in men (Norwood-Hamilton pattern); diffuse crown thinning with a preserved frontal hairline in women (Ludwig pattern) — this male/female pattern distinction is itself diagnostically useful.

Distribution

Scalp, following the sex-specific patterns above; family history is common though not universal.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Androgen (specifically dihydrotestosterone, DHT)-mediated progressive miniaturization of genetically susceptible hair follicles — DHT binds androgen receptors in the follicle, shortening the anagen (growth) phase and progressively producing thinner, shorter hairs with each cycle until follicles become vellus-like or non-functional; this explains why finasteride (blocking DHT production via 5-alpha-reductase inhibition) is mechanistically targeted rather than simply an empirically-discovered treatment.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis based on pattern and progression; consider checking iron studies, thyroid function, and (in women) androgen levels if the pattern is atypical, rapidly progressive, or accompanied by other signs of hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, irregular menses).

Management

Topical minoxidil (OTC) is first-line for both sexes; oral finasteride (a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor) is an effective option for men, generally avoided in women of reproductive age given teratogenicity risk if pregnant; spironolactone is a common off-label option for women with a hormonal component; low-level laser therapy and platelet-rich plasma injections are additional, more variably evidence-supported options.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Topical minoxidil: minimum 3-4 months before any visible change, with maximal effect typically at 12 months — set this long timeline explicitly, since premature discontinuation before 3-4 months is the most common reason for perceived treatment failure. Oral finasteride: similar 3-6 month minimum timeline to assess benefit.

Red Flags

Rapid, patchy (rather than patterned/diffuse), or inflammatory/scarring hair loss is NOT androgenetic alopecia and needs a different workup (see Alopecia Areata below, or consider scarring alopecia/lupus if there's associated scalp erythema or scarring).

Documentation Language

"[Bitemporal recession and vertex thinning (Norwood [stage]) / diffuse crown thinning with preserved frontal hairline (Ludwig [stage])]. Family history: [present/absent]. Onset/progression timeline: [X]. Iron studies/TSH [checked if atypical presentation]."

Clinical Pearl

Set expectations that minoxidil and finasteride are meant to SLOW further loss and modestly regrow some hair, not restore a full head of hair to a pre-loss baseline — mismatched expectations are one of the most common reasons patients discontinue an otherwise appropriately effective regimen.

02

Alopecia Areata

Morphology / Appearance

Well-circumscribed, round/oval patches of complete hair loss with a smooth, non-scarring scalp surface; "exclamation point" hairs (hairs that taper toward the scalp) at the patch margins are a classic supportive finding.

Distribution

Can affect any hair-bearing area (scalp most commonly, but eyebrows/eyelashes/beard too); alopecia totalis (complete scalp hair loss) and alopecia universalis (complete body hair loss) represent more severe ends of the same disease spectrum.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

An organ-specific autoimmune process in which CD8+ T-cells attack anagen-phase hair follicles, collapsing the follicle's normal immune-privileged status — this is mechanistically why JAK inhibitors (blocking the interferon-gamma signaling that drives this T-cell attack) are effective, representing a direct pathophysiology-to-treatment link developed specifically for this disease.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis; biopsy reserved for atypical presentations where scarring alopecia or another process is a genuine consideration; screen for other autoimmune conditions given alopecia areata's association with autoimmune thyroid disease and vitiligo.

Management

Intralesional corticosteroid injection is first-line for limited patchy disease; topical high-potency corticosteroids or contact immunotherapy (diphencyprone) for more extensive disease; JAK inhibitors (baricitinib and others) represent a significant recent advance for extensive/refractory alopecia areata, including alopecia totalis/universalis, a population with previously very limited effective systemic options.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Intralesional corticosteroid injection: reassess regrowth at 4-6 weeks, may repeat every 4-6 weeks for several cycles. JAK inhibitors: meaningful regrowth typically requires a minimum 3-6 month trial before judging response, given the biology of the hair cycle itself.

Red Flags

No acute emergency association, but the psychological impact of alopecia areata (especially rapid, visible hair loss) is significant and often under-addressed — screen for and address the mental health impact, not just the hair loss itself.

Documentation Language

"Well-circumscribed patch(es) of non-scarring hair loss at [site(s)], [total number/extent — % scalp or totalis/universalis if applicable]. Exclamation-point hairs [present/absent] at margins. Screening for comorbid autoimmune thyroid disease/vitiligo [performed]."

Clinical Pearl

Explicitly counsel patients that alopecia areata is autoimmune, not contagious or caused by anything they did, and that spontaneous regrowth is common in limited disease — this reassurance meaningfully reduces the anxiety and self-blame many patients bring to this diagnosis.

03

Onychomycosis

Morphology / Appearance

Thickened, discolored (yellow-brown), brittle, and often crumbling nail plate, typically starting distally and progressing proximally; subungual debris accumulation is common.

Distribution

Toenails far more commonly than fingernails, reflecting the warm, moist, occlusive shoe environment that favors dermatophyte growth.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Dermatophyte fungi (most commonly Trichophyton rubrum) invade the nail plate via the distal subungual space, digesting keratin and causing the nail to thicken, discolor, and separate from the nail bed — the warm, occlusive, often minimally-ventilated shoe environment and reduced peripheral circulation/immune surveillance with age both favor fungal establishment and persistence in toenails specifically.

Workup

Confirm the diagnosis before treating systemically — KOH prep, fungal culture, or PAS staining on a nail clipping — since several other conditions (psoriatic nail disease, lichen planus, trauma) can closely mimic onychomycosis clinically, and oral antifungal treatment carries real hepatotoxicity monitoring requirements not worth incurring for a misdiagnosis.

Management

Oral terbinafine is first-line for confirmed onychomycosis given superior cure rates compared to topical therapy, but requires baseline and monitoring liver function given hepatotoxicity risk; topical ciclopirox or efinaconazole are lower-efficacy but lower-risk options, reasonable for milder disease or when oral therapy is contraindicated/declined.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Oral terbinafine: typically 6 weeks for fingernails, 12 weeks for toenails — but visible clinical improvement lags treatment completion significantly since it takes the nail's full growth cycle (up to 12-18 months for a toenail) to grow out clear; counsel this explicitly so patients don't expect a clear nail immediately after finishing the medication course.

Red Flags

No acute emergency association, but recognize that recurrence after successful treatment is common (30-50% in various studies), especially without addressing environmental reservoirs (shoes, shower surfaces) — a purely medication-focused approach without this counseling often leads to relapse.

Documentation Language

"Thickened, [yellow-brown discolored], friable nail(s) at [digit(s)], distal subungual pattern. KOH prep/culture/PAS: [result]. Baseline LFTs [obtained] prior to oral terbinafine given hepatotoxicity monitoring requirement."

Clinical Pearl

Always confirm onychomycosis with a KOH prep/culture/PAS stain BEFORE starting oral terbinafine — treating presumptively based on appearance alone is a common source of unnecessary systemic medication exposure (with its monitoring burden) for what may actually be psoriatic nail disease or another mimic entirely.

04

Key Nail Signs as Systemic Disease Clues

Morphology / Appearance

Clubbing (bulbous fingertip enlargement with loss of the normal nail angle) — pulmonary/cardiac disease. Pitting — psoriasis (most common) or alopecia areata. Beau's lines (transverse grooves) — a marker of a prior systemic illness/physiologic stress at a specific point in time (can be dated by measuring distance from the cuticle). Splinter hemorrhages — endocarditis (classically) though also seen with trauma and several other conditions, so context matters. Half-and-half nails (proximal white, distal pink/brown) — chronic kidney disease.

Distribution

Nail findings should be examined bilaterally and across multiple digits — a single-digit finding is more likely trauma-related, while a symmetric, multi-digit finding is more likely systemic.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Not a single etiology — each sign reflects a distinct systemic process (clubbing: chronic hypoxia/VEGF-mediated soft tissue changes at the nail bed; splinter hemorrhages: microvascular emboli/damage; Beau's lines: temporary arrest of nail matrix growth during a systemic illness/stressor). The common thread is that the nail matrix is metabolically sensitive to systemic perturbation, making it a uniquely dateable/trackable tissue.

Workup

Correlate any concerning nail finding with the broader clinical picture and history rather than interpreting it in isolation — nail clubbing found incidentally, for example, warrants a pulmonary/cardiac workup discussion, not just a dermatology note.

Management

Management is entirely directed at the underlying systemic condition once identified — the nail finding itself is a diagnostic clue, not something to treat directly.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Not applicable to the sign itself — management timeline follows whatever underlying systemic condition is identified.

Red Flags

New clubbing or new splinter hemorrhages in a patient with fever or a murmur should prompt urgent consideration of endocarditis rather than a routine dermatology referral.

Documentation Language

"[Nail finding] noted on [digit(s)/bilateral]. Correlated with [relevant systemic history/findings]. Distance from cuticle measured at [X]mm for Beau's lines, allowing estimation of the causative event at approximately [X weeks/months] prior given average nail growth rate (~0.1mm/day fingernails)."

Clinical Pearl

Nail exam is a genuinely high-yield, frequently-skipped part of the skin exam — a few extra seconds looking at all ten nails during any full-body skin check can surface a systemic disease clue that would otherwise be missed entirely.

05

Oral &amp; Genital Mucosal Lesions (Lichen Planus, Aphthous Ulcers, Lichen Sclerosus)

Morphology / Appearance

Oral lichen planus: white, lacy (reticular) patches on buccal mucosa, sometimes with painful erosive areas. Aphthous ulcers: shallow, painful, round-to-oval ulcers with a yellow-white base and red halo, recurring in the same general oral locations. Lichen sclerosus (genital): white, atrophic, "cigarette-paper" thin skin, classically in a figure-eight distribution around the vulva/perianal area in women, or affecting the glans/foreskin in men.

Distribution

Oral mucosa (buccal, gingival, tongue) for the first two; anogenital skin specifically for lichen sclerosus — though extragenital lichen sclerosus can rarely occur elsewhere.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Oral lichen planus shares the same T-cell-mediated basal keratinocyte attack as cutaneous lichen planus. Aphthous ulcers reflect a localized T-cell-mediated mucosal immune response, often triggered by local trauma, stress, or nutritional deficiency (B12, folate, iron) in susceptible individuals. Lichen sclerosus's exact trigger is unclear but involves autoimmune-mediated dermal sclerosis/collagen changes, with a recognized (though incompletely understood) association with autoimmune conditions generally.

Workup

Biopsy for any persistent, atypical, indurated, or non-healing oral or genital lesion to exclude malignancy — oral and genital squamous cell carcinoma can arise within chronic lichen planus or lichen sclerosus, and a lesion that isn't following the expected benign course needs tissue diagnosis.

Management

High-potency topical corticosteroids remain the mainstay for both erosive oral lichen planus and lichen sclerosus (ultra-potent topical steroids specifically for lichen sclerosus, which responds well and requires long-term maintenance therapy to prevent progressive scarring); topical anesthetics/protective pastes and addressing any local trauma triggers for recurrent aphthous ulcers; recurrent, severe, or atypical aphthous ulcers should prompt consideration of an underlying systemic condition (Behçet's disease, inflammatory bowel disease, HIV, nutritional deficiency).

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

High-potency topical corticosteroids for lichen sclerosus: indefinite maintenance therapy (often 2-3x/week even once controlled) given the disease's progressive-scarring-if-untreated nature — this is explicitly NOT a short-course treatment. Aphthous ulcers: individual lesions resolve in 1-2 weeks regardless of treatment; recurrent disease warrants the systemic workup noted above if frequency/severity is significant.

Red Flags

Any oral or genital lesion showing induration, non-healing ulceration, or failure to respond to appropriate treatment over a reasonable timeframe needs biopsy to exclude malignant transformation — this is a genuinely underappreciated site for missed squamous cell carcinoma given patient/provider reluctance to examine and biopsy these areas thoroughly.

Documentation Language

"[Oral lichen planus: lacy white reticular pattern on buccal mucosa, erosive component present/absent] or [Aphthous ulcer: shallow round ulcer with yellow base/red halo, [X]mm, recurrence frequency] or [Lichen sclerosus: white atrophic 'cigarette-paper' plaques in figure-eight distribution, [anatomic extent]]. Biopsy [performed/deferred] given [induration/non-healing/atypical features]."

Clinical Pearl

Untreated lichen sclerosus causes PROGRESSIVE, irreversible anatomic scarring (labial fusion, clitoral hood adhesions, phimosis) if not adequately treated long-term — this is not a cosmetic-only concern, and patients need to understand that ongoing maintenance therapy (even when asymptomatic) is about preventing permanent anatomic change, not just symptom control.

01

Atopic Dermatitis (Pediatric-Specific Considerations)

Morphology / Appearance

Same core morphology as adult atopic dermatitis (erythematous, scaly, pruritic patches) but with an AGE-DEPENDENT distribution shift — infants show facial and extensor involvement (the flexural pattern typical of older children/adults hasn't developed yet), transitioning to the classic flexural pattern as the child gets older and starts crawling/walking.

Distribution

Infants: cheeks, scalp, extensor surfaces of extremities (areas not subject to friction from crawling). Older children/adults: classic flexural (antecubital/popliteal fossae).

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Same core filaggrin-barrier-defect/Th2-skewed immune pathophysiology as adult atopic dermatitis (see the Inflammatory Dermatoses tab), but the DISTRIBUTION shift with age reflects a mechanical, not immunologic, explanation — infants aren't yet crawling/experiencing the friction exposure that produces the classic flexural pattern, so the disease manifests wherever skin barrier is thinnest and most exposed (face, extensor surfaces) at that developmental stage.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis; consider food allergy evaluation only in infants/young children with moderate-severe eczema and a clear history suggesting a specific food trigger (immediate reaction pattern) — routine, broad food-allergy testing without a clear clinical trigger is generally discouraged given poor predictive value and the risk of unnecessarily restrictive diets.

Management

Same core treatment approach as adult atopic dermatitis (emollients as foundation, topical corticosteroids stepped by potency and body site, topical calcineurin inhibitors as steroid-sparing options) — pediatric-specific counseling should emphasize that appropriately-dosed topical steroids used correctly are safe and that "steroid phobia" (a very common parental concern) often leads to undertreatment and worse outcomes than appropriate steroid use.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Same general timeline as adult atopic dermatitis (1-2 week flare treatment, continuous emollient maintenance) — most childhood atopic dermatitis improves or resolves by adolescence, a natural-history point worth sharing explicitly at diagnosis to contextualize the expected multi-year but generally improving course.

Red Flags

Same eczema herpeticum red flag as adults applies to children — widespread, punched-out vesicles/erosions on an eczema background, especially with fever, needs same-day evaluation and antiviral treatment.

Documentation Language

"[Age]-month/year-old with [facial/extensor or flexural] eczematous patches, [X]% BSA, sleep disruption [present/absent]. Food allergy history: [clear immediate-reaction trigger identified/not identified] — [testing pursued/deferred] accordingly. Steroid-phobia counseling provided regarding appropriate topical steroid use."

Clinical Pearl

Most childhood atopic dermatitis improves or resolves by adolescence — sharing this natural history explicitly with parents (alongside effective symptom management in the meantime) helps contextualize the current treatment burden as a temporary, manageable phase rather than an assumed lifelong condition.

02

Diaper Dermatitis

Morphology / Appearance

Irritant contact dermatitis pattern: erythematous, sometimes erosive patches on convex surfaces in direct contact with the diaper (buttocks, thighs, lower abdomen), classically SPARING the skin folds (since urine/stool contact is reduced in the protected folds) — this sparing pattern helps distinguish it from candidal diaper dermatitis.

Distribution

Diaper-covered area; a distribution that specifically INVOLVES the skin folds with satellite papules/pustules beyond the main rash border suggests superimposed candidiasis rather than simple irritant dermatitis.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Irritant contact dermatitis from prolonged occlusive contact with urine/stool — urease-producing bacteria in stool raise local skin pH, activating fecal enzymes (proteases, lipases) that directly damage the stratum corneum; superimposed Candida albicans exploits this already-compromised barrier, explaining why fold involvement/satellite lesions signal a secondary process rather than simple irritation.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis based on the distribution pattern described above; consider a KOH prep if candidal superinfection is suspected but the diagnosis is unclear.

Management

Frequent diaper changes, gentle cleansing, and barrier ointments (zinc oxide, petrolatum) are foundational for simple irritant diaper dermatitis; add a topical antifungal (nystatin, clotrimazole) if candidal superinfection is suspected (fold involvement, satellite lesions, or failure to improve with barrier measures alone); low-potency topical corticosteroids can be added briefly for significant inflammation, but higher-potency steroids should be avoided given occlusion under the diaper markedly increases systemic absorption risk in this age group.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Barrier measures + frequent changes: improvement expected within days; if not improved in 3-5 days, add topical antifungal for presumed candidal superinfection and reassess again in another week.

Red Flags

Diaper dermatitis that is severe, persistent despite appropriate treatment, or accompanied by other skin findings elsewhere should prompt consideration of an underlying condition (severe seborrheic dermatitis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis in refractory/atypical cases, zinc deficiency in severe erosive cases) rather than simply escalating topical treatment indefinitely.

Documentation Language

"Erythematous [erosive if severe] patches over diaper-contact convex surfaces, [skin folds spared/involved with satellite lesions — suggesting candidal superinfection]. Diaper-change frequency and barrier product use reviewed. KOH prep [performed if diagnosis unclear]."

Clinical Pearl

AVOID higher-potency topical corticosteroids in the diaper area specifically because occlusion under the diaper significantly increases systemic absorption in infants — this is a common, avoidable prescribing pitfall distinct from steroid potency selection elsewhere on an infant's body.

03

Molluscum Contagiosum

Morphology / Appearance

Small (2-5mm), dome-shaped, flesh-colored to pearly papules with a characteristic central umbilication (a small central dimple) — this central umbilication is the key distinguishing feature from other small papular eruptions.

Distribution

Can occur anywhere but commonly clusters in areas of skin-to-skin contact or shaving/friction (trunk, extremities, and in older children/adults, the genital area via sexual transmission — genital molluscum in an adult should prompt broader STI screening consideration).

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

A poxvirus (molluscum contagiosum virus) infects epidermal keratinocytes directly, causing localized proliferation and the characteristic central umbilication where the virus-filled core eventually expresses/crusts — spread occurs via direct skin contact or fomites, and autoinoculation (spreading lesions on one's own body via scratching/shaving) is common, explaining clustered distribution patterns.

Workup

Clinical diagnosis based on the classic umbilicated papule appearance; biopsy is rarely needed given how characteristic the morphology is.

Management

Molluscum is generally self-limited in immunocompetent children, resolving over months to a couple of years without treatment — WATCHFUL WAITING (no treatment) is a completely reasonable, evidence-supported default, especially for asymptomatic lesions in young children. When treatment is desired (parental preference, lesions in a cosmetically sensitive area, or an immunocompromised patient with more extensive/persistent disease), options include cantharidin application, cryotherapy, or curettage.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Untreated (watchful waiting): typical natural resolution over 6-18 months, occasionally longer, in immunocompetent patients — set this explicit, sometimes lengthy timeline so parents don't expect faster spontaneous resolution than is realistic. If treated: cantharidin/cryotherapy often requires repeat sessions every 2-3 weeks until cleared.

Red Flags

Widespread, unusually large, or persistent molluscum in an adult or child should prompt consideration of underlying immunosuppression (including HIV in the appropriate context) given how disproportionately extensive molluscum can become in immunocompromised patients.

Documentation Language

"[Number] dome-shaped, umbilicated, flesh-colored papules at [site(s)], [duration]. [Immunocompetent, no risk factors for extensive disease] or [immunosuppression evaluation pursued given atypical extent/persistence]. Watchful waiting discussed and elected, OR [treatment modality] performed today."

Clinical Pearl

Many parents present specifically requesting treatment out of concern the lesions will spread or scar — explicitly counseling that watchful waiting is a legitimate, guideline-supported option (not "doing nothing" or neglecting the problem) helps parents feel comfortable choosing observation over a procedure their child may find distressing.

04

Verruca (Warts)

Morphology / Appearance

Verruca vulgaris (common wart): rough, hyperkeratotic papules, often on hands/fingers. Verruca plantaris (plantar wart): similar but on the sole, often flattened by weight-bearing pressure and sometimes mistaken for a callus — paring the surface reveals characteristic pinpoint bleeding (thrombosed capillaries), a helpful distinguishing feature from a simple callus.

Distribution

Hands and feet most commonly, reflecting HPV inoculation through minor skin breaks in these frequently-exposed, frequently-traumatized areas.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

Human papillomavirus (HPV, numerous cutaneous subtypes) infects keratinocytes through minor breaks in the epidermal barrier, driving hyperproliferation of infected cells — the virus stays largely confined to the epidermis, and the classic pinpoint bleeding on paring reflects thrombosed dermal papillary capillaries extending upward into the hyperplastic epidermis, a finding absent in a simple callus (pure epidermal thickening without this vascular component).

Workup

Clinical diagnosis based on morphology, including the pinpoint-bleeding-on-paring finding described above; biopsy is rarely needed except for atypical, treatment-refractory, or diagnostically uncertain lesions.

Management

Many warts resolve spontaneously over time, especially in children, making watchful waiting a reasonable option for asymptomatic lesions, similar in principle to molluscum management. When treatment is desired: over-the-counter or in-office salicylic acid application, cryotherapy, or cantharidin for pediatric patients (often better tolerated than cryotherapy's discomfort in young children); more refractory lesions may need combination therapy or referral for more advanced options (intralesional immunotherapy, laser).

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Untreated: many resolve spontaneously within 1-2 years, especially in children (immune-mediated clearance) — again, set this explicit timeline. Treated (cryotherapy/cantharidin): typically repeated every 2-4 weeks over several sessions; if no improvement after 3-4 sessions, reconsider diagnosis or escalate to a different modality.

Red Flags

A rapidly growing, painful, bleeding, or atypical-appearing "wart"— particularly in an older adult or an unusual location — should prompt biopsy to exclude a squamous cell carcinoma or amelanotic melanoma mimicking a wart, since these can be visually confused, especially on the sole.

Documentation Language

"[Hyperkeratotic papule(s)] at [site], pinpoint bleeding on paring [present, supporting wart over callus]. Duration: [X]. Prior treatment attempts: [OTC salicylic acid, cryotherapy, etc. and response]. Watchful waiting vs. active treatment discussed."

Clinical Pearl

Cryotherapy is often more painful for young children than cantharidin application, which is generally painless at the time of application (though it does cause a blister afterward) — matching the treatment modality to what a specific child will actually tolerate meaningfully improves follow-through with treatment.

05

Infantile Hemangioma

Morphology / Appearance

Bright red ("strawberry"), raised, well-demarcated vascular lesion — classically ABSENT or barely visible at birth, appearing in the first few weeks of life and then undergoing a proliferative growth phase (rapid enlargement over the first several months) before spontaneously involuting/regressing over subsequent years — this characteristic growth-then-involution timeline is itself diagnostic and distinguishes it from other vascular lesions (like a port-wine stain, which is present at birth and does not proliferate or involute).

Distribution

Can occur anywhere, but location matters significantly for risk assessment — periorbital hemangiomas can threaten vision (amblyopia from occlusion or astigmatism from globe compression), and hemangiomas over the lower face/neck/chin in a "beard" distribution raise concern for an associated airway hemangioma.

Etiology / Pathophysiology (Deeper Dive)

A benign vascular endothelial cell proliferation, with GLUT1 positivity on histology serving as a specific marker distinguishing true infantile hemangiomas from other vascular anomalies/malformations — the proliferative phase reflects active, unregulated endothelial growth, while involution reflects a programmed apoptotic process as the lesion matures, analogous in concept (though not mechanism) to normal fetal vascular remodeling.

Workup

Usually a clinical diagnosis based on the characteristic appearance and growth timeline; imaging (ultrasound or MRI) is reserved for deep/segmental hemangiomas, those in high-risk locations (periorbital, airway-adjacent), or when the diagnosis is genuinely unclear from the surface appearance alone; multiple (5+) cutaneous hemangiomas should prompt an abdominal ultrasound to screen for hepatic hemangiomas.

Management

Most small, uncomplicated infantile hemangiomas require NO treatment and are observed through their natural growth-and-involution course. Oral propranolol (a beta-blocker) is first-line for hemangiomas requiring treatment (functional risk — periorbital, airway; ulceration; or significant cosmetic concern in a visible location), and has transformed management of this condition — started early in the proliferative phase for maximal effect, with cardiovascular monitoring given propranolol's systemic beta-blocking effects in an infant.

Expected Duration / Reassessment Timeline

Proliferative phase: most rapid growth in the first 3-6 months of life — this is the highest-value window to initiate propranolol if treatment is indicated, since earlier treatment during active proliferation is more effective than treatment started after the growth phase has largely completed. Involution: gradual over several years (often most of the visible improvement by age 4, with subtler continued improvement to age 10).

Red Flags

A periorbital hemangioma, a "beard-distribution" hemangioma (airway involvement risk), or any hemangioma showing ulceration or rapid growth threatening a vital structure needs prompt referral rather than routine watchful-waiting follow-up.

Documentation Language

"[Absent at birth / noted at X weeks of age], now [size/color], in [proliferative/plateau/involuting] phase based on growth trajectory. Location: [assess specifically for periorbital, 'beard distribution', or other high-risk site]. [Multiple lesions present — abdominal ultrasound to screen for hepatic hemangiomas performed/planned]."

Clinical Pearl

The distinction between a hemangioma (absent at birth, proliferates then involutes) and a vascular malformation like a port-wine stain (present at birth, grows proportionately with the child, does NOT spontaneously involute) is a fundamental, easily-taught distinction that changes the entire counseling conversation with parents — a port-wine stain will not simply go away and warrants different long-term planning (including screening for associated syndromes like Sturge-Weber if periocular/forehead).

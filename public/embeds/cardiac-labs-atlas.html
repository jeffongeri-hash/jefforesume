Cardiac Enzymes, Labs & Diagnostics Atlas

Master-level reference: troponin I/T, CK-MB, myoglobin, BNP/NT-proBNP (origin, kinetics, false +/− causes) · troponin T vs. I comparability & ESC 0/1h-0/2h-0/3h repeat protocols · HEART, PERC & pregnancy-adapted D-dimer · Holter, pacemaker interrogation, echocardiogram, and stress test interpretation.

Every cardiac biomarker is a story about where it comes from, how fast it leaks out of dying/stressed tissue, and how fast the body clears it. Get those three facts right and the false-positive/false-negative traps stop being traps.

The Core Principle — Tissue Origin Drives Everything

Cardiac troponins (I and T) are structural proteins of the cardiac contractile apparatus, part of the troponin-tropomyosin complex regulating actin-myosin interaction. The cardiac ISOFORMS are genetically distinct from skeletal muscle troponin, which is what gives them their cardiac specificity — but specificity for CARDIOMYOCYTE INJURY is not the same as specificity for ACUTE CORONARY OCCLUSION. Any process that injures cardiomyocytes (myocarditis, sepsis, PE with RV strain, tachyarrhythmia, renal failure with reduced clearance) elevates troponin without a culprit coronary lesion.

CK-MB is the myocardial-predominant isoenzyme of creatine kinase, but it is NOT purely cardiac — skeletal muscle contains a smaller but real fraction of CK-MB, which is exactly why trauma, heavy exertion, and myopathy cause false-positive elevations.

Myoglobin is a small, non-cardiac-specific oxygen-carrying protein present in both cardiac AND skeletal muscle. Its low molecular weight is why it leaks out fastest of all the markers — and also why it is the least specific.

BNP/NT-proBNP are released from ventricular myocytes in response to WALL STRESS (stretch), not necrosis — a fundamentally different biology from troponin/CK-MB. This is why BNP rises in heart failure, but also in anything else that stresses the ventricular wall (PE with RV strain, pulmonary hypertension, renal failure with volume overload) even without primary cardiac disease.

Cardiac Troponin I (cTnI)

Tissue OriginCardiac-specific isoform of troponin I, part of the troponin-tropomyosin complex regulating calcium-mediated actin-myosin interaction; NOT expressed in skeletal muscle in a cross-reactive form, giving it high cardiac specificity.
OnsetConventional assay 4–10 h · hs-cTnI often detectable 1–3 h
Peak12–24 h (varies by assay; some hs-cTnI assays peak ~9–12 h post-PCI)
DurationElevated 4–10 days (up to ~10–14 days after large MI)
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Renal failure/CKD — elevates cTnI less dramatically than cTnT, but still occurs (reduced clearance, chronic subclinical myocardial injury from LVH/uremic cardiomyopathy).
  • Sepsis/critical illness — microvascular injury and demand ischemia elevate troponin without plaque rupture.
  • Pulmonary embolism with RV strain — right ventricular myocyte stretch/injury.
  • Myocarditis, tachyarrhythmia (especially sustained SVT/VT), strenuous endurance exercise (marathon runners), stroke/subarachnoid hemorrhage (catecholamine-mediated myocardial injury), and heterophile/antibody interference (rare assay-specific false positives).
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • True 'false decrease' is uncommon for troponin (unlike BNP) since it is a necrosis marker without a strong physiologic suppressor — but biotin supplementation can interfere with some immunoassay platforms and falsely LOWER (or occasionally falsely raise) troponin results depending on the assay format; ask about high-dose biotin use if a result doesn't fit the clinical picture.
  • Drawing too early (before the biomarker has had time to rise from the index event) is the most common reason a troponin is inappropriately low/normal — this is a timing problem, not a true analytical false negative.
Pearl Troponin is a marker of MYOCARDIAL INJURY, not a marker of ACUTE CORONARY OCCLUSION specifically. An elevated troponin always means something is injuring cardiomyocytes; the job of the clinician is to determine whether that something is a type 1 MI (plaque rupture) or one of the many type 2/non-ischemic causes.

Cardiac Troponin T (cTnT)

Tissue OriginCardiac-specific isoform of troponin T, the tropomyosin-binding subunit of the troponin complex; like cTnI, the cardiac isoform is genetically distinct from skeletal troponin T (though this distinction is less absolute than for troponin I — skeletal muscle can re-express a small amount of the cardiac TnT gene in chronic myopathic/regenerating muscle, a nuance behind some of its false-positive profile).
OnsetConventional assay 4–10 h · hs-cTnT often detectable 1–3 h
Peak12–48 h
DurationElevated 4–10+ days; a SECOND PEAK (~4–5 days later) is well described for hs-cTnT specifically and is NOT associated with worse prognosis — a distinct kinetic quirk hs-cTnI does not reliably share.
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Chronic kidney disease/dialysis — THE single biggest source of chronic troponin elevation, and it affects cTnT MORE than cTnI. In CKD stage 3a+, median hs-cTnT is often frankly 'pathological' (e.g., ~27 ng/L in one series) while hs-cTnI in the same patients frequently remains normal (~11 ng/L) — this asymmetry is a key reason some clinicians prefer cTnI in dialysis patients.
  • Skeletal myopathies/muscular dystrophy — regenerating skeletal muscle can re-express the cardiac TnT gene, causing chronic low-level elevation unrelated to cardiac injury (a false-positive mechanism more specific to TnT than TnI).
  • Same general list as cTnI otherwise: sepsis, PE/RV strain, myocarditis, tachyarrhythmia, strenuous exercise, stroke.
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • Same early-draw timing caveat as cTnI.
  • Biotin interference is a recognized issue for the Roche Elecsys hs-cTnT assay specifically (a streptavidin-biotin based immunoassay) — high-dose biotin supplementation can suppress the measured result.
Pearl If a renal patient's troponin story doesn't add up, remember the assay-specific asymmetry: cTnT runs chronically higher in CKD/dialysis than cTnI does. A 'markedly elevated' cTnT in a stable dialysis patient may simply be their biochemical baseline — the DELTA (change from their own prior baseline) matters far more than the absolute number in this population.

CK-MB (Creatine Kinase-MB)

Tissue OriginThe myocardial-predominant isoenzyme of creatine kinase (an energy-transfer enzyme). Cardiac muscle is roughly 20-40% CK-MB (vs. mostly CK-MM in skeletal muscle), but skeletal muscle still contains a real, non-trivial CK-MB fraction — this incomplete specificity is the whole story behind its false-positive profile.
Onset4–6 h (up to 4–9 h per some sources)
Peak~24 h
DurationReturns to normal by 48–72 h
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Skeletal muscle trauma, rhabdomyolysis, or heavy/unaccustomed exertion — the classic false-positive scenario, since damaged skeletal muscle releases its own CK-MB fraction.
  • Chronic myopathy/muscular dystrophy — chronically elevated CK-MB from ongoing skeletal muscle turnover.
  • Renal failure — reduced clearance.
  • Recent surgery, intramuscular injections, seizures, and prolonged immobilization with muscle breakdown.
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • No well-established physiologic 'false decrease' mechanism — the main pitfall is TIMING (drawn too early, before the 4–6h rise, or too late, after it has returned to baseline by 48–72h) leading to a falsely reassuring normal value outside its diagnostic window.
Pearl CK-MB has been almost entirely superseded by troponin for the diagnosis of acute MI, but it retains a specific niche: because it returns to normal faster (48–72h) than troponin (which can stay up for over a week), CK-MB is still useful for detecting a REINFARCTION in a patient whose troponin is already elevated and slowly trending down from a recent MI — a new CK-MB rise is more interpretable than trying to read a second troponin bump against an already-elevated baseline.

Myoglobin

Tissue OriginA small, low-molecular-weight oxygen-storage/transport protein present in BOTH cardiac and skeletal muscle — it has essentially no cardiac specificity, which is its fundamental limitation.
Onset2–4 h — the EARLIEST-rising of all the classic markers, a direct consequence of its small size
Peak6–12 h
DurationReturns to normal within 24–36 h
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Any skeletal muscle injury — trauma, rhabdomyolysis, intramuscular injections, prolonged seizure activity, heavy exertion.
  • Renal failure — reduced clearance is a major confounder given myoglobin's small size and renal excretion.
  • This poor specificity is exactly why myoglobin's ROLE HAS COLLAPSED since troponin became the diagnostic standard — it is rarely used for diagnosis today, though its early rise still has some niche use as a rapid rule-out adjunct in centers without access to high-sensitivity troponin.
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • No specific physiologic false-decrease mechanism; the main pitfall is again the NARROW WINDOW — a normal myoglobin drawn beyond ~24–36h from symptom onset tells you nothing, since it has already returned to baseline regardless of infarct size.
Pearl Myoglobin's value was always about SPEED, not specificity: a rise of 25–40% over 1–2 hours is suggestive of acute MI, and its negative predictive value climbs quickly in the first few hours (reaching ~89% NPV by 4 hours in older studies) — but a positive myoglobin means almost nothing on its own given its poor specificity, and it has essentially no role in modern hs-troponin-based algorithms.

BNP (B-type Natriuretic Peptide)

Tissue OriginSynthesized as a prohormone (proBNP) predominantly in VENTRICULAR myocytes in response to wall stress/stretch (pressure or volume overload), then cleaved into active BNP and inactive NT-proBNP. Released in response to STRETCH, not necrosis — a fundamentally different trigger from troponin.
OnsetRises within hours of significant wall-stress onset
PeakReflects real-time hemodynamic stress rather than a fixed post-injury curve
DurationHalf-life ~20 minutes (BNP is actively degraded by neprilysin and cleared via natriuretic peptide receptors) — much shorter than NT-proBNP's half-life (~60–90 minutes, cleared mostly renally, passively)
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Renal dysfunction (independent of cardiac function), advancing age, female sex (~20–30% higher at comparable cardiac function, estrogen-related), atrial fibrillation (atrial stretch/rate independently raise levels), systemic inflammation/sepsis, hyperthyroidism.
  • SACUBITRIL/VALSARTAN (Entresto) — neprilysin inhibition blocks BNP degradation, artificially RAISING measured BNP independent of true cardiac status; this is a critical, frequently-tested pitfall.
  • Macro-proBNPemia (a rare artifact of high-molecular-weight proBNP complexes reacting aberrantly with some assays).
  • Acute PE with RV strain, and pulmonary hypertension generally — right heart wall stress raises BNP even without primary LV disease.
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • OBESITY is the single most important cause of a falsely LOW BNP — by a large margin, BNP is disproportionately suppressed compared to NT-proBNP in obese patients (natriuretic peptide clearance receptors are more abundant in adipose tissue). A 'normal' BNP in a dyspneic patient with BMI >35 should NOT be trusted to exclude heart failure — consider halving the usual cutoff or switching to NT-proBNP.
  • The period IMMEDIATELY after acute coronary syndrome onset (before wall stress has had time to trigger a rise) and pericardial effusion/constrictive physiology (which can paradoxically blunt the stretch signal) can also transiently underestimate true cardiac status.
Pearl BNP <100 pg/mL in an acutely dyspneic patient carries a NPV >98% for heart failure — EXCEPT in obesity, where this cutoff loses reliability and should be adjusted downward or abandoned in favor of NT-proBNP. Never use BNP to monitor response in a patient on sacubitril/valsartan — switch to NT-proBNP, which is unaffected by neprilysin inhibition.

NT-proBNP

Tissue OriginThe biologically INACTIVE N-terminal fragment cleaved from proBNP alongside active BNP, released in equimolar amounts — same trigger (ventricular wall stress) as BNP, different clearance pathway.
OnsetParallels BNP release
PeakReflects ongoing hemodynamic stress
DurationHalf-life ~60–90 minutes — LONGER than BNP's ~20 minutes, since NT-proBNP is cleared predominantly by passive renal filtration rather than active enzymatic degradation, making it accumulate more with renal impairment and persist longer in circulation
Causes of FALSE Elevation
  • Renal impairment has a SUBSTANTIALLY LARGER effect on NT-proBNP than on BNP (even modest CKD with eGFR 30–60 significantly elevates it) — the reason BNP is sometimes preferred specifically in renal patients.
  • Advancing age (age-specific thresholds are used clinically, e.g., >1800 pg/mL in older age brackets), female sex, atrial fibrillation, inflammation, hyperthyroidism.
  • NOT affected by sacubitril/valsartan (unlike BNP) — this is exactly why NT-proBNP is the preferred marker for serial monitoring in patients on ARNI therapy.
Causes of FALSE Decrease
  • Obesity also lowers NT-proBNP, but to a MUCH SMALLER degree than it lowers BNP — this relative resistance to obesity-related suppression is another reason NT-proBNP is often favored over BNP in obese patients with suspected heart failure.
Pearl The single highest-yield clinical pearl across both natriuretic peptides: use NT-proBNP (not BNP) to monitor a patient on sacubitril/valsartan, and use NT-proBNP (or an adjusted/lower BNP cutoff) rather than standard BNP thresholds in an obese patient with suspected heart failure. In dialysis patients, a GFR-adjusted NT-proBNP threshold around 1200 pg/mL has been proposed rather than trusting standard cutoffs.

What ACTUALLY Meets Criteria for a CHF Exacerbation — There's No Single Threshold

Unlike troponin/MI, there is no universal consensus diagnostic threshold that defines 'decompensation.' Natriuretic peptides are used in TWO distinct ways, and conflating them is the most common source of confusion: (1) a RULE-OUT threshold for whether HF is contributing to undifferentiated dyspnea at all (BNP <100 pg/mL or NT-proBNP <300 pg/mL, NPV >98% — remembering the obesity caveat that falsely lowers BNP), and (2) a SUPPORTIVE/rule-in range once HF is already established as a diagnostic possibility (BNP >400–500 pg/mL, or age-stratified NT-proBNP roughly >450 pg/mL under 50, >900 pg/mL 50–75, >1800 pg/mL over 75).

For a patient with KNOWN CHRONIC HF, the diagnostically useful signal is almost never the absolute number — it's the CHANGE FROM THAT PATIENT'S OWN STABLE ('dry'/euvolemic) BASELINE. This is explicitly addressed in a joint HFA-ESC/HFSA/JHFS scientific statement: natriuretic peptides have substantial intrinsic biological (day-to-day, non-disease-related) variability — roughly 25% for NT-proBNP and roughly 40% for BNP — so small fluctuations are expected NOISE, not necessarily a clinical event.

The same statement gives an actual working threshold: a change of approximately 25–30% OR MORE from a patient's known stable concentration suggests a genuine change in clinical state (such as decompensation), because that magnitude exceeds the expected biological noise band for both markers.

Practical framework: (1) find the patient's most recent EUVOLEMIC/compensated value if one exists — this is their personal reference point, not a population cutoff; (2) calculate the percent change from that specific reference point to today's value; (3) if the change exceeds ~25–30%, treat it as a real signal warranting clinical correlation, not noise; (4) always correlate with exam/imaging (weight change from documented dry weight, JVD, rales, edema, orthopnea, CXR, point-of-care ultrasound for B-lines/IVC) since the number never diagnoses decompensation alone; (5) actively consider non-volume explanations for a rising value — new atrial fibrillation, worsening renal function, acute coronary ischemia, acute PE with RV strain, sepsis/inflammation, or a medication change (starting sacubitril/valsartan raises BNP, but NOT NT-proBNP, independent of true status).

Worked Case — BNP 1000 (1 year ago) → 200 (2 months ago) → 800 (today): Is This an Exacerbation?

1. Figure out what each old number actually represents
Before doing any math, find out the CONTEXT of the two prior values — this is the step people skip. Was the 200 two months ago drawn while the patient was clinically euvolemic/at their baseline (i.e., their personal 'dry' natriuretic peptide level)? Or was it drawn mid-treatment, post-diuresis, at a doctor's visit where they happened to feel well? Was the 1000 a year ago from a PRIOR decompensation episode (before treatment), or just another data point? Old notes, discharge summaries, and documented dry weight at each visit are where you find this. Without this context, the raw numbers alone can mislead.
2. Identify the right comparator — recent baseline beats remote baseline
Assuming no major interval clinical change, the 2-MONTH-OLD value (200) is the far more relevant comparator than the 1-YEAR-OLD value (1000), simply because it's more likely to reflect this patient's CURRENT baseline physiology (renal function, rhythm, medications). The 1-year-old value is still useful contextually (see step 5) but shouldn't be the primary anchor for judging today's change.
3. Calculate the change and check it against the noise threshold
200 → 800 is a 300% relative increase (4-fold). The expected biological noise band for these markers is roughly 25% (NT-proBNP) to 40% (BNP), and a change of ≥25–30% is considered a genuine, clinically meaningful signal per the joint HFA/HFSA/JHFS statement. A 300% rise is DRAMATICALLY beyond either of those thresholds — this is not assay noise or day-to-day biological variability. Something real changed.
4. Correlate with the clinical picture TODAY — the number alone never makes the diagnosis
Look for: dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, and their trajectory; weight gain compared to the patient's DOCUMENTED dry weight (not just 'how they look'); JVD, rales, S3, peripheral edema on exam; CXR for vascular congestion/effusions; point-of-care ultrasound for B-lines and a plethoric IVC. If these are present and consistent, the biomarker trend supports (not solely diagnoses) an acute decompensation. If the patient looks and feels well despite the number, do NOT anchor on 'the BNP is high' — move to step 5.
5. Actively hunt for alternative explanations for the rise before accepting 'decompensation' at face value
Check current renal function against their baseline (even modest new CKD progression meaningfully raises NT-proBNP independent of volume status). Check the rhythm — new or faster atrial fibrillation independently raises natriuretic peptides. Check for a medication change — if sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto) was STARTED between the 200 and today's draw, that alone inflates measured BNP (but NOT NT-proBNP) without necessarily reflecting true decompensation; this is why knowing exactly which marker (BNP vs. NT-proBNP) was used for all three values matters. Check troponin/ECG for an acute ischemic event (ACS can both directly raise natriuretic peptides via acute wall stress and precipitate a true decompensation). Consider acute PE (RV strain) and infection/sepsis as other legitimate causes of a rising value that aren't simple volume overload.
6. Use the remote (1-year-old) value as corroborating context, not the primary comparator
If the 1000 from a year ago represented a PAST acknowledged decompensation, it tells you this patient's personal 'wet' range runs roughly 800–1000+. Today's 800 sitting close to that prior decompensated range, on top of a 4-fold rise from a recent stable baseline, is corroborating evidence pointing the same direction — toward a real, current decompensation rather than a benign fluctuation.
Bottom line
A rise this large (300%, far beyond the ~25–30% biologic noise threshold) from a recent, presumably-stable baseline is a genuine biological signal, not noise — but whether it equals 'CHF exacerbation' still depends on correlating it with today's symptoms, exam, and imaging, and on ruling out non-volume drivers (renal change, new arrhythmia, medication change, ischemia, PE, infection) that can raise the number without a primary fluid-overload event. The biomarker trend earns this diagnosis a strong place at the top of your differential; it doesn't clinch it by itself.

Can You Directly Compare a Troponin T Value to a Troponin I Value? NO.

Troponin T and troponin I are measured by COMPLETELY DIFFERENT IMMUNOASSAYS using different antibodies against different epitopes of different proteins. Even though both are typically reported in the same units (ng/L for high-sensitivity assays), the NUMBERS ARE NOT INTERCHANGEABLE — a troponin T of 20 ng/L and a troponin I of 20 ng/L do not represent the same degree of myocardial injury, and there is no simple conversion factor between them.

Each has its OWN assay-specific and often sex-specific 99th percentile upper reference limit that must be used to interpret it: a commonly cited hs-cTnT (Roche Elecsys) 99th percentile is 14 ng/L (same cutoff used for both sexes in many labs), while hs-cTnI assays have distinctly different, often sex-specific cutoffs — for example, the Abbott Architect hs-cTnI 99th percentile is commonly cited around 16 ng/L for women and 34 ng/L for men (values vary somewhat by platform and reference population).

The two assays are also NOT interchangeable between manufacturers even within the SAME troponin type — hs-cTnI from Abbott, Beckman, Siemens, and Ortho platforms all have their own distinct cutoffs and cannot be substituted for one another in a validated rule-out algorithm. If a patient's first troponin was run on one assay/platform and the repeat is run on a different one, the ESC delta-based algorithms are NOT valid — you need paired values from the SAME assay.

Bottom line for your bedside question: you can compare a patient's OWN troponin T value to their OWN prior troponin T value (trending the same assay over time is exactly how repeat-testing algorithms work), but you cannot take Patient A's troponin T level and compare it numerically to Patient B's troponin I level, or to a general 'troponin' reference range, without knowing which specific assay produced each number.

Are They at Least Clinically Equivalent in Performance?

For the general population and the standard indication (ruling in/out acute MI), international guidelines currently treat hs-cTnT and hs-cTnI as CLINICALLY EQUIVALENT — comparable sensitivity and specificity for AMI, similar negative predictive value in validated rapid rule-out algorithms.

But real differences exist beneath that equivalence. hs-cTnI tends to rise and fall FASTER than hs-cTnT after myocardial injury (faster release from necrotic tissue), and only hs-cTnT has a well-documented SECOND PEAK phenomenon around 4–5 days post-MI (not seen reliably with hs-cTnI) — a kinetic quirk that does not appear to worsen prognosis but can confuse interpretation if you're trending values a week out.

The biggest practical divergence is RENAL DISEASE: hs-cTnT is chronically elevated in a much larger proportion of CKD/dialysis patients than hs-cTnI is, because cTnT clearance depends more on the kidney while cTnI clearance is thought to rely more on hepatic/other pathways. In one series of CKD stage ≥3a patients without MI, median hs-cTnT was frankly pathological (~27 ng/L) while median hs-cTnI in the same patients was normal (~11 ng/L). If you're managing a dialysis patient with a borderline troponin, which assay your lab uses meaningfully changes how alarming that number should be.

Practical translation: don't ask 'is troponin high' in the abstract — ask 'is THIS assay's value, in THIS patient's context (especially renal function), above THIS assay's validated cutoff, and has it CHANGED appropriately on a repeat drawn on the SAME assay.'

The Repeat-Troponin Timing Question — Why Your Hospital Says 2 Hours and Others Say 1

The variation you've noticed (2-hour protocols vs. 1-hour protocols at other hospitals) reflects REAL, guideline-endorsed variation — not one hospital being wrong. The European Society of Cardiology (ESC) formally endorses THREE validated rapid algorithms for high-sensitivity troponin: the 0h/1h algorithm, the 0h/2h algorithm, and the older 0h/3h algorithm. The 0h/1h and 0h/2h algorithms carry the ESC's strongest (Class I) recommendation; the 0h/3h algorithm has been explicitly DOWNGRADED in recent guidance because multiple studies show it is less accurate at excluding MI than the faster protocols.

WHICH algorithm a given hospital uses depends on which has been VALIDATED FOR THEIR SPECIFIC ASSAY. The 0h/1h and 0h/2h cutoff values are assay-specific (different numbers for Roche hs-cTnT vs. Abbott hs-cTnI vs. Siemens vs. Beckman platforms) and were derived from prospective validation studies using that exact assay. A hospital cannot simply adopt the '1-hour' protocol unless their specific assay has published, validated 0/1h cutoffs — if it doesn't, they default to the 0/2h or 0/3h approach instead. This is very likely the real explanation for the difference you've observed: different hospitals are running different troponin assays, each with its own validated timing.

There is also a practical workflow dimension: even where a 1-hour protocol is analytically valid, some institutions prefer a 2- or 3-hour interval to build in a wider margin for phlebotomy/lab turnaround delays, or because their EHR order sets and nursing workflows were built around an older 3-hour standard and haven't been updated. This is an operational choice layered on top of the underlying assay validation.

None of this means the protocols are interchangeable within a single patient encounter — whichever algorithm your hospital uses, the FIRST and REPEAT draws must both come from the same validated assay and be interpreted using that specific algorithm's cutoffs, not a generic 'troponin doubled' rule of thumb.

What ACTUALLY Meets Criteria for NSTEMI — It's Not Just a Number

Per the Fourth Universal Definition of MI, the diagnosis has TWO required parts, and a single elevated number satisfies neither alone. Part 1: at least one troponin value above the assay's 99th percentile URL — this alone only establishes MYOCARDIAL INJURY, not infarction. Part 2: a RISE AND/OR FALL pattern on serial testing, PLUS at least one of: ischemic symptoms, new ischemic ECG changes, new pathologic Q waves, imaging evidence of new regional wall motion abnormality/loss of viable myocardium, or angiographic/autopsy identification of a coronary thrombus.

A troponin that is elevated but STABLE (not rising or falling) across serial draws is, by definition, CHRONIC myocardial injury, not acute MI — the classic example is a dialysis patient with a chronically elevated baseline troponin that doesn't move. This is exactly why the delta between serial values matters as much as the absolute number.

Here is the honest gap in the guideline: what magnitude of 'rise and/or fall' actually counts as significant is NOT precisely numerically defined in the Fourth Universal Definition for spontaneous (type 1/2) MI — this is acknowledged in the literature as a genuine point of ongoing debate, not something you're missing. In PRACTICE, this ambiguity is resolved operationally by the ESC 0/1h, 0/2h, and 0/3h algorithms, which DO specify concrete, assay-specific absolute delta thresholds (in ng/L) for rule-in/rule-out — those algorithm cutoffs are the closest thing to a hard numeric answer for 'how much of a rise counts,' and they're deliberately assay-specific because a fixed percentage doesn't behave consistently across the full range of troponin values.

The one place the guideline DOES commit to a specific percentage is periprocedural (type 4a PCI-related, type 5 CABG-related) MI, where 'stable' is explicitly defined as ≤20% variation, and a periprocedural diagnosis requires a rise of >20% to an absolute value exceeding a multiple (5x for PCI, 10x for CABG) of the 99th percentile — but this specific 20%/5x/10x framework applies ONLY to the periprocedural context, not to a typical ED rule-in/rule-out decision.

Bottom line for your bedside question: NSTEMI = (99th percentile exceeded) + (a real rise/fall pattern, best judged against your specific assay's validated algorithm delta) + (a clinical/ECG/imaging correlate of ischemia). Any one of those three legs missing means the diagnosis isn't yet met, regardless of how high the single number is.

Clinical Pearls
  • Ask your lab which specific hs-troponin assay (manufacturer AND troponin type) is in use, and which ESC algorithm has been validated and adopted for it — that's the real answer to 'why does my hospital use 2 hours.'
  • A single very low hs-troponin at presentation (well below the assay's limit of detection, typically only if symptom onset was >3 hours prior) can sometimes rule out MI with a SINGLE draw under some 0/1h protocols — but this shortcut is assay- and protocol-specific and should not be assumed to apply universally.
  • Never mix assay platforms between the index and repeat draw when applying a delta-based algorithm — doing so invalidates the validated cutoffs.
  • In renal failure, sepsis, and other chronic troponin-elevating states, the ABSOLUTE value matters less than whether there is a significant, assay-specific DELTA between serial draws — a stably elevated troponin without a rising/falling pattern points away from an acute event.

HEART score, PERC, and the pregnancy-adapted D-dimer algorithm are the three risk tools that most directly change ED disposition for chest pain and suspected PE — each one is validated for a specific population and fails when used outside it.

HEART Score — Undifferentiated Chest Pain

Five components, each scored 0–2, for a total of 0–10: History (suspicion based on the story), ECG, Age, Risk factors (traditional CAD risk factors and known atherosclerotic disease), and Troponin.

Risk tiers: LOW risk 0–3 (30-day MACE ~0.9–1.7%, historically cited as low as 2.5% in the original derivation), MODERATE risk 4–6 (~12–16.6%), HIGH risk 7–10 (~50–65%). Low-risk patients are generally considered safe for outpatient follow-up without further inpatient workup; moderate/high-risk patients generally warrant further inpatient evaluation.

HEART requires a troponin value to complete, and in practice a REPEAT troponin is commonly needed to finalize the score and disposition — which is exactly why the repeat-troponin timing protocol you're asking about is operationally tied to how fast a HEART score (and therefore a disposition decision) can be finalized.

Known limitation: the History and ECG components are inherently somewhat subjective, and inter-rater agreement on the 'real-world' HEART score is imperfect outside of tightly controlled research settings — use it as a structured aid to clinical judgment, not a replacement for it.

PERC Rule — Ruling Out PE Without Any Testing

An 8-item bedside checklist, validated ONLY for patients already judged LOW pretest probability for PE (it is not a stand-alone screening tool for undifferentiated patients) and only studied in the 18–50 age range.

The 8 criteria (ALL must be met/negative for PERC-negative): age <50, initial heart rate <100 bpm, initial O2 saturation ≥95%, no unilateral leg swelling, no hemoptysis, no recent surgery/trauma (within 4 weeks requiring hospitalization/general anesthesia), no prior VTE (PE or DVT), and no exogenous estrogen use (oral contraceptives, HRT).

If a patient is PERC-negative AND was already judged low pretest probability, the rule supports foregoing D-dimer/imaging entirely, with a pooled PE-miss rate typically cited under 1% in validation meta-analyses. Some later, larger studies have found the miss rate and negative likelihood ratio less reassuring than originally reported, so PERC should be applied as a rule-out aid layered on genuine low clinical suspicion — not as a substitute for gestalt in a patient who otherwise looks concerning.

D-Dimer in Pregnancy — the Pregnancy-Adapted YEARS Algorithm

Standard D-dimer is nearly useless late in pregnancy using non-pregnant cutoffs — D-dimer rises physiologically throughout gestation, so a fixed <500 ng/mL threshold produces an unacceptably high false-positive (and therefore unnecessary-imaging) rate by the third trimester.

The pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm solves this by combining a SIMPLIFIED 3-item clinical gestalt with a VARIABLE D-dimer cutoff rather than raising the cutoff uniformly. The three YEARS items are: (1) clinical signs of DVT, (2) hemoptysis, and (3) whether PE is the most likely diagnosis.

The algorithm: if the patient meets NONE of the 3 YEARS criteria, PE can be excluded if the D-dimer is <1000 ng/mL. If the patient meets ONE OR MORE of the 3 criteria, PE can only be excluded if the D-dimer is <500 ng/mL (the standard non-pregnant threshold). This NEJM-validated approach allows a substantial proportion of pregnant patients with suspected PE to avoid CT pulmonary angiography or V/Q scanning (and the associated radiation exposure to mother and fetus) entirely.

Practical framing: a pregnant patient with no DVT signs, no hemoptysis, and a low overall clinical suspicion gets the MORE PERMISSIVE 1000 ng/mL cutoff; a pregnant patient with any one of those three features is held to the STRICTER standard 500 ng/mL cutoff before imaging can be safely deferred.

TIMI & GRACE — Not Diagnostic Tools, Prognostic/Therapeutic Tools

Unlike HEART (designed for undifferentiated ED chest pain), TIMI and GRACE were derived and validated in patients with ALREADY CONFIRMED OR STRONGLY SUSPECTED ACS — they stratify prognosis and help guide the timing/intensity of invasive management (e.g., early invasive vs. selective invasive strategy in NSTE-ACS), not whether the patient has ACS in the first place.

Using TIMI/GRACE as a rule-out tool in an undifferentiated chest pain patient is a category error — current ACC/AHA guidance explicitly notes these scores are not diagnostic instruments and should be reserved for patients with an established or strongly suspected ACS diagnosis.

Additional Risk Scores to Know

Wells Score (Pulmonary Embolism)

UsePretest probability of PE, the companion tool to PERC/D-dimer when PERC is not applicable or is positive.
Components7 items: clinical signs/symptoms of DVT (+3), PE is the #1 diagnosis or equally likely (+3, the single most heavily weighted and most subjective item), heart rate >100 (+1.5), immobilization ≥ 3 days or surgery in the past 4 weeks (+1.5), prior DVT/PE (+1.5), hemoptysis (+1), active malignancy (+1).
InterpretationTwo-tier (most guideline-recommended): ≤4 = PE unlikely (~8% prevalence, D-dimer can reasonably exclude PE); >4 = PE likely (~34% prevalence, proceed to imaging rather than D-dimer). Three-tier: <2 low, 2–6 moderate, >6 high.

Revised Geneva Score (Pulmonary Embolism)

UseAn alternative to Wells that relies entirely on objective clinical variables (age, heart rate, prior VTE, recent surgery/fracture, hemoptysis, active cancer, unilateral leg pain, pain on palpation + edema) — no subjective gestalt item, which some find preferable for inter-rater consistency.
ComponentsThree-tier interpretation: <3 points = low probability, 4–10 = intermediate, ≥11 = high probability.
InterpretationPerforms comparably to Wells in most validation studies; some data suggest it underperforms Wells in specific populations (e.g., critical illness), so neither tool should override strong clinical gestalt in a genuinely high-suspicion patient.

PESI / sPESI (Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index)

UseOnce PE is DIAGNOSED, PESI risk-stratifies for 30-day mortality/complication risk — the key tool for deciding outpatient treatment vs. admission for a confirmed PE.
ComponentsFull PESI has 11 weighted variables (age in years contributes directly; male sex +10; cancer history +30; heart failure history +10; chronic lung disease +10; HR ≥110 +20; SBP <100 +30; RR ≥30 +20; temp <36°C +20; altered mental status +60; O2 sat <90% +20). The simplified version (sPESI) collapses this to 6 binary yes/no items, each worth 1 point (age >80, cancer, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, HR ≥110, SBP <100, O2 sat <90%).
InterpretationsPESI = 0 identifies patients potentially safe for outpatient PE management; ≥1 point suggests a higher-risk PE warranting inpatient care. Full PESI stratifies into 5 classes (I–V) with progressively increasing 30-day mortality.

CHA₂DS₂-VASc (Stroke Risk in Atrial Fibrillation)

UseEstimates annual ischemic stroke risk in non-valvular AF and directly informs the anticoagulation decision.
ComponentsCongestive heart failure/LV dysfunction (1), Hypertension (1), Age ≥75 (2), Diabetes (1), prior Stroke/TIA/thromboembolism (2), Vascular disease — prior MI/PAD/aortic plaque (1), Age 65–74 (1), Sex category — female (1). Maximum 9–10 depending on convention.
InterpretationScore 0 (male)/1 (female, from sex point alone) = low risk, anticoagulation generally not recommended; score ≥2 in men or ≥3 in women generally favors anticoagulation. This score is dynamic — recalculate at follow-up, since risk climbs as patients age into new brackets.

HAS-BLED (Bleeding Risk on Anticoagulation)

UseThe bleeding-risk companion to CHA₂DS₂-VASc — used to identify and address MODIFIABLE bleeding risk factors, not simply to withhold anticoagulation.
ComponentsHypertension (uncontrolled, SBP >160) (1), Abnormal renal function (dialysis/transplant/Cr >2.6) (1), Abnormal liver function (1), prior Stroke (1), Bleeding history/predisposition (1), Labile INR (1), Elderly >65 (1), Drugs (antiplatelets/NSAIDs) or alcohol excess (1 each, max 2).
InterpretationScore ≥3 signals high bleeding risk — the intent is NOT to withhold anticoagulation in high CHA₂DS₂-VASc patients, but to correct what's modifiable (blood pressure control, reviewing concurrent antiplatelets/NSAIDs, alcohol counseling) and increase monitoring frequency.

Killip Classification (STEMI Severity)

UseA simple bedside classification of heart failure severity complicating acute MI, strongly predictive of in-hospital mortality — no labs required, pure physical exam.
ComponentsClass I: no signs of heart failure. Class II: rales/crackles, S3, or elevated JVP. Class III: frank pulmonary edema. Class IV: cardiogenic shock (hypotension + peripheral hypoperfusion — oliguria, cyanosis, diaphoresis).
InterpretationClassic mortality gradient roughly 6% (I) → 17% (II) → 38% (III) → 81% (IV) in the original description; contemporary reperfusion-era cohorts show lower absolute numbers but the same steep, graded relationship — Killip IV remains one of the strongest single predictors of in-hospital death after STEMI.

CRUSADE Bleeding Score (NSTE-ACS)

UseEstimates in-hospital major bleeding risk in NSTE-ACS patients being considered for antiplatelet/anticoagulant therapy — the bleeding-risk complement to TIMI/GRACE, analogous to how HAS-BLED complements CHA₂DS₂-VASc in AF.
ComponentsIncorporates baseline hematocrit, creatinine clearance, heart rate, sex, signs of heart failure at presentation, prior vascular disease, diabetes, and systolic blood pressure into a weighted score.
InterpretationHigher scores identify patients where bleeding avoidance strategies (radial access, lower-intensity anticoagulation, shorter DAPT duration) are especially worth emphasizing — used to inform, not replace, the ACS treatment decision.

Ottawa Heart Failure Risk Scale (OHFRS)

UseSpecifically designed for the ED disposition decision in acute heart failure — directly answers 'can this patient go home' after initial treatment, which is exactly the disposition question CHF patients raise.
ComponentsA 10-item scale incorporating history of MI, HR ≥110 on arrival, O2 sat <90% at triage, new ischemic ECG changes, NT-proBNP ≥5000 ng/L, elevated troponin, prior history of intubation for respiratory distress, and several other clinical/exam variables assessed AFTER initial ED treatment (not on arrival).
InterpretationScore of 0 corresponds to roughly 2.8% risk of a serious adverse event (30-day death, ICU-level care, relapse admission); score of 9 corresponds to roughly 90% risk. A threshold of >1 maximizes sensitivity but increases admissions; >2 reduces unnecessary admissions with similar sensitivity to unstructured clinical judgment. Explicitly NOT for use before initial ED intervention or in hemodynamically unstable patients.

EDACS (Emergency Department Assessment of Chest pain Score)

UseAn alternative to HEART for risk-stratifying undifferentiated chest pain, generally paired with an accelerated 0/2h troponin protocol as part of a complete accelerated diagnostic pathway.
ComponentsCombines age, sex, known coronary risk factors, and specific pain characteristics into a weighted numeric score, then combines that with troponin and ECG results in the full ADP (accelerated diagnostic protocol).
InterpretationDesigned to identify a larger proportion of low-risk patients suitable for early discharge than HEART alone in some validation cohorts — institutional adoption varies, so know which score (HEART vs. EDACS) your department has actually validated and built its chest pain pathway around.

A Holter report is a summary of a continuous ambulatory ECG recording (classically 24–48h, though extended patch monitors now run 1–2 weeks). Reading one well means knowing which numbers are just descriptive noise and which ones actually change management.

Step 1 — Confirm the Recording Is Adequate

Check the MONITORING DURATION and the % OF TIME WITH ANALYZABLE DATA (artifact-free). A report with large gaps or heavy artifact undermines everything that follows — a 'normal' Holter with only 60% usable data is a much weaker negative than one with 98% usable data.

Check whether the patient's SYMPTOM DIARY correlates with any recorded event. A Holter's single most useful function is often simply confirming (or excluding) that a specific symptom episode corresponds to an arrhythmia — a report that captured symptoms with a NORMAL rhythm at that exact moment is a genuinely reassuring negative; a report where symptoms occurred during a gap in recording proves nothing either way.

Step 2 — Overall Rate and Rhythm Summary

Average, minimum, and maximum heart rate over the recording period, plus the overall underlying rhythm (sinus, atrial fibrillation, paced, etc.). Note the TIME OF DAY for the minimum/maximum — a minimum heart rate of 38 bpm at 3 AM in a young, fit patient is very different from the same number during a symptomatic episode of lightheadedness at noon.

Heart rate variability (HRV) metrics are sometimes reported but are primarily a RESEARCH/prognostic tool (low HRV correlates with worse cardiovascular outcomes in some populations) rather than a number that directly changes acute management in most routine reads.

Step 3 — Ectopy Burden

PVC BURDEN (reported as a % of total beats that are PVCs) is one of the most clinically important numbers on the report. A PVC burden below roughly 10% is generally considered low-risk; a burden ABOVE ROUGHLY 10–15% (some literature cites thresholds as low as 10%, others up to 20-24% depending on the study) raises concern for PVC-INDUCED CARDIOMYOPATHY, a reversible form of cardiomyopathy from a very high burden of ectopy — this is the number that most often changes management (echo to assess EF, consideration of ablation/antiarrhythmic therapy).

Also note the PVC MORPHOLOGY (single monomorphic focus vs. multiple morphologies — multifocal PVCs suggest more diffuse electrical instability) and any COUPLING PATTERNS (bigeminy, trigeminy) or RUNS (couplets, triplets, or sustained/non-sustained VT).

PAC burden is reported similarly but is generally of less prognostic concern unless very high burden or associated with runs of SVT/atrial fibrillation; a high PAC burden can be a harbinger of incident atrial fibrillation and is worth noting in a patient with palpitations or cryptogenic stroke workup.

Step 4 — Pauses, Blocks, and Bradyarrhythmias

PAUSES are reported by their longest duration; a sinus pause under ~3 seconds is often incidental (especially during sleep in young, fit patients with high vagal tone), while pauses approaching or exceeding 3 SECONDS while awake, or ANY pause associated with symptoms (syncope, presyncope), are significant and often prompt pacemaker evaluation.

AV BLOCK episodes should be characterized by degree: first-degree (prolonged PR, rarely significant alone), second-degree Mobitz I/Wenckebach (usually benign, vagally mediated, common in sleep), second-degree Mobitz II (more concerning, often indicates infranodal disease progressing toward complete block), and any THIRD-DEGREE (complete) AV block, which is a significant finding regardless of symptoms and generally warrants pacemaker evaluation.

SICK SINUS SYNDROME patterns — alternating bradycardia and tachycardia (tachy-brady syndrome), sinus pauses, or chronotropic incompetence (failure of the heart rate to rise appropriately with activity) — are often best appreciated on Holter given their intermittent nature.

Step 5 — Tachyarrhythmias

Any SUPRAVENTRICULAR TACHYCARDIA (SVT) episodes should be noted by duration, rate, and symptom correlation — brief, asymptomatic runs are common and often incidental; sustained or symptomatic runs are more clinically important.

ATRIAL FIBRILLATION/FLUTTER burden (the % of total monitored time spent in AF) is a key number, especially in the context of cryptogenic stroke workup (looking for occult paroxysmal AF) — even brief, asymptomatic episodes of AF lasting minutes can be clinically actionable depending on the clinical question being asked.

VENTRICULAR TACHYCARDIA runs (3+ consecutive PVCs at a rate >100) should be characterized as NON-SUSTAINED (<30 seconds, terminates spontaneously) or SUSTAINED (≥30 seconds or hemodynamically unstable) — sustained VT is a significant finding requiring urgent cardiology involvement regardless of the rest of the report.

Clinical Pearls
  • The most common bedside question a Holter answers is 'did this patient's symptom correspond to an arrhythmia' — always check the symptom-rhythm correlation log before anything else.
  • A PVC burden climbing above ~10-15% is the number that should trigger you to check an echocardiogram for PVC-induced cardiomyopathy, even in an otherwise asymptomatic patient.
  • Mobitz I (Wenckebach) at night in a young athlete is usually a normal vagal finding; the same pattern during the day, or any Mobitz II or complete heart block at any time, is a different clinical animal.
  • A 'normal' Holter does not exclude an arrhythmic cause of symptoms if the patient never had a symptom during the recording window — consider extended monitoring (patch monitor, event monitor, implantable loop recorder) if suspicion remains high and the initial Holter was unrevealing.

Pacemaker language is built on a standardized 3–5 letter code (the NBG/NASPE-BPEG code) and a small set of ECG patterns. Once you know what 'A-paced' and 'V-paced' actually mean electrically, both the 12-lead and the interrogation printout stop being mysterious.

The NBG Pacemaker Code — What DDD, VVI, etc. Actually Mean

Letter 1 — CHAMBER PACED: A (atrium), V (ventricle), D (dual, both), or O (none).

Letter 2 — CHAMBER SENSED: A, V, D, or O (none) — this is the chamber(s) the device is 'listening' to for intrinsic (native) electrical activity.

Letter 3 — RESPONSE TO A SENSED EVENT: I (inhibited — a sensed native beat stops/withholds the pacing output), T (triggered — a sensed event triggers an immediate paced output, rarely used alone), D (dual — both inhibition and triggering, the standard modern dual-chamber behavior), or O (no response / fixed-rate asynchronous pacing).

A 4th letter (R) indicates RATE RESPONSIVENESS — the device has a sensor (usually an accelerometer) that increases the paced rate with physical activity, mimicking a normal chronotropic response.

Common modes decoded: VVI = paces the ventricle, senses the ventricle, inhibits pacing if it senses a native ventricular beat (classic single-lead mode, often used in chronic AF with slow ventricular response, since there's no atrial signal worth tracking). DDD = paces AND senses BOTH chambers, with dual response — the standard, most physiologic dual-chamber mode: a sensed native P-wave inhibits the atrial output but TRIGGERS a ventricular output after a programmed AV delay if no native QRS follows. AAI = atrial-only pacing/sensing, used when AV conduction is intact and only the SA node is diseased (rare in isolation given AV disease risk over time).

What 'A-Paced' and 'V-Paced' Actually Look Like

ATRIAL PACING (A-paced): a pacing spike immediately precedes a P-wave, which is then followed by a NORMAL, NARROW QRS complex (assuming intact AV conduction) — because once the atrium is artificially depolarized, the impulse travels down the native His-Purkinje system exactly as it would with a natural sinus beat.

VENTRICULAR PACING (V-paced): a pacing spike immediately precedes a WIDE QRS complex with a morphology that typically resembles LEFT BUNDLE BRANCH BLOCK (when the lead tip is in the right ventricle, as is standard) — wide and bizarre because the impulse spreads slowly, muscle-to-muscle, rather than through the fast native conduction system.

DUAL-CHAMBER (AV sequential) PACING: you will see TWO spikes — an atrial spike followed by a P-wave, then after the programmed AV delay, a ventricular spike followed by a wide QRS. This is the classic appearance of a functioning DDD pacemaker.

On an interrogation report/EGM printout, 'AS' denotes a sensed (intrinsic) atrial event, 'AP' denotes a paced atrial event, 'VS' denotes a sensed (intrinsic) ventricular event, and 'VP' denotes a paced ventricular event — a typical annotated strip might read AS-VP (native P-wave sensed, but no native QRS followed within the AV delay, so the ventricle was paced) or AP-VS (atrium paced, but the patient's own conduction beat the pacemaker to the ventricle, so a native QRS was sensed and ventricular pacing was appropriately inhibited).

Reading the Interrogation Report — Key Sections

BATTERY STATUS / ELECTIVE REPLACEMENT INDICATOR (ERI): reported as remaining voltage/estimated longevity; ERI signals it's time to plan a generator change.

LEAD PARAMETERS: sensing amplitude (how large a native signal the lead detects, in mV) and pacing threshold (the minimum output, in volts at a given pulse width, needed to reliably CAPTURE — i.e., produce a paced beat). A RISING threshold over time can indicate lead maturation issues, fibrosis at the lead tip, or lead fracture/dislodgement and needs attention since it threatens loss of capture.

PERCENT PACING: the % of time the device is actually pacing each chamber (e.g., '2% RA pacing, 98% RV pacing') — this is clinically important because HIGH CHRONIC RV PACING BURDEN is associated with pacing-induced cardiomyopathy over time (the same dyssynchronous, LBBB-like activation pattern that makes V-paced beats wide on ECG is mechanically inefficient if it dominates), which is why device programming increasingly tries to MINIMIZE unnecessary RV pacing when possible.

STORED EVENTS / ARRHYTHMIA LOGS: episodes of atrial high-rate events (AHRE, often the device's terminology for detected atrial fibrillation/flutter), mode switches (the device automatically switching out of a tracking mode like DDD into a non-tracking mode like DDI/VVI when it detects a pathologic atrial tachyarrhythmia, to avoid tracking the fast atrial rate into the ventricle), and any detected ventricular arrhythmias with therapy delivered (for a combined pacemaker-defibrillator/ICD).

CAPTURE and SENSING TESTS: the interrogation typically includes a real-time threshold test confirming each lead still reliably captures and senses — loss of capture (a pacing spike with NO following P-wave or QRS) or failure to sense (spikes delivered inappropriately close to/within a native beat, or failure to inhibit appropriately) are the two classic device malfunctions to look for.

Clinical Pearls
  • AS-VP means 'the atrium did its own thing, but the ventricle needed help' — this is completely normal DDD behavior in a patient with AV node disease and intact sinus function.
  • A wide, LBBB-morphology QRS immediately following a pacing spike is expected and normal for RV pacing — don't mistake it for a new bundle branch block or ischemia on an unrelated ECG read.
  • High cumulative RV pacing burden (commonly cited concern above roughly 40–50% depending on the study) raises concern for pacing-induced cardiomyopathy — if a paced patient develops new reduced EF, ask what % of the time they're being ventricularly paced.
  • A rising pacing threshold or a sudden drop in sensed amplitude on serial interrogations is an early warning of lead problems and should prompt closer monitoring or lead revision before overt failure to capture/sense occurs.
  • Mode switching episodes in the log are the device's own detection of atrial fibrillation/flutter — review these directly if you're trying to establish an AF burden in a pacemaker patient, since this is often more sensitive than any external monitor.

An echo report is organized around a handful of structural questions in a consistent order: how well does the LV squeeze, how well does it relax, are the valves working, and are the chambers the right size. Reading it in that order turns a wall of numbers into a coherent story.

Step 1 — Left Ventricular Systolic Function (Ejection Fraction)

EF is reported as a percentage (normal roughly 55–70%) or increasingly as a category: NORMAL (≥52%men/54%women roughly, guideline cutoffs vary slightly), MILDLY reduced (41–49%, now termed HFmrEF — heart failure with mildly reduced EF), MODERATELY reduced, and SEVERELY reduced (≤35%, HFrEF).

EF is most often measured by Simpson's biplane method (tracing the endocardial border in two views) or visually estimated ("eyeballed") — the report should specify the method, since visual estimation has more inter-observer variability.

GLOBAL vs. REGIONAL dysfunction matters: a uniformly reduced EF with no regional wall motion abnormality suggests a global process (dilated cardiomyopathy, longstanding hypertension, toxin/chemo-related, etc.), while a regional wall motion abnormality in a specific coronary distribution (e.g., anteroseptal akinesis = LAD territory) points toward ischemic/infarct-related dysfunction.

Step 2 — Wall Motion Abnormalities

Reported per segment using a standardized 17-segment model, with each segment graded: NORMAL, HYPOKINETIC (reduced but present motion), AKINETIC (no motion), or DYSKINETIC (paradoxical outward bulging during systole, classic for an aneurysm or a very large transmural infarct).

Matching the abnormal segments to a CORONARY TERRITORY is the clinically useful step: anteroseptal/anterior/apical = LAD; inferior/inferoseptal = RCA (or dominant vessel); lateral = LCx. A wall motion abnormality that doesn't respect a single coronary territory suggests a non-ischemic process (myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or a global insult) instead.

APICAL BALLOONING with sparing of the base (giving an 'octopus pot' appearance) is the classic pattern of Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy — a key pattern-recognition finding that doesn't fit a single coronary distribution.

Step 3 — Diastolic Function

Diastolic function describes how well the ventricle RELAXES and FILLS, independent of how well it squeezes — a patient can have preserved EF but significant diastolic dysfunction (this is exactly the physiology of HFpEF, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction).

Graded I through III (sometimes 'indeterminate'): Grade I (impaired relaxation) is the mildest and most common with normal aging — often not clinically significant in isolation. Grade II (pseudonormal filling) and Grade III (restrictive filling pattern) reflect progressively higher left atrial/filling pressures and are more clinically significant.

Key inputs to the grade: E/A ratio (mitral inflow), E/e' ratio (a surrogate for LV filling pressure — an E/e' >14 generally suggests elevated filling pressures), left atrial volume index (chronically elevated filling pressures enlarge the LA over time, making LA size a good 'chronic average' marker of diastolic burden), and tricuspid regurgitation velocity (a surrogate for pulmonary artery systolic pressure, which rises with chronically elevated left-sided filling pressures).

Step 4 — Valves

Each valve is assessed for STENOSIS (restricted opening/forward flow, graded by valve area and gradients) and REGURGITATION (backward leak, graded trace/mild/moderate/severe by color Doppler jet characteristics, vena contracta width, and other quantitative measures).

Aortic stenosis severity: mild (valve area >1.5 cm², mean gradient <20 mmHg), moderate (1.0–1.5 cm², 20–40 mmHg), severe (<1.0 cm², mean gradient ≥40 mmHg, or peak velocity ≥4 m/s) — severe AS with symptoms (syncope, angina, dyspnea) is a major trigger for valve replacement evaluation.

Mitral regurgitation and other valve lesions are graded by similar mild/moderate/severe conventions; a report should also comment on valve MORPHOLOGY (calcification, prolapse, vegetation-suspicious findings, bicuspid vs. tricuspid aortic valve) since the mechanism changes management.

Step 5 — Chamber Size & Other Structural Findings

LEFT ATRIAL size/volume index is a key chronic marker (see diastolic function above) — an enlarged LA in the absence of significant mitral valve disease points toward chronically elevated filling pressures or a history of atrial fibrillation.

LV WALL THICKNESS (septal/posterior wall) assesses for hypertrophy — concentric (uniform thickening, classically from hypertension or aortic stenosis) vs. asymmetric septal hypertrophy (raising concern for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy).

RIGHT VENTRICULAR size and function get their own assessment (TAPSE — tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion — is the most common simple RV function metric; reduced TAPSE, roughly <17 mm, suggests RV dysfunction) — critical in suspected PE with RV strain, pulmonary hypertension, or right-sided heart failure.

PERICARDIAL EFFUSION is reported by size (trace/small/moderate/large) and, if present, should prompt assessment for signs of TAMPONADE PHYSIOLOGY: RV diastolic collapse, exaggerated respiratory variation in mitral/tricuspid inflow velocities, and a plethoric, non-collapsing IVC — these are the ultrasound findings that turn 'there's fluid around the heart' into a hemodynamic emergency.

Clinical Pearls
  • Always read EF alongside diastolic function — a 'normal echo' with preserved EF can still have significant Grade II–III diastolic dysfunction driving a patient's heart failure symptoms (HFpEF).
  • A regional wall motion abnormality that respects a single coronary territory suggests ischemic disease; one that doesn't (especially apical ballooning with basal sparing) should raise Takotsubo or a global/non-ischemic process instead.
  • TAPSE and RV size deserve a specific look in any patient with suspected PE, unexplained hypotension, or known pulmonary hypertension — don't let the report's LV-heavy default framing cause you to skip the right heart.
  • A pericardial effusion report is incomplete without an explicit comment on tamponade physiology (RV collapse, IVC plethora, respiratory flow variation) — ask for it if it's missing and the clinical picture is concerning.

Reading a stress test report means answering three separate questions in sequence: did the patient reach an adequate workload, did anything concerning happen physiologically (symptoms, BP, arrhythmia), and did the ECG/imaging show ischemia — a report can fail on any one of these independent of the others.

Types of Stress Testing

EXERCISE (treadmill/bicycle) ECG stress test: the patient walks/pedals to increasing workload while a 12-lead ECG is monitored; the preferred approach when the patient can exercise adequately and has a baseline ECG that is interpretable for ischemia (i.e., no baseline LBBB, paced rhythm, or significant baseline ST abnormality that would obscure ischemic changes).

STRESS ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY: exercise or pharmacologic (dobutamine) stress with echo imaging before and immediately after peak stress, looking for new or worsening REGIONAL WALL MOTION ABNORMALITIES as the ischemic signal (rather than relying on ECG changes alone) — useful when the baseline ECG is uninterpretable, or when imaging is desired for additional information (valve function, EF).

NUCLEAR (SPECT/PET) PERFUSION IMAGING: radiotracer uptake is compared between stress and rest states; a PERFUSION DEFECT present at stress but that FILLS IN (reverses) at rest indicates inducible ischemia, while a defect that persists at both stress and rest indicates a fixed defect (prior infarct/scar, not acutely ischemic). Pharmacologic agents (regadenoson, adenosine, dipyridamole — vasodilators; or dobutamine as an inotropic/chronotropic alternative) are used when the patient cannot exercise adequately.

Choice of pharmacologic agent matters clinically: vasodilator agents (regadenoson etc.) are contraindicated or require caution in significant reactive airway disease/bronchospasm and in high-grade AV block without a pacemaker; dobutamine is generally preferred in those situations but has its own cautions (significant arrhythmia history, severe hypertension).

Step 1 — Was the Test Adequate?

TARGET HEART RATE: exercise tests aim for ≥85% of the age-predicted maximum heart rate (220 − age, roughly) to be considered a diagnostically adequate effort; a test stopped well short of this target (without another clear reason like severe symptoms or a positive finding) is considered SUBMAXIMAL and a 'negative' result carries much less reassurance.

METABOLIC EQUIVALENTS (METs) ACHIEVED: functional capacity itself is independently prognostic — achieving ≥10 METs generally portends a good prognosis regardless of other findings, while achieving <5 METs is a poor prognostic sign even without positive ischemic findings.

REASON FOR STOPPING should always be explicitly stated: achieved target heart rate (ideal), patient fatigue/dyspnea (may indicate submaximal or deconditioning), angina, significant arrhythmia, or a hypotensive/hypertensive blood pressure response — all of which carry different implications.

Step 2 — Hemodynamic and Symptomatic Response

BLOOD PRESSURE RESPONSE: normal is a progressive rise in systolic BP with increasing workload. A FAILURE OF SYSTOLIC BP TO RISE, or an actual DROP in systolic BP with increasing exercise, is a significant abnormal finding (suggests severe ischemia/LV dysfunction with exercise, or severe aortic stenosis) and is itself an indication to terminate the test and a marker of higher risk.

SYMPTOMS: chest pain/angina reproduced during the test, especially if it matches the patient's presenting symptom and resolves with rest, is a significant positive finding independent of the ECG.

ARRHYTHMIA: frequent or complex ventricular ectopy, or any sustained arrhythmia provoked by exercise, is noted and factored into overall risk assessment regardless of the ischemic ECG/imaging findings.

Step 3 — ECG / Imaging Ischemic Findings

ECG-based positive test: ≥1 mm of HORIZONTAL OR DOWNSLOPING ST-segment depression (measured 60–80 ms after the J-point), or ST-segment ELEVATION in a patient without a prior Q-wave/infarct in that territory (a more ominous finding suggesting transmural ischemia/high-grade proximal stenosis). Upsloping ST depression alone is a much softer, often non-specific finding.

Imaging-based positive test: new or worsening regional wall motion abnormality on stress echo, or a reversible perfusion defect on nuclear imaging (present at stress, fills in at rest) — imaging modalities generally add sensitivity over ECG-only testing, particularly in patients with baseline ECG abnormalities or single-vessel disease.

A 'HIGH-RISK' pattern on any modality — for example, ischemic changes appearing at a LOW workload/heart rate, involving a LARGE myocardial territory, occurring in MULTIPLE coronary distributions, or accompanied by a hypotensive BP response or significant arrhythmia — should prompt consideration of direct referral to coronary angiography rather than watchful waiting, regardless of the specific numeric ST depression.

The Duke Treadmill Score

A validated composite score specifically for EXERCISE ECG (not imaging) stress tests, combining three inputs: exercise time (minutes, standard Bruce protocol), the maximal ST-segment deviation (mm), and the angina index (0 = no angina, 1 = non-limiting angina during the test, 2 = angina that caused the test to stop).

Formula: Duke Treadmill Score = exercise time (minutes) − (5 × ST deviation in mm) − (4 × angina index).

Risk categories: LOW risk ≥+5 (excellent prognosis, 5-year survival >99% in the original validation cohort), MODERATE risk −4 to +4, HIGH risk ≤−5 (5-year survival substantially worse, and these patients are often referred directly for catheterization).

This score is specifically validated for standard exercise ECG treadmill testing and does not directly apply to pharmacologic or imaging-based stress protocols, which have their own separate risk frameworks (e.g., summed stress/rest scores for nuclear perfusion imaging).

Clinical Pearls
  • A stress test can be 'negative' for ischemia and still be a poor test — always check whether target heart rate/adequate METs were actually achieved before accepting a negative result as reassuring.
  • A drop in systolic blood pressure during exercise is a red flag finding that should change your risk assessment regardless of what the ECG or imaging shows.
  • Calculate (or look for) the Duke Treadmill Score on every standard exercise ECG stress test report — it synthesizes exercise capacity, ST changes, and angina into a single validated prognostic number that is more informative than any single component alone.
  • Match the pharmacologic agent to the patient: avoid vasodilator stress agents in significant bronchospastic lung disease or high-grade AV block without a pacemaker; use dobutamine cautiously in patients with significant arrhythmia history or severe hypertension.

1. HEART Score

Low risk (0–3): ~0.9–1.7% 30-day MACE, consider outpatient follow-up. Moderate (4–6): ~12–16.6%. High (7–10): ~50–65%, consider inpatient workup. A repeat troponin is usually required to finalize this score.

2. PERC Rule (low pretest probability only)

Uncheck a box only if that criterion is ABSENT for this patient. All boxes unchecked = PERC-negative. Valid ONLY in patients already judged low pretest probability, ages 18–50.

01 · Endocrine

Sliding-Scale Insulin as Monotherapy for Glycemic Control in Hospitalized Patients

Ambrus DB, O'Connor MJ. Things We Do for No Reason™: Sliding-scale insulin as monotherapy for glycemic control in hospitalized patients. J Hosp Med. 2019;14(2):114–116. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3109

Original rationale

Sliding-scale insulin (SSI) is simple to order — no weight-based dosing calculation, no need to anticipate oral intake. It "treats what you see" with a straightforward algorithm.

Why it persists

Deeply ingrained EMR order-set default; nursing and physician familiarity with a simple, reactive algorithm that doesn't require reassessing the regimen daily.

What the evidence actually shows

  • SSI monotherapy is purely reactive — it treats hyperglycemia only after it has already occurred, rather than preventing it.
  • The landmark RABBIT-2 trial (Umpierrez et al., Diabetes Care 2007) found basal-bolus insulin regimens produced significantly better glycemic control than SSI alone in non-ICU hospitalized patients with type 2 diabetes, with fewer treatment failures.
  • SSI-alone regimens are associated with higher rates of both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia — the reactive, "chase the number" approach tends to overcorrect in both directions.
  • Major society guidance (ADA/Endocrine Society-aligned) now recommends basal-bolus or basal-plus regimens as standard for most non-critically-ill hospitalized patients requiring insulin.

When SSI still has a role

As a correctional/supplemental component layered on top of scheduled basal insulin — not as the sole regimen — or transiently on admission/NPO status before an eating pattern is established.

Bottom line

Don't use sliding-scale insulin as the sole glycemic regimen for hospitalized patients expected to eat. Pair basal insulin with correctional and prandial dosing instead.

02 · Infection Control

Contact Precautions for MRSA and VRE

Young K, Doernberg SB, Snedecor RF, Mallin E. Things We Do for No Reason: Contact precautions for MRSA and VRE. J Hosp Med. 2019;14(3):178–180. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3126
Genuinely contested: this entry drew a formal published rebuttal in the same journal — Maragakis LL, Jernigan JA. "Things We Do For Good Reasons: Contact Precautions for Multidrug-resistant Organisms, Including MRSA and VRE." J Hosp Med. 2019;14(3):194–196. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3169. Presented here as an active debate, not a settled conclusion.

Original rationale

Gowns and gloves for patients colonized/infected with MRSA or VRE, to physically interrupt transmission of multidrug-resistant organisms between patients.

Why it persists

Long-standing infection-control default embedded in hospital policy, and a strong intuitive logic that barrier precautions must reduce transmission.

The case against (Young et al.)

  • Contact precautions are associated with increased rates of noninfectious adverse events — including injuries and medication errors — in isolated patients.
  • Noninfectious hospital adverse events were shown to decline after elimination of contact precautions for MRSA/VRE in at least one before-after cohort study.
  • Contact precautions carry real costs — PPE expense, and documented associations with reduced clinician contact time and patient psychological effects.

The case for continuing (Maragakis & Jernigan rebuttal)

  • Argue the harm data are largely observational/associational, not causally isolated to the precautions themselves.
  • Point out inconsistency in the original argument — the same authors would preserve precautions for rarer, higher-resistance organisms (VRSA/VISA) while dropping them for MRSA/VRE, without a clear transmission-risk rationale for the distinction.

Bottom line

This is one of the series' genuinely unresolved entries. Follow your institution's current infection-control policy — don't treat either side of this debate as settled, and expect it to keep evolving.

03 · Nursing Care / Sleep

Routine Overnight Vital Sign Checks

Orlov NM, Arora VM. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routine overnight vital sign checks. J Hosp Med. 2020;15(5):272–274. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3442

Original rationale

Standard nursing protocols schedule vital signs every 4–6 hours around the clock, ensuring early detection of overnight decompensation.

Why it persists

Default order-set frequency applied uniformly regardless of clinical stability; nobody actively decides to reduce overnight checks, so the default simply continues.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Sleep loss from overnight interruptions is associated with hyperglycemia of hospitalization and worse patient-reported experience.
  • Poor inpatient sleep is linked to disrupted pain control and delayed recovery — inpatient sleep disruption is a recognized, modifiable harm in its own right, not a neutral inconvenience.
  • For clinically stable patients on a predictable trajectory, routine overnight vitals rarely change management compared with a modified or "sleep-friendly" schedule.

When overnight checks are still necessary

Hemodynamically unstable patients, those on frequent-monitoring protocols (e.g., new anticoagulation, active titration, recent procedure), or any patient with a real clinical reason to expect overnight change.

Bottom line

Reserve every-4-hour overnight vitals for patients with an actual indication; consider a modified schedule (e.g., extending the interval overnight) for stable patients to protect sleep.

04 · Rounding / Education

Card-Flipping Rounds

Ricotta DN, Freed JA, Hale AJ. Things We Do for No Reason™: Card flipping rounds. J Hosp Med. 2020;15(8):498–501. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3374

Original rationale

Rounding by moving briskly room-to-room, one "card" (patient) at a time, feels efficient and ensures every patient gets seen in a fixed rotation.

Why it persists

Habit and workflow inertia carried over from residency training structures; it's the default rounding style taught without much reflection on alternatives.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Purely sequential, card-flipping rounds don't reliably prioritize the sickest or most time-sensitive patients first — order is dictated by geography or list order, not acuity.
  • The format limits structured teaching time and bedside presence compared with rounding models built around illness scripts and deliberate prioritization.
  • Team-based, prioritized rounding structures (huddles, acuity-based sequencing) are associated with better team communication and more effective use of rounding time.

Bottom line

Structure rounds around acuity and teaching goals rather than a fixed room-order rotation — prioritize the sickest and most time-sensitive patients deliberately.

05 · Transfusion Medicine

Routinely Prescribing Transfusion Premedication to Prevent Acute Transfusion Reactions

Lim MY, Pagano MB, Metcalf RA. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routinely prescribing transfusion premedication to prevent acute transfusion reactions. J Hosp Med. 2020;15:684–686. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3372

Original rationale

Prophylactic acetaminophen and diphenhydramine before blood product transfusion, to prevent febrile and allergic transfusion reactions.

Why it persists

Standing order-set default embedded in transfusion protocols across most hospitals; premedication feels like a low-risk, reasonable precaution.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Randomized trials have not shown that routine premedication meaningfully reduces the incidence of febrile or allergic transfusion reactions compared with no premedication.
  • Diphenhydramine carries real, avoidable side effects — sedation, anticholinergic effects, and delirium risk, particularly relevant in older hospitalized patients.
  • Premedication can also mask or blunt early signs of a genuine transfusion reaction (fever, mild allergic symptoms), delaying recognition rather than preventing the reaction itself.

When premedication still makes sense

Patients with a documented history of recurrent febrile or allergic transfusion reactions — targeted, not routine, premedication.

Bottom line

Stop routine premedication for all transfusions. Reserve it for patients with a documented reaction history, and watch unmedicated patients appropriately during transfusion instead.

06 · Renal / Labs

Calculating a "Corrected Calcium" Level

Kenny CM, Murphy CE, Boyce DS, Ashley DM, Jahanmir J. Things We Do for No Reason™: Calculating a "corrected calcium" level. J Hosp Med. 2021;16(8):499–501. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3619
A hospitalized patient with hypoalbuminemia has an elevated "corrected calcium" using the standard albumin-adjustment formula — prompting an unnecessary hypercalcemia work-up for a patient whose true ionized calcium is normal.

Original rationale

Total serum calcium is partly protein-bound (mostly to albumin), so a correction formula was developed to estimate what the total calcium "would be" at a normal albumin level, as a proxy for physiologically active (ionized) calcium.

Why it persists

The correction formula is fast, free, and requires no additional blood draw — it's built into many lab systems as an automatic calculated value.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Multiple retrospective studies do not support the accuracy of corrected-calcium equations for reflecting true physiologic (ionized) calcium status in hospitalized patients.
  • The formula performs particularly poorly at correcting for the abnormal serum proteins commonly seen in hospitalized patients — exactly the population it's most often applied to.
  • Relying on the corrected value can trigger unnecessary work-ups for pseudo-hypercalcemia or pseudo-hypocalcemia that wouldn't occur with a direct measurement.

Bottom line

Don't rely on calculated "corrected calcium" for clinically significant hypo- or hypercalcemia. Obtain a direct ionized calcium (iCa) assay when the result will actually change management.

Verify locally: your lab's iCa assay availability and turnaround time — some settings still require send-out testing, which may affect real-time decision-making.

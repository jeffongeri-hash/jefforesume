Prevention & QI

The full prevention spectrum: what happens to survivors after discharge, what catches deterioration before it becomes a code, and what protects both patients and providers when things are reviewed after the fact.

01 Post-Sepsis Syndrome (PICS)

This is not a minor asterisk on "survival"

Up to 50% of sepsis survivors are left with physical and/or psychological long-term effects — post-sepsis syndrome. In one prospective ICU cohort, PICS was present in 70% of survivors at 3 months, 60% at 6 months, and 35% at 12 months — declining over time but far from resolving. A separate 3-year follow-up cohort found 91.2% of survivors had new physical impairments, with new cognitive impairment in 57.9% and new psychological impairment in 40.9% — and only about 30% of the original cohort had regained full functional independence by 3 years (45% had died, ~25% remained functionally dependent).

The three PICS domains

Physical — ICU-acquired weakness, deconditioning, new disability in activities of daily living.

Cognitive — impaired memory, executive function, processing speed; strongly associated with in-ICU delirium duration.

Psychological — anxiety, depression, and PTSD-spectrum symptoms; extends to family members as "PICS-F."

The ABCDEF bundle — the mitigation framework worth knowing

Assess/manage pain · Both spontaneous awakening and breathing trials · Choice of sedation (minimize benzodiazepines) · Delirium monitoring/management · Early mobility · Family engagement. This isn't a post-discharge intervention — it's ICU-stay decisions (sedation depth, delirium screening, how early PT/OT gets involved) that measurably shape the survivorship trajectory. Worth reinforcing with critical care colleagues as directly protective against the numbers above, not just a comfort measure.

02 Readmission Prevention

Readmission at 1 year
44.0%
Pooled meta-analysis, 76 studies — readmission risk climbs over time rather than plateauing quickly.
Readmission within 3 months
~1/3 overall; >40% elderly
Most commonly due to recurrent sepsis or a related complication, not an unrelated new problem.
Long-term mortality
15% at 1mo → 42.3% at 5yr
Sepsis mortality risk does not end at hospital discharge — it's a chronic-disease-like trajectory.
Why the excess readmission risk
Post-sepsis immunosuppression
Immune dysregulation persists for weeks to months after the acute episode, raising vulnerability to new infection — plus elevated risk of HF exacerbation, AKI/CKD progression, and aspiration.
Practical discharge-planning implications

Sepsis survivors — especially those with an ICU stay or prolonged delirium — warrant explicit anticipatory guidance at discharge: expect possible new cognitive/physical limitations, know the specific reasons to return (recurrent infection symptoms, HF/volume overload signs, new confusion), and ideally get connected to post-ICU/post-sepsis follow-up clinics where they exist. This is squarely a "the ED/inpatient team's discharge instructions matter" finding, not just an ICU survivorship-clinic problem.

03 Failure-to-Rescue & Early Warning Systems

Definition

AHRQ defines failure-to-rescue (FTR) as failure or delay in recognizing and responding to a hospitalized patient experiencing complications from a disease process or intervention — typically operationalized as mortality following a complication, though no single definition is universal across institutions. The physiologic rationale for early warning systems: patients typically show signs of deterioration for a median of ~6 hours before cardiac arrest — theoretically enough time to intervene if the signs are caught and acted on.

Does the intervention actually work? Honest evidence grading

A meta-analysis pooling 15 studies (2000–2016) found rapid response systems reduced hospital mortality by 15% (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.76–0.94) — directionally consistent with the SCREEN trial data from Volume 4 (electronic alerting + education + feedback, aRR 0.85 for 90-day mortality across 60,055 patients). But AHRQ's own more recent evidence synthesis grades this as low-to-moderate quality evidence due to heterogeneity — some hospitals see substantial reductions, others see none, and at least one large prospective study of 232 hospitals found no measurable change in an already-declining mortality trend after RRT implementation. Take the "RRTs work" narrative as directionally true but not settled science, and know that implementation quality (composition, activation criteria, afferent-limb design) likely matters more than simply having a team on paper.

This ties directly back to Volumes 1 and 4

NEWS2 (Volume 1) is one of the most common afferent-limb triggers feeding rapid response/escalation systems, and it isn't sepsis-specific — it flags any deteriorating patient, which is exactly the point: your sepsis screening and your hospital's general failure-to-rescue infrastructure should be the same system, not two competing ones. If your institution's sepsis alert and its general deterioration/RRT alert fire independently and inconsistently, that's a systems gap worth flagging to your QI committee — the SCREEN trial's success came specifically from combining alerting with staff education and feedback, not the alert algorithm alone.

04 Medicolegal & Documentation Fallout

Sepsis is not a hypothetical malpractice risk — it's a documented top driver

The most rigorous published analysis (Newman-Toker et al., Johns Hopkins/CRICO, Diagnosis 2019), examining 55,377 closed malpractice claims from CRICO's Comparative Benchmarking System (representing ~29% of all US malpractice claims, 2006–2015), found that vascular events, infections, and cancer — the "Big Three" — account for 74.1% of high-severity diagnosis-related harm claims (53.0% of which resulted in death). Within the infection category specifically, sepsis was the most frequently missed diagnosis. A separate CRICO analysis of inpatient closed claims specifically named infections/sepsis, MI/cardiac events, and failure-to-rescue complications as the top three inpatient diagnostic-error categories — meaning Volume 6's three topics (PICS, failure-to-rescue, and medicolegal risk) aren't three unrelated concerns, they're describing the same underlying vulnerability from three different angles.

What actually drives these claims, per the same body of literature

Failure to order appropriate tests when infection symptoms are present · misinterpreting or failing to follow up on abnormal results (the SIRS-positive-but-uninvestigated scenario from Volume 1) · inadequate documentation of monitoring, assessment, and clinical reasoning · communication breakdowns at shift change or between departments (a classic failure point for a patient trending toward sepsis over several hours across multiple providers).

05 Documentation Checklist — Synthesizing the Whole Series

VOL 1 — If a SIRS/qSOFA criterion is positive but you're not pursuing a sepsis workup, document the specific reasoning (e.g., the HR>90-from-pain-resolved-with-analgesia scenario) — don't leave a positive screening criterion unaddressed in the chart.
VOL 2 — Document SEP-1 timestamps explicitly: time-zero, culture draw time, antibiotic administration time, fluid initiation time — regardless of which way the clinical decision goes.
VOL 3 — Document the actual weight-based fluid volume given, the reassessment method used, and the specific decision point that triggered vasopressor initiation or escalation.
VOL 4 — Document the empiric regimen rationale (source suspected, resistance risk factors considered) and the planned de-escalation/reassessment point.
VOL 5 — For special populations, document explicitly which population-specific adjustment you made and why (e.g., "reduced initial bolus given known HFrEF, reassessed with POCUS after 500mL aliquot").
VOL 6 — At discharge for sepsis survivors, document anticipatory guidance given and any post-sepsis follow-up arranged — this closes the loop between acute care documentation and the readmission-prevention data above.
The single unifying principle across all six volumes

Every volume in this series has converged on the same point from a different direction: reasoning documented in real time is both better medicine and better protection than a good outcome alone. A patient can do well despite an undocumented decision, or poorly despite a well-reasoned one — but the chart is what gets reconstructed months or years later, by you, by a colleague, or by a reviewer who wasn't in the room.

Acute Red Eye

The single highest-volume ocular complaint in the ED. The job is triage, not just diagnosis: separate the five vision- or globe-threatening causes from the much larger pool of benign, self-limited red eye before reaching for antibiotic drops.

Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma

Critical

Pupillary block → IOP spike → optic nerve ischemia within hours

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Fixed mid-dilated pupil + steamy cornea + IOP in the 40–80 mmHg range in a patient with severe pain, halos, and vomiting is angle closure until proven otherwise — the systemic symptoms alone are frequently misdiagnosed as SAH or gastroenteritis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A pre-existing narrow anterior chamber angle (more common in hyperopic, Asian, Inuit, and older patients as the lens continues to grow with age) becomes mechanically occluded when the pupil sits mid-dilated — the position of maximal iris-lens apposition. This blocks aqueous outflow through the trabecular meshwork, and intraocular pressure rises acutely, often into the 40–80 mmHg range. High pressure ischemically damages the iris sphincter (fixing the pupil), causes corneal epithelial edema (the 'steamy' cornea and halos), and threatens the optic nerve directly.

Classic precipitants: pharmacologic dilation (including scopolamine patches, anticholinergic/antihistamine use), dim-room activities (movie theaters), and any mydriatic exam drop given without first checking angle anatomy.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, hours
PainSevere, often with headache, nausea/vomiting
VisionMarkedly decreased, halos around lights
Key exam findingMid-dilated, fixed/sluggish pupil; steamy/hazy cornea; shallow anterior chamber; IOP 40–80 mmHg
Distinguishing testTonometry (IOP); gonioscopy if available
💡

Two of: ocular pain, nausea/vomiting, intermittent blurring with halos, plus three of: IOP >21, conjunctival injection, corneal edema, mid-dilated fixed pupil, shallow chamber — meets diagnostic criteria without needing gonioscopy.

💡

Document IOP numerically, not just 'elevated' — the exact value drives which agents you can safely combine (e.g., avoid additional aqueous suppressants once maximized).

💡

Nausea/vomiting/headache without exam-documented pupil and conjunctival findings is where this gets missed as a GI or neuro complaint.

Anterior Uveitis (Iritis)

Urgent

Inflammatory cell/flare in the anterior chamber; ciliary spasm drives the pain

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Perilimbal flush (redness concentrated around the limbus, sparing the periphery) plus consensual photophobia separates uveitis from simple conjunctivitis, where redness is diffuse and photophobia is absent or mild.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Inflammation of the iris and ciliary body (anterior uveitis) produces leukocytes and protein in the aqueous humor, seen at the slit lamp as 'cells and flare.' Ciliary muscle spasm causes deep, boring pain that classically worsens with accommodation and light exposure in the affected eye. Roughly half of cases are idiopathic; the rest cluster around HLA-B27-associated spondyloarthropathies, sarcoidosis, inflammatory bowel disease, and infectious causes (HSV, syphilis, TB).

A consensual photophobia test is a useful bedside maneuver: shining light in the unaffected eye causes pain in the affected eye via consensual pupillary constriction and ciliary spasm — a finding not seen in simple conjunctivitis.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, days
PainDeep, aching; worse with accommodation/light
VisionMildly to moderately decreased, may be normal early
Key exam findingCells and flare in anterior chamber; perilimbal (ciliary) flush; consensual photophobia; possible posterior synechiae or keratic precipitates
Distinguishing testSlit lamp exam for cell/flare; consensual light test
💡

Posterior synechiae (iris adhering to lens) can distort pupil shape — look for an irregular, poorly-dilating pupil as a clue to recurrent or long-standing disease.

💡

New-onset uveitis in a young patient with back pain or IBD history warrants HLA-B27/rheumatologic workup, not just topical steroids.

💡

Topical steroids for uveitis should be initiated in coordination with ophthalmology when possible — steroids are contraindicated if the underlying cause could be infectious (HSV keratouveitis).

Scleritis

Urgent

Deep, boring pain that wakes the patient; ~50% have underlying systemic autoimmune disease

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Phenylephrine 2.5–10% blanches the superficial conjunctival/episcleral vessels of episcleritis but leaves the deep episcleral plexus of scleritis unaffected — a persistently red eye after phenylephrine points to scleritis, not episcleritis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Scleritis is inflammation of the sclera itself, and unlike episcleritis it can threaten vision through scleral thinning, secondary uveitis (present in roughly a third of anterior scleritis cases), and rarely globe perforation in necrotizing forms. Severe, boring pain radiates in the trigeminal distribution and classically wakes patients from sleep. About half of cases are associated with a systemic autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, relapsing polychondritis).

The involved vessels are the deep episcleral plexus, which gives the sclera a violaceous, bluish-red hue best appreciated in natural daylight rather than exam-room fluorescent light.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, days; pain often nocturnal
PainSevere, deep, boring, radiates; globe tender to palpation
VisionCan be decreased if posterior or with secondary uveitis/keratitis
Key exam findingViolaceous/bluish-red scleral hue in daylight; scleral edema; does NOT blanch with phenylephrine
Distinguishing testPhenylephrine 2.5–10% blanching test (negative in scleritis)
Schematic: superficial (episcleral) vs deep (scleral) vascular involvement — the basis of the phenylephrine blanching test
💡

New scleritis in a patient over 50 with sinus/renal/pulmonary symptoms should prompt ANCA workup for granulomatosis with polyangiitis — ocular disease can be the presenting sign.

💡

Necrotizing scleritis without inflammation ('scleromalacia perforans') can be painless and easily missed in long-standing rheumatoid arthritis.

Endophthalmitis

Critical

Intraocular infection, classically post-surgical or post-injection; sight-threatening within hours to days

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Any red, painful eye with decreased vision in the days-to-weeks after intraocular surgery or injection is endophthalmitis until an ophthalmologist says otherwise — this is a same-day surgical emergency, not a routine post-op check.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Bacterial or fungal infection of the vitreous/aqueous, most often introduced via recent intraocular surgery (cataract surgery is the leading cause), intravitreal injection (e.g., anti-VEGF agents), penetrating trauma, or endogenous seeding from bacteremia/fungemia in an immunosuppressed or hospitalized patient. Inflammation and organism toxins rapidly destroy retinal architecture; visual outcomes correlate directly with time to intravitreal antibiotic administration.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, days after intraocular procedure (or with bacteremia)
PainModerate to severe, deep
VisionMarkedly decreased, progressive
Key exam findingHypopyon, marked anterior chamber reaction, vitreous haze/loss of red reflex, lid edema
Distinguishing testRecent surgical/injection history is the key historical clue; B-scan ultrasound if view obscured
💡

Ask specifically about recent cataract surgery, intravitreal injections, or any ocular procedure — patients may not volunteer this unprompted.

💡

Hypopyon (layered white cells in the anterior chamber) is a hard exam finding to miss once you know to look, but easy to overlook if the room is dim or the slit lamp isn't used.

Bacterial Conjunctivitis

Routine

Purulent discharge, papillary reaction; largely a clinical diagnosis

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Vision that clears with blinking and normal pupil/cornea exam supports simple conjunctivitis — true vision loss or pain out of proportion should prompt reconsideration of the sight-threatening differentials above.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Direct bacterial infection of the conjunctiva (commonly Staph, Strep, H. influenzae in children, and Pseudomonas in contact lens wearers) produces a papillary conjunctival reaction and thick, purulent discharge that reaccumulates rapidly after wiping — a useful bedside discriminator from the watery discharge of viral disease.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, 1–3 days
PainMild irritation/foreign body sensation, not true pain
VisionNormal or minimally blurred (clears with blinking)
Key exam findingPurulent discharge reaccumulating within minutes; papillary reaction; no significant preauricular adenopathy
Distinguishing testClinical; culture only if severe, recurrent, neonatal, or contact-lens-associated
💡

Contact lens wearers with red eye need to be treated as presumed Pseudomonas keratitis risk until fluorescein exam excludes a corneal infiltrate — do not treat empirically as simple conjunctivitis without looking at the cornea.

Viral Conjunctivitis

Routine

Watery discharge, follicular reaction, preauricular node; highly contagious

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A tender preauricular node with watery discharge is the classic viral tell — counsel on contagion precautions (fomite spread, hand hygiene, avoiding shared towels) since this is often more contagious than patients expect.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Adenovirus is the dominant cause. Presents with watery discharge, a follicular conjunctival reaction, and a tender preauricular lymph node — a triad rarely seen together in bacterial disease. Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (EKC) is markedly contagious via fomites and can cause subepithelial corneal infiltrates that produce glare and photophobia for weeks after the acute infection resolves.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, often starts unilateral then becomes bilateral within days
PainMild irritation, foreign body sensation
VisionNormal acutely; can be affected weeks later by subepithelial infiltrates (EKC)
Key exam findingWatery discharge, follicular reaction, tender preauricular node
Distinguishing testClinical; fluorescein to exclude corneal involvement
💡

Counsel patients that EKC-associated subepithelial infiltrates can cause glare/haloes for weeks to months after the red eye resolves — this is a common source of return visits if not anticipated.

Herpes Simplex Keratitis

Urgent

Dendritic corneal ulcer; steroids can convert this into a corneal-melt emergency

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Topical steroids given for undifferentiated red eye can convert HSV epithelial keratitis into a progressive stromal melt — fluorescein staining before reaching for any steroid is the single highest-yield safety step in red eye management.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Reactivation of latent HSV-1 in the trigeminal ganglion produces epithelial keratitis with a pathognomonic branching (dendritic) ulcer with terminal bulbs, best seen with fluorescein staining and cobalt blue light. Corneal sensation is often reduced (test with a wisp of cotton) — a useful and underused bedside clue, since patients with reduced corneal sensation report less pain than the exam findings would suggest.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, days
PainVariable — often less than expected due to reduced corneal sensation
VisionBlurred if visual axis involved
Key exam findingDendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs on fluorescein stain; decreased corneal sensation
Distinguishing testFluorescein stain under cobalt blue light; corneal sensation testing
💡

Never prescribe topical steroids for a red eye without a fluorescein exam first, specifically to exclude a dendrite.

💡

Recurrent HSV keratitis is a leading infectious cause of corneal blindness in the developed world — oral antiviral prophylaxis is often indicated after recurrent episodes.

Subconjunctival Hemorrhage

Routine

Painless, sharply demarcated blood under the conjunctiva; alarming appearance, benign course

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Painless with fully preserved vision and a normal pupil/cornea exam is the reassurance triad — recurrent or bilateral subconjunctival hemorrhage without trauma should prompt a blood pressure check and bleeding-disorder/anticoagulation review.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Rupture of a small conjunctival vessel (from Valsalva, coughing, straining, minor trauma, anticoagulation, or spontaneously) causes blood to pool between the conjunctiva and sclera. The sharply demarcated, uniformly red patch looks dramatic but reflects a self-limited, painless process with no visual consequence.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, noticed suddenly (often on mirror-check)
PainNone; mild irritation at most
VisionNormal
Key exam findingSharply demarcated, flat, uniformly red patch; normal pupil, cornea, vision
Distinguishing testClinical; check blood pressure and anticoagulation/antiplatelet status, INR if on warfarin
💡

Document the absence of pain, vision change, and globe tenderness explicitly — this is the chart language that protects against a missed traumatic globe injury in an after-the-fact review.

Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO)

Critical

"Stroke of the eye" — a 4.5-hour treatment window analogous to acute ischemic stroke

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Treat CRAO on the same clock as acute ischemic stroke: the field consensus 4.5-hour window for IV thrombolysis mirrors AIS protocols, tissue viability falls sharply after ~240 minutes of ischemia, and more than half of patients nationally present after the window has already closed — making time-of-onset documentation the single most important data point you collect.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Embolic or thrombotic occlusion of the central retinal artery causes acute retinal ischemia. Ganglion cell loss becomes irreversible at roughly 240 minutes of ischemia, mirroring the tissue-death timeline of cerebral stroke — which is exactly why CRAO is increasingly managed through acute stroke pathways. Fewer than 20% of patients recover meaningful vision without intervention. Classic fundus findings are retinal whitening (edema of the inner retinal layers) with a contrasting 'cherry-red spot' at the fovea, where the thin retina overlies the intact choroidal circulation.

CRAO carries the same embolic-source workup obligation as any stroke: carotid imaging, echocardiogram, and cardiac rhythm monitoring, since the peak risk of a subsequent cerebrovascular or cardiovascular event is in the first 7 days.

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden, seconds to minutes
PainNone
VisionSevere, often counting-fingers or worse, monocular
Key exam findingCherry-red spot, retinal whitening, afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)
Distinguishing testDilated fundus exam; treat as acute stroke — time-window-dependent IV thrombolysis per current AHA/AAO protocols where available
Schematic fundus: cherry-red foveal spot against diffusely whitened, edematous retina
💡

The fundus can appear deceptively normal in the first hours in a small but real minority of cases — a normal-appearing fundus early on does not exclude CRAO if the RAPD and history fit.

💡

Ophthalmology involvement is often not available within the treatment window in many EDs; CRAO increasingly routes through the acute stroke/telestroke pathway for this reason. Confirm your institution's current pathway and door-to-needle expectations rather than assuming ophthalmology will drive timing.

💡

GCA is a critical alternate diagnosis to exclude in any patient over 50 with acute monocular vision loss (see Sudden Painful Vision Loss / arteritic ischemic optic neuropathy) — missing it risks bilateral, irreversible blindness.

Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO)

Urgent

"Blood and thunder" fundus — diffuse hemorrhage in all four quadrants

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Diffuse hemorrhages in all four retinal quadrants ("blood and thunder") distinguishes CRVO from the localized wedge-shaped hemorrhage of a branch occlusion and from the pale, cherry-red-spot picture of arterial occlusion.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Thrombosis of the central retinal vein, typically at the lamina cribrosa where the vein shares a common adventitial sheath with the central retinal artery, causes venous congestion, capillary hemorrhage, and macular edema. Risk factors mirror venous thromboembolism risk broadly: hypertension, hyperviscosity states, glaucoma, and age. Vision loss is typically less abrupt and less severe than CRAO, though severe (ischemic) CRVO can progress to neovascular glaucoma weeks later.

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden to subacute, over hours
PainNone (unless neovascular glaucoma develops later)
VisionVariable, mild to severe depending on ischemic vs non-ischemic type
Key exam findingDiffuse retinal hemorrhages in all four quadrants ('blood and thunder' fundus), venous dilation and tortuosity, disc edema
Distinguishing testDilated fundus exam; fluorescein angiography (ophthalmology) to grade ischemic vs non-ischemic
💡

Ischemic CRVO carries meaningful risk of neovascular glaucoma at roughly 60–90 days post-event ('90-day glaucoma') — ensure follow-up is scheduled, not left to patient-initiated return.

Retinal Detachment (Rhegmatogenous)

Critical

Curtain/shadow progressing across the visual field, preceded by flashes and floaters

Hallmark / Bottom Line

"Macula-on" detachment is a same-day surgical emergency where hours matter for visual prognosis; "macula-off" still needs urgent repair but the emergent time pressure is lower since central vision is already lost — asking specifically whether central vision is still intact helps triage urgency over the phone.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A retinal tear (often from posterior vitreous detachment traction) allows liquefied vitreous to pass through and separate the neurosensory retina from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium. Vision loss corresponds anatomically to detached retina: a superior detachment causes an inferior visual field defect (curtain rising from below) and vice versa, since the retina projects an inverted image. Macula-off detachment (central vision lost) versus macula-on (peripheral field only) is the single biggest prognostic branch point, since visual outcome after repair correlates strongly with how long the macula was detached.

Quick Reference

OnsetProgressive over hours to days, often after preceding flashes/floaters
PainNone
VisionProgressive field loss described as a curtain or shadow; central vision preserved until macula involved
Key exam findingPeripheral field defect corresponding to detached area; dilated fundus shows undulating, elevated, out-of-focus retina
Distinguishing testDilated fundus exam; bedside ocular ultrasound (B-scan) if the view is obscured by hemorrhage
💡

New posterior vitreous detachment symptoms (flashes, floaters) without a definitive detachment yet still warrants dilated exam within 24 hours, since roughly 10–15% of symptomatic PVD patients are found to have a retinal tear.

💡

Point-of-care ocular ultrasound has good sensitivity for detachment when the fundus view is obscured (e.g., vitreous hemorrhage) — look for a hyperechoic, mobile membrane that remains attached at the optic disc.

Vitreous Hemorrhage

Urgent

Sudden floaters/haze from bleeding into the vitreous cavity; obscures the view needed to find the cause

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Vitreous hemorrhage is a sign, not a final diagnosis — the priority is ruling out an underlying retinal tear or detachment with ultrasound, since missing the underlying cause is the actual threat to vision.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Bleeding into the vitreous cavity most commonly arises from proliferative diabetic retinopathy neovascularization, retinal tear/detachment, or trauma. Patients describe a shower of floaters, cobwebs, or a dark haze rather than a discrete curtain. The diagnostic challenge is that the hemorrhage itself obscures the fundus view needed to find the underlying cause (tear, detachment, proliferative retinopathy).

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden
PainNone
VisionVariable — floaters/haze to near-complete loss depending on hemorrhage volume
Key exam findingLoss of red reflex, hemorrhage obscuring fundus detail
Distinguishing testB-scan ultrasound to evaluate for underlying retinal tear/detachment when the view is poor
💡

Advise against strenuous activity, heavy lifting, and blood thinners review pending ophthalmology evaluation, as gravity-dependent settling can occasionally improve the view for exam within days.

Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (Non-arteritic, NAION)

Urgent

Painless monocular vision loss with a swollen disc; the 'wake-up' stroke of the optic nerve

Hallmark / Bottom Line

The single most important task at the bedside is separating this non-arteritic form from arteritic (GCA-related) ischemic optic neuropathy — send ESR, CRP, and platelets on every patient over 50 with this presentation, because missing GCA risks bilateral blindness within days.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Hypoperfusion of the posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head, typically in patients with a 'disc at risk' (a small, crowded optic disc with little to no physiologic cup) and vasculopathic risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnea, nocturnal hypotension). Vision loss is classically noticed on waking. Unlike CRAO, the fundus shows a swollen, hyperemic disc rather than retinal whitening, since the infarct is confined to the nerve head itself.

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden, often noticed on waking
PainNone
VisionModerate to severe, often altitudinal (upper or lower half) field loss
Key exam findingSwollen, hyperemic optic disc; RAPD; small/crowded contralateral disc
Distinguishing testESR/CRP/platelets to exclude arteritic (GCA) cause — this distinction changes management entirely
💡

There is no proven acute treatment for NAION (unlike CRAO/stroke pathways) — the emergency-department task is diagnostic triage (excluding GCA) and risk-factor identification, not acute reperfusion therapy.

💡

Ask about sleep apnea and nocturnal hypotensive medication dosing — both are modifiable risk factors for fellow-eye involvement.

Retinal Migraine vs. Amaurosis Fugax

Urgent

Transient monocular vision loss — treat as a TIA-equivalent until proven otherwise

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Transient monocular vision loss gets the same vascular workup as a hemispheric TIA regardless of how classic the story sounds for 'ocular migraine' — the diagnosis of retinal migraine should only be made after that workup is negative, not before.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Transient monocular vision loss (classically described as a curtain descending and then resolving over seconds to minutes) is most often embolic (amaurosis fugax, from carotid or cardioembolic source) and should be worked up exactly like a TIA. True retinal migraine (vasospasm) is a diagnosis of exclusion and much less common than the label suggests in clinical practice — the two are frequently conflated, and defaulting to the migraine label without a vascular workup is a recognized source of missed strokes.

Quick Reference

OnsetTransient, seconds to minutes, fully resolved by the time of exam
PainNone (headache may follow in true migraine, but is not required)
VisionNormal at time of exam by definition
Key exam findingNormal exam is expected; look for retinal emboli (Hollenhorst plaques) if seen during the event or shortly after
Distinguishing testCarotid imaging, echocardiogram, ECG/rhythm monitoring — the same stroke-source workup as any TIA
💡

A normal exam at time of ED presentation is expected and does not exclude a vascular cause — don't let a reassuring exam stop the source workup.

Bilateral Occipital (Cortical) Vision Loss

Critical

Normal-appearing eyes with lost vision — the lesion is behind the eyes entirely

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A completely normal pupil and fundus exam in a patient reporting severe bilateral vision loss is not reassuring — it is the specific pattern that should trigger emergent brain imaging rather than an ophthalmology referral.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Bilateral occipital lobe ischemia (posterior cerebral artery territory stroke, often from a top-of-basilar or vertebrobasilar process) or, less commonly, PRES (posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome) causes cortical blindness with an entirely normal-appearing eye exam — normal pupils (since the reflex arc is intact), normal fundus, no RAPD. Patients may have Anton syndrome (denial of blindness) in severe cases, which can mislead the history if taken at face value.

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden
PainNone (headache may accompany PRES)
VisionSevere, bilateral, often complete
Key exam findingEntirely normal pupil exam and fundus — the mismatch between severe vision loss and a normal eye exam IS the finding
Distinguishing testEmergent CT/MRI brain; blood pressure review if PRES suspected
💡

Anton syndrome (cortical blindness with confabulated denial of the deficit) can make the history sound falsely reassuring — a mismatch between the patient's stated confidence and their functional behavior (bumping into objects) is a clue.

Orbital Compartment Syndrome (Retrobulbar Hemorrhage)

Critical

The orbit is a closed compartment — bleeding behind the eye compresses the optic nerve directly

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Proptosis + RAPD + IOP >40 in the setting of trauma or a recent orbital/eyelid procedure is an indication for immediate bedside lateral canthotomy and cantholysis — a procedure performed with laceration-tray instruments already in the room, not held for ophthalmology or CT.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Blood or edema accumulating in the fixed bony orbit (most often after blunt trauma, but also post-surgical, post-blepharoplasty, or even after prolonged prone positioning or vigorous Valsalva) raises intraorbital pressure with nowhere to expand. Rising pressure compresses the optic nerve and its blood supply directly, and IOP rises secondarily as the globe is pushed against a taut orbital septum. This is a clinical diagnosis — treatment should not wait for CT confirmation when the exam is diagnostic.

Blindness rates in untreated or delayed retrobulbar hemorrhage are reported around 44–52%; successful decompression is time-sensitive, with best outcomes when performed within roughly 60–120 minutes of symptom onset.

Quick Reference

OnsetRapid, minutes to a few hours post-trauma/procedure
PainSevere, tense, worsening
VisionProgressively decreasing, may reach light-perception only
Key exam findingProptosis, tense/resistant globe on palpation, ophthalmoplegia, RAPD, IOP often >40 mmHg
Distinguishing testTonometry + clinical exam; do not delay treatment for imaging if exam is diagnostic
Schematic: retrobulbar hemorrhage within the closed bony orbit compressing the globe and optic nerve
💡

The only real contraindication to canthotomy is suspected open globe — check for a positive Seidel sign, irregular pupil, or hyphema first.

💡

Document IOP numerically before and after canthotomy; a drop toward normal with visual improvement is your confirmation the diagnosis and treatment were correct.

💡

This diagnosis has been reported without trauma too (post-CPR, prone positioning) — keep it on the differential for unexplained proptosis and vision loss in any setting.

Arteritic Ischemic Optic Neuropathy (Giant Cell Arteritis)

Critical

Vision loss from GCA is preventable with same-day steroids — and can become bilateral within days if missed

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Start high-dose corticosteroids on clinical suspicion alone — before ESR/CRP results, before biopsy — because the fellow eye can be involved within days if treatment is delayed, and the second eye's vision loss is what medicolegal reviews focus on when GCA is missed.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Giant cell arteritis is a large/medium-vessel vasculitis in patients typically over 50 that can occlude the posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head, causing sudden, severe, often profound monocular vision loss. Unlike the non-arteritic form, this is frequently accompanied by systemic symptoms: new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, and polymyalgia rheumatica symptoms — though isolated ocular presentations without systemic symptoms do occur and are a recognized diagnostic blind spot.

ESR and CRP are both useful but imperfect: concordant elevation of ESR and CRP yields very high sensitivity for a positive temporal artery biopsy, but a meaningful minority of biopsy-proven cases have been reported with normal inflammatory markers, so a normal ESR/CRP does not fully exclude the diagnosis if clinical suspicion is high. Thrombocytosis further supports the diagnosis and has been shown to help predict a positive biopsy.

Quick Reference

OnsetSudden, over minutes to hours; may have preceding amaurosis fugax episodes
PainMay have scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, headache (ocular pain itself is variable)
VisionSevere, often profound (counting fingers or worse)
Key exam findingPale, swollen optic disc; RAPD; temporal artery tenderness/decreased pulse in some
Distinguishing testSTAT ESR, CRP, platelets, CBC; temporal artery biopsy or ultrasound (halo sign) for confirmation — but do not wait for biopsy to treat
💡

Ask specifically about jaw claudication (pain with chewing that improves with rest) and scalp tenderness (pain brushing hair) — patients rarely volunteer these unless asked directly.

💡

Any patient over 50 with acute monocular vision loss should get ESR/CRP/platelets sent as part of the initial ED workup, regardless of how confidently the presentation looks like 'routine' NAION or CRAO — occult/isolated ocular GCA without systemic symptoms is a documented blind spot even in academic centers.

💡

Treatment is empiric high-dose corticosteroids started immediately on clinical suspicion; do not delay for biopsy or even for lab confirmation.

Optic Neuritis

Urgent

Pain with eye movement + RAPD in a young adult — the classic first presentation of MS

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pain with eye movement plus a normal-appearing fundus in a young adult is the classic retrobulbar optic neuritis picture — the absence of visible disc swelling should not be used to argue against the diagnosis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Demyelinating inflammation of the optic nerve is the most common cause of unilateral painful vision loss in the US, and is the initial presentation of multiple sclerosis in roughly 1 in 5 patients who go on to develop MS. Pain, especially with eye movement, is present in the large majority of patients and classically precedes or accompanies the vision loss by a few days; it typically resolves within a week even as visual loss continues to evolve. Vision loss develops over hours to days and stabilizes over 1–2 weeks, with the large majority of patients beginning spontaneous improvement within three weeks regardless of treatment.

The fundus exam is frequently normal (retrobulbar neuritis, inflammation posterior to the disc) — a normal-appearing optic disc does not exclude optic neuritis, which surprises many trainees expecting to see disc swelling.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, over hours to days
PainPresent in the large majority (~90%); worse with eye movement; typically resolves within a week
VisionVariable, mild to severe; color vision (dyschromatopsia) and contrast sensitivity often disproportionately affected
Key exam findingRAPD; disc may be swollen (papillitis, ~1/3) or entirely normal (retrobulbar, ~2/3); reduced color vision
Distinguishing testMRI brain and orbits with gadolinium — both confirms the diagnosis and is the strongest predictor of subsequent MS risk
💡

Baseline brain MRI lesion burden is the strongest predictor of MS conversion; risk is substantially higher with one or more lesions versus none, and this discussion belongs in the ED-to-neurology handoff, not deferred entirely to outpatient follow-up.

💡

IV corticosteroids speed visual recovery but do not change the final visual outcome or long-term MS risk trajectory — set expectations accordingly when discussing treatment with patients.

💡

If vision fails to show any improvement within about 5 weeks, the diagnosis of typical demyelinating optic neuritis should be reconsidered (compressive lesion, NMOSD, MOGAD, sarcoid, infectious causes).

Microbial Keratitis with Vision-Threatening Infiltrate

Urgent

A central corneal infiltrate is a same-day ophthalmology referral, not a routine conjunctivitis

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Any contact lens wearer with a painful red eye needs the cornea specifically examined for an infiltrate before being treated as simple conjunctivitis — empiric antibiotic drops without excluding a vision-threatening ulcer is a recognized source of delayed diagnosis and vision loss.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Bacterial (especially Pseudomonas in contact lens wearers), fungal, or acanthamoeba keratitis can produce a corneal stromal infiltrate that is painful, vision-threatening if it involves the visual axis, and can progress to perforation if untreated. Cataract

Routine

Progressive lens opacification; the most common cause of gradual, treatable vision loss worldwide

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Progressive glare and halos around lights, worse at night, in a patient with a visibly opacified lens on exam is the classic cataract triad — distinguish from glaucoma, where visual field loss (not glare) predominates and the lens is often clear.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Age-related denaturation and aggregation of lens crystallin proteins progressively cloud the normally transparent lens, scattering light and reducing image quality. Nuclear sclerotic, cortical, and posterior subcapsular subtypes have distinct patterns and risk factors (posterior subcapsular cataracts are classically accelerated by chronic corticosteroid use and are covered elsewhere in this atlas under steroid cautions).

PainNone
VisionProgressive blur, glare (especially night driving), reduced contrast sensitivity
Key exam findingVisible lens opacity on slit lamp/red reflex exam; loss of red reflex clarity
Distinguishing testSlit lamp exam; formal visual acuity testing
💡

Cataract surgery is one of the most effective and commonly performed procedures in medicine; a visually significant cataract with no other complicating diagnosis is not an urgent ED problem but does warrant ophthalmology referral for surgical evaluation.

💡

Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma

Urgent

The 'silent thief of sight' — asymptomatic until irreversible peripheral field loss is already advanced

Hallmark / Bottom Line

This is the one diagnosis on this list where the absence of symptoms is itself the danger — by the time a patient notices a field defect, a substantial proportion of optic nerve fibers have typically already been lost, which is why population screening (not symptom-triggered evaluation) is how this disease is actually caught early.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Progressive optic nerve damage, typically (though not always) associated with elevated IOP from increased trabecular meshwork outflow resistance, causes slow, painless peripheral visual field loss that patients characteristically don't notice until it's far advanced, since central vision and each eye's field independently compensate for the other's blind spots for a long time. Unlike angle closure, the anterior chamber angle is open and anatomically normal.

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual, years
PainNone
VisionPeripheral field loss, classically asymptomatic until advanced; central vision preserved until late
Key exam findingIncreased cup-to-disc ratio, disc rim thinning/notching, elevated IOP (though can be normal-tension)
Distinguishing testTonometry, formal visual field testing, optic nerve imaging (OCT)
💡

Normal-tension glaucoma (optic nerve damage and field loss with statistically normal IOP) is a recognized entity — a normal pressure reading does not fully exclude glaucomatous damage if the disc/field findings fit.

💡

First-degree relatives of glaucoma patients have meaningfully elevated risk and should be specifically counseled toward regular screening exams.

Diabetic Retinopathy

Urgent

Progressive retinal microvascular damage; the leading cause of blindness in working-age adults

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Diabetic macular edema, not the more dramatically-named proliferative disease, is the more common overall cause of vision loss in diabetic patients — don't equate 'no neovascularization' with 'no vision-threatening disease.'

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Chronic hyperglycemia damages retinal capillary walls, causing microaneurysms, hemorrhages, and progressive capillary non-perfusion (non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy). As ischemia worsens, VEGF-driven neovascularization develops (proliferative diabetic retinopathy), risking vitreous hemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment. Diabetic macular edema, from breakdown of the blood-retinal barrier, can cause central vision loss at any stage of the disease and is actually the more common overall cause of vision loss in diabetics than the proliferative complications.

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual, over years of diabetes duration; can accelerate rapidly in pregnancy or with rapid glycemic correction
PainNone
VisionGradual blur (macular edema) or sudden loss (vitreous hemorrhage/detachment from proliferative disease)
Key exam findingMicroaneurysms, dot-blot hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates; neovascularization at the disc or elsewhere in proliferative disease
Distinguishing testDilated fundus exam; OCT for macular edema; fluorescein angiography for perfusion status
💡

Annual dilated eye exams are recommended for essentially all diabetic patients starting at diagnosis (type 2) or a few years after diagnosis (type 1), regardless of symptoms, given how much retinopathy can progress silently.

💡

Age-Related Macular Degeneration (Dry/Non-Neovascular)

Routine

Hallmark / Bottom Line

New-onset metamorphopsia (straight lines appearing wavy on an Amsler grid) or a sudden change in a previously stable dry AMD patient should raise concern for conversion to wet AMD — this is the trigger for urgent (same-day-to-next-day) ophthalmology evaluation, not routine follow-up.

Accumulation of drusen (extracellular deposits beneath the retinal pigment epithelium) and progressive RPE/photoreceptor atrophy in the macula cause gradual central vision decline while peripheral vision remains intact. This is the more common (roughly 80-90%) subtype of AMD overall, though the neovascular ('wet') subtype accounts for the majority of severe vision loss from AMD when it develops, and can convert from dry AMD at any point, presenting acutely (cross-referenced in the Geriatric special-population entry, Vol 6).

Compressive Optic Neuropathy (Tumor)

Urgent

A mass along the visual pathway causing progressive, often initially subtle, vision loss

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A bitemporal visual field defect is a chiasmal-lesion pattern until proven otherwise and should prompt MRI — this specific field pattern is one of the most anatomically localizing findings in all of neuro-ophthalmology.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A slowly enlarging mass (pituitary adenoma, meningioma, or other intracranial/orbital tumor) compresses the optic nerve, chiasm, or tract, causing gradual, painless vision loss with a characteristic visual field pattern depending on the exact location (e.g., bitemporal hemianopia with chiasmal compression from a pituitary lesion). Optic disc pallor develops over time as axons are lost, distinguishing chronic compressive damage from the swollen disc appearance of acute processes elsewhere in this atlas.

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual, months, sometimes with a stepwise or plateauing pattern
PainUsually absent, though headache can accompany a pituitary or other mass lesion

💡

Thyroid Eye Disease (Graves Orbitopathy)

Urgent

Autoimmune orbital inflammation and muscle enlargement; ptosis is NOT a feature and should prompt an MG workup instead

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Ptosis is NOT a feature of thyroid eye disease (lid RETRACTION is the classic finding instead) — any ptosis in a patient with known or suspected TED should prompt evaluation for coexisting myasthenia gravis, a well-documented overlap given both are autoimmune conditions with a recognized association.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

An autoimmune process (most often associated with Graves hyperthyroidism, though it can occur with Hashimoto's or in euthyroid patients) causes orbital fibroblast activation, glycosaminoglycan deposition, and extraocular muscle enlargement, producing proptosis, lid retraction, restrictive strabismus, and diplopia. The inferior and medial rectus muscles are most commonly and severely affected, classically restricting upgaze and abduction.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute to chronic, over weeks to months
PainMild orbital ache/pressure, worse with eye movement in active disease
VisionDiplopia from restrictive strabismus; can threaten vision via compressive optic neuropathy in severe cases
Key exam findingProptosis, lid retraction (scleral show), restricted upgaze/abduction, conjunctival injection over the involved rectus muscle insertions
Distinguishing testThyroid function tests and TSH-receptor antibody; orbital CT/MRI showing enlarged extraocular muscles (tendon-sparing, a key distinguishing imaging feature)
💡

Teprotumumab (an IGF-1 receptor-targeting monoclonal antibody) has trial-level evidence for reducing proptosis and improving diplopia in moderate-to-severe active disease, representing a meaningful shift from older steroid/radiotherapy-centered treatment paradigms.

💡

Compressive optic neuropathy from severe muscle enlargement at the orbital apex is the vision-threatening complication to watch for — assess color vision and look for an RAPD in any TED patient with worsening symptoms.

Myasthenia Gravis (Ocular)

Urgent

Fatigable, fluctuating diplopia and ptosis — the great mimic that can look like almost any other cause of binocular diplopia on this list

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Any diplopia or ptosis pattern that seems to 'not quite fit' a clean single cranial nerve palsy, or that clearly fluctuates in severity between exams or through the day, should raise myasthenia gravis — the ice pack test is a genuinely useful, low-cost bedside screen (cooling improves neuromuscular transmission, transiently improving ptosis in true MG).

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Autoimmune antibodies (most often anti-acetylcholine receptor, sometimes anti-MuSK, and occasionally seronegative for both) impair neuromuscular transmission at the junction, causing fatigable weakness. Ocular myasthenia can mimic virtually any pattern of diplopia, including individual or combined cranial nerve palsy patterns, which is why fatigability and fluctuation (better in the morning, worse with sustained effort or as the day progresses) are the key clinical clues rather than any single fixed pattern.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, fluctuating; classically worse with fatigue/sustained use, better with rest
PainNone
VisionDiplopia, ptosis (often asymmetric and variable in degree between exams)
Key exam findingFatigable ptosis (worsens with sustained upgaze), variable/inconsistent ocular misalignment between exams, positive ice pack test (improvement of ptosis after ice application)
Distinguishing testIce pack test (bedside), acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies, repetitive nerve stimulation/single-fiber EMG
💡

MG and thyroid eye disease can coexist in the same patient given their shared autoimmune substrate — a diplopia/ptosis picture that doesn't cleanly fit either diagnosis alone should prompt considering both together.

💡

Pupils are always spared in myasthenia gravis (a purely neuromuscular junction disease affecting skeletal muscle, not the autonomically-controlled pupil) — pupil involvement points away from MG and toward a different diagnosis.

Internuclear Ophthalmoplegia (INO)

Urgent

A brainstem, not a muscle or nerve, lesion — impaired adduction on the affected side with nystagmus of the abducting fellow eye

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Preserved convergence in the face of impaired adduction on lateral gaze is the key bedside discriminator that this is an internuclear (brainstem tract) problem rather than a medial rectus muscle or CN III nerve palsy, since convergence is mediated by a separate pathway that bypasses the MLF.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), the brainstem white-matter tract coordinating conjugate horizontal gaze between the two eyes, impairs adduction of the eye on the side of the lesion when looking toward the opposite side, with nystagmus of the contralateral (abducting) eye. Multiple sclerosis is the classic cause in younger patients (often bilateral INO), while brainstem stroke is the more common cause in older patients (typically unilateral).

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute (stroke) or subacute (demyelinating)
PainNone
VisionDiplopia on lateral gaze; convergence is typically preserved (distinguishing a true INO from a medial rectus palsy, since convergence uses a different pathway)
Key exam findingImpaired adduction of one eye on lateral gaze toward the opposite side, with nystagmus of the fellow (abducting) eye
Distinguishing testPreserved convergence supports INO over a medial rectus/CN III problem; MRI brain (brainstem-focused) for definitive localization
💡

Bilateral INO in a young patient is a classic MS presentation and should prompt the same MRI-with-gadolinium workup discussed for optic neuritis (Vol 4), given the shared demyelinating substrate.

💡

Unilateral INO in an older patient with vascular risk factors points toward a small brainstem stroke — this is a localizing finding worth an urgent stroke-pathway MRI in that population.

Routine

Keratoconus and other corneal surface irregularities can split a single image into two

Lens Subluxation/Dislocation

Urgent

A partially dislocated lens can present an edge into the visual axis, splitting the image

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Iridodonesis/phacodonesis (a visibly tremulous iris or lens with saccadic eye movement) is the exam finding that should prompt you to look specifically for a subluxated lens edge crossing the visual axis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Weakness or disruption of the zonular fibers suspending the lens (from trauma, or connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome or homocystinuria) allows partial lens displacement. Adie's Tonic Pupil

Routine

A benign, denervated pupil with a classic light-near dissociation and slow, tonic redilation

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Light-near dissociation (poor light reaction, better/slower near reaction) plus denervation supersensitivity to dilute pilocarpine is the confirmatory pattern — this is a benign, non-emergent diagnosis once confirmed, unlike the mydriasis patterns above.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Damage to the ciliary ganglion or short posterior ciliary nerves (often idiopathic, sometimes post-viral) denervates the iris sphincter and ciliary muscle, causing a large pupil with minimal or no direct/consensual light reaction but a relatively preserved (though slow, 'tonic') near response — the classic light-near dissociation. Aberrant nerve regeneration over time causes segmental, vermiform iris constriction visible at the slit lamp, and a supersensitivity to dilute pilocarpine.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute onset noticed by the patient, though the underlying process is a chronic denervation
PainNone; may have associated absent deep tendon reflexes (Adie syndrome, when combined with this finding)
VisionBlurred near vision from impaired accommodation; photophobia from poor pupillary constriction
Key exam findingLarge pupil, poor/absent direct and consensual light reaction, slow tonic constriction to a near target, segmental vermiform iris movement on slit lamp
Distinguishing testDilute pilocarpine (0.1%) instilled in both eyes: the Adie pupil constricts (denervation supersensitivity) while the normal pupil does not

Idiopathic Adie's pupil is far more common in young women and is generally a benign, isolated finding requiring no emergent workup once confirmed pharmacologically.

💡

Bilateral, progressive Adie's pupils with absent deep tendon reflexes constitutes Holmes-Adie syndrome, a recognized (still generally benign) clinical entity.

Direct pharmacologic blockade of the iris sphincter (anticholinergic exposure, whether intentional eye drops, accidental exposure to a scopolamine patch or plant alkaloid, or systemic anticholinergic medication) or sympathomimetic stimulation causes a markedly dilated, essentially non-reactive pupil, often out of proportion to any other neurologic finding.

Ask specifically and non-judgmentally about accidental exposure: handling scopolamine patches then touching the eye, contact with jimsonweed/other anticholinergic plants, or use of nebulized ipratropium with a poorly-fitted mask are all classic real-world scenarios.

💡

The pharmacologic testing gradient (fails to constrict to pilocarpine = pharmacologic block; constricts to dilute 0.1% = Adie's; constricts only to standard-strength = CN III or normal) is the key stepwise tool for sorting these entities when the history is unclear.

Flashes and Floaters

New flashes and floaters deserve a dilated exam within 24 hours even when they turn out to be benign PVD — the entire point of urgency is catching the roughly 10-15% of these patients who actually have a retinal tear underneath the symptom.

Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD)

Urgent

The vitreous gel separates from the retina — usually benign, but the reason for the urgent exam is what might be hiding underneath

Hallmark / Bottom Line

New, acute flashes and floaters warrant a dilated exam within 24 hours regardless of how classically benign the presentation looks — roughly 10-15% of these patients are found to have an actual retinal tear on that exam, which is the entire point of the urgency.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Age-related liquefaction and shrinkage of the vitreous gel causes it to separate from its attachment to the retina, typically starting posteriorly. Traction on the retina during this separation causes photopsias (flashes, from mechanical stimulation of photoreceptors), while a newly mobile, often ring-shaped condensation of collagen (a Weiss ring, corresponding to the former peripapillary vitreous attachment) is perceived as a floater. The process itself is a normal part of aging and, in isolation, requires no treatment — the reason for urgency is that vitreous traction during PVD can cause a retinal tear in a meaningful minority of patients.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute onset of symptoms, reflecting a chronic underlying aging process reaching a symptomatic point
PainNone
VisionNew flashes (especially in peripheral vision, worse in dim light) and floaters (often described as a cobweb, ring, or spider); acuity typically preserved unless a complication has developed
Key exam findingWeiss ring visible on dilated exam; no retinal break or detachment if uncomplicated
Distinguishing testDilated fundus exam with scleral depression to examine the peripheral retina to the ora serrata
💡

A shower of many new floaters, or floaters accompanied by a curtain/shadow in the peripheral vision, should raise concern for an already-developed retinal tear or detachment rather than uncomplicated PVD — see Vol 1's Retinal Detachment entry.

💡

PVD is nearly universal by later life; the goal of the urgent exam isn't to prevent PVD itself but to catch the minority with a coexisting, treatable retinal tear before it progresses to detachment.

Migraine with Aura (Visual/Ophthalmic Migraine)

Routine

Positive, expanding visual phenomena (not simple flashes) that evolve over 20-30 minutes and typically resolve completely

Hallmark / Bottom Line

True migrainous visual aura is BINOCULAR (present in both eyes when each is tested individually, since the phenomenon originates in the cortex, downstream of where the two eyes' inputs have already combined) and evolves gradually over 20-30 minutes — both features distinguish it from the sudden, monocular flashes of PVD/retinal traction.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Cortical spreading depression, a wave of neuronal/glial depolarization followed by suppressed activity moving across the occipital cortex, produces a characteristic visual aura: a scintillating, expanding arc or zigzag pattern (fortification spectrum), often with a central scotoma, that typically builds over 20-30 minutes and then resolves, sometimes followed by headache (though can occur without headache, particularly in patients with prior typical migraine history).

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, with a characteristic gradual build/march over 20-30 minutes
PainMay be followed by headache, or can occur in isolation (acephalgic migraine)
VisionPositive visual phenomena: scintillating zigzag/fortification pattern, expanding scotoma; BINOCULAR (present in both eyes' fields when tested individually, unlike a retinal-origin phenomenon)
Key exam findingEntirely normal eye exam during and after the episode
Distinguishing testClinical, based on the characteristic evolving pattern and binocularity; test each eye individually during the episode if possible to confirm binocular (cortical) origin
💡

New visual aura in a patient over 50, or aura without any personal/family migraine history, still deserves a broader workup to exclude an alternative cause (TIA, occipital lesion) rather than being assumed migrainous by pattern alone in a first-time presentation.

💡

Testing each eye individually during an active episode is a genuinely useful bedside maneuver: a binocular phenomenon confirms cortical origin, while a monocular phenomenon should be worked up as an ocular/retinal cause instead.

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Inflammation involving the vitreous and/or posterior segment (from infectious, autoimmune, or idiopathic causes) introduces inflammatory cells into the vitreous cavity, perceived as floaters, often with an insidious onset and frequently accompanied by blurred vision from vitreous haze or associated retinal/choroidal involvement, distinct from the sudden, mechanical-traction-driven floaters of PVD.

Distinguishing testDilated exam to characterize vitreous cell/haze grade and search for a causative retinal/choroidal lesion; directed infectious/autoimmune workup based on exam pattern and risk factors

Ptosis

New, acute ptosis is a cranial nerve or neuromuscular junction problem until proven otherwise — the job is sorting mechanical/aponeurotic causes (common, benign) from the neurogenic causes (CN III, Horner's, myasthenia) that carry real stakes.

Aponeurotic (Involutional) Ptosis

Routine

Age-related stretching/dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis — the most common cause of ptosis overall

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Normal pupil, normal eye movements, and preserved levator function (the lid still moves well, just starts from a lower resting position) together confirm a mechanical/aponeurotic cause rather than a neurogenic one — this triad is the reassurance pattern for this specific diagnosis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Age-related, or sometimes contact-lens-wear or prior-surgery-related, stretching or thinning of the levator aponeurosis (the tendon connecting the levator muscle to the tarsal plate) reduces the mechanical force transmitted to lift the lid, causing gradual, painless ptosis, typically with an elevated (higher-than-normal) lid crease as the aponeurosis has stretched.

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual, over years
PainNone
VisionNormal unless the lid is low enough to obstruct the visual axis
Key exam findingElevated/high lid crease, normal levator function (good excursion on upgaze), no pupil or motility abnormality
Distinguishing testLevator function measurement; exclude neurogenic causes by confirming normal pupil and full ocular motility
💡

This is a cosmetic and sometimes visually-obstructive condition, not an emergency; surgical repair (levator advancement/plication) is elective once the diagnosis is confirmed and neurogenic causes excluded.

Congenital Ptosis

Routine

Present since birth, from a dysgenetic (poorly developed) levator muscle rather than any acquired process

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Poor levator function (in contrast to the preserved excursion of aponeurotic ptosis) plus lifelong presence confirms a congenital, dysgenetic process rather than an acquired one.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Isolated congenital ptosis results from dysgenesis (poor development) of the levator muscle itself, replaced partially by fibrous/fatty tissue, causing both a ptotic lid and characteristically POOR levator function/excursion (unlike aponeurotic ptosis, where excursion is preserved) — the lid often shows lagophthalmos on downgaze (lid lag, the opposite of what's seen with a normally functioning levator) as an additional distinguishing sign.

Quick Reference

OnsetPresent since birth
PainNone
VisionAmblyopia risk if severe enough to occlude the visual axis in early childhood
Key exam findingPoor levator excursion, lid lag on downgaze, often a normal/absent lid crease
Distinguishing testLevator function measurement; old photographs confirming lifelong presence
💡

Any congenital ptosis severe enough to occlude the visual axis needs prompt pediatric ophthalmology evaluation given amblyopia risk during the critical visual development period — this is not simply a cosmetic issue to defer in infancy.

Carotid-Cavernous Fistula

Urgent

An abnormal arteriovenous connection producing pulsatile proptosis with an audible/palpable bruit

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

An abnormal direct or indirect connection between the carotid arterial system and the cavernous sinus (from trauma, spontaneous rupture of a cavernous carotid aneurysm, or a dural arteriovenous fistula) allows high-pressure arterial blood to flow into the venous cavernous sinus and, retrograde, into the orbital venous system, causing pulsatile proptosis, chemosis, and dilated, tortuous ('corkscrew') episcleral vessels.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Trigeminovascular system activation during a migraine attack directly produces photophobia (and often phonophobia) as one of the defining diagnostic features of the migraine itself, via central sensitization of pain pathways that also process light sensitivity — this is a genuine, mechanistically-linked symptom, not an incidental accompaniment.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, during the headache episode; may begin before or persist after the headache itself
PainThrobbing/pulsatile headache, often unilateral
VisionMay include visual aura (see Flashes and Floaters section above) preceding the headache
Key exam findingNormal eye exam between and typically during episodes
Distinguishing testClinical, based on migraine diagnostic criteria; normal eye exam supports this over an ocular cause
💡

Photophobia can occasionally precede or persist after the headache component, or occur with an atypical/absent headache — a known personal migraine history is helpful context, but new presentations still deserve the same normal-exam confirmation before attributing symptoms to migraine.

Corneal Scar (Post-Infectious or Post-Traumatic)

Routine

A quiet, chronic, well-demarcated opacity from prior injury or infection — the 'cold' end-stage of an old process

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A QUIET eye (no redness, no pain, no active epithelial defect) around a stable white corneal opacity is the reassurance pattern distinguishing an old scar from active infectious/inflammatory disease — the absence of inflammation is itself the diagnostic finding.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Prior corneal injury (infectious keratitis, trauma, or surgery) heals with disorganized collagen deposition (fibrosis) rather than the normal regular lamellar arrangement, producing a permanent, stable, well-demarcated opacity that scatters light and can cause irregular astigmatism if it involves the visual axis, but shows no active inflammation.

Quick Reference

OnsetChronic, stable over time; the causative event may be remote
PainNone (a scar itself is not painful, unlike the active process that caused it)
VisionReduced if central/visual-axis-involving; may cause irregular astigmatism
Key exam findingWell-demarcated, stable, avascular (or minimally vascularized) opacity with a QUIET surrounding eye — no injection, no epithelial defect, no anterior chamber reaction
Distinguishing testHistory of prior injury/infection; slit lamp confirms a quiet eye around the opacity, distinguishing it from active disease
💡

Corneal Edema (Acute, from Elevated IOP or Endothelial Failure)

Urgent

A diffusely hazy, 'steamy' cornea from fluid imbibition, either acute (angle closure) or chronic (endothelial failure)

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Diffuse haze without a focal infiltrate, combined with a markedly elevated IOP, points to acute angle closure (full protocol in Vol 1); the same diffuse haze pattern with normal IOP and a history of worse-in-the-morning vision points to chronic endothelial failure instead.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

The corneal endothelium normally pumps fluid out of the stroma to maintain corneal clarity (relative dehydration); either an acute IOP spike overwhelming this pump (as in acute angle-closure glaucoma) or chronic endothelial cell dysfunction/loss (Fuchs endothelial dystrophy, or endothelial decompensation after intraocular surgery) allows fluid to accumulate, producing a diffusely hazy, 'ground-glass' or 'steamy' corneal appearance rather than a focal infiltrate or scar.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute (angle closure, IOP-driven) or chronic/progressive (endothelial dystrophy/failure)
PainSignificant if acute/IOP-driven; minimal in chronic endothelial failure
VisionDiffusely hazy, worse in the morning with chronic endothelial disease
Key exam findingDiffuse corneal haze/thickening rather than a focal lesion; markedly elevated IOP if acute angle closure; guttae (endothelial cell dropout) visible on specular reflection in Fuchs dystrophy
Distinguishing testTonometry (acute cause); pachymetry/specular microscopy for chronic endothelial disease
💡

"Worse on waking, clearing through the day" is a classic historical clue for Fuchs endothelial dystrophy or other chronic endothelial failure, reflecting the overnight closed-eye fluid accumulation cycle.

Band Keratopathy

Routine

Calcium deposition in a horizontal band across the interpalpebral cornea, from chronic ocular disease or systemic calcium/phosphate abnormality

A horizontal band restricted to the interpalpebral zone, rather than a diffuse or focal process, is a distinctive pattern that should prompt consideration of an underlying chronic ocular inflammatory process or systemic calcium/phosphate disorder.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Calcium salt deposition in the superficial corneal stroma and Bowman's layer, classically in a horizontal band corresponding to the interpalpebral (exposed, between-the-lids) zone, occurs with chronic ocular inflammation (longstanding uveitis, especially juvenile idiopathic arthritis-associated uveitis), chronic glaucoma, or systemic calcium/phosphate metabolic derangement (chronic kidney disease, hyperparathyroidism).

Molluscum Contagiosum

Routine

A chronic, unilateral, treatment-resistant follicular conjunctivitis should prompt a careful search of the lid margin for a small umbilicated papule — treating the conjunctivitis without removing the underlying molluscum lesion will not resolve the eye findings.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A poxvirus infection of the skin produces small, dome-shaped, characteristically umbilicated (central dimple) papules; when lesions occur on or near the lid margin, shed viral particles can cause a chronic, unilateral follicular conjunctivitis that fails to resolve with standard antibiotic or antihistamine therapy until the causative lid lesion itself is identified and treated.

Eyelid Malignancy (Basal Cell Carcinoma and Others)

Urgent

The lesion that shouldn't be missed — a non-healing ulcer, loss of lashes, or progressive lid margin distortion in an older patient

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Madarosis (loss of eyelashes) directly over a persistent lid lesion is a specific and often underappreciated red flag for malignancy, since inflammatory/benign lesions typically don't destroy the lash follicles the way an invasive tumor does.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Basal cell carcinoma (the most common eyelid malignancy overall, classically lower-lid and medial-canthus predominant), squamous cell carcinoma, and sebaceous gland carcinoma (the important, aggressive masquerader of recurrent chalazion, discussed in Vol 3b) can all present as a persistent, slowly enlarging eyelid lesion in an older patient, often with madarosis (lash loss), ulceration, or a pearly, telangiectatic, rolled-border appearance (classic for basal cell carcinoma specifically).

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual, over months, progressive rather than resolving
PainUsually minimal/absent
VisionNormal unless the lesion is large enough to distort the lid margin/ocular surface
Key exam findingNon-healing ulcer, pearly/telangiectatic rolled border, lash loss (madarosis) over the lesion, or progressive lid margin architectural distortion
Distinguishing testBiopsy for any lesion with these red-flag features, or any lesion that fails to resolve/recurs after presumed benign treatment

💡

A type IV (delayed) hypersensitivity reaction to a topical allergen — often a preservative in eye drops, a cosmetic product, or even nail polish/hand cream transferred to the delicate periorbital skin by touch — causes localized, itchy, sometimes vesicular or scaling periorbital swelling and erythema, distinct from the conjunctival-predominant itching of allergic conjunctivitis.

Systemic Fluid Overload (Renal, Cardiac, Hepatic)

Routine

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Bilateral, painless, non-tender, non-erythematous periorbital edema that's worse in the morning and improves through the day is a systemic-disease pattern, not an infectious or allergic ocular one — this presentation should prompt a systemic review of systems rather than eye-directed treatment.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Systemic conditions causing generalized fluid retention or hypoalbuminemia (nephrotic syndrome and other renal disease, congestive heart failure, liver disease/cirrhosis) manifest early and visibly in the periorbital region given the thinness and laxity of this skin, classically worse on waking (from overnight recumbent fluid redistribution) and improving somewhat through the day as the patient becomes upright.

Urgent

Hallmark / Bottom Line

The distinctive violaceous/heliotrope color (not simply red or skin-colored edema) over the upper lids, especially with any proximal muscle weakness on directed exam, is specific enough to prompt this diagnosis and its associated systemic workup.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

An autoimmune inflammatory myopathy (dermatomyositis) produces a characteristic violaceous-to-lilac ('heliotrope') discoloration and edema of the upper eyelids, often accompanying (or preceding) Gottron's papules on the hands and proximal muscle weakness; adult-onset dermatomyositis also carries a meaningful association with underlying malignancy, particularly in older patients, making this a systemic diagnosis with real downstream workup implications.

Orbital Myositis

Urgent

Idiopathic or autoimmune inflammation of one or more extraocular muscles, causing pain that worsens specifically with movement in the direction that muscle would act

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pain that is specifically reproduced or worsened by moving the eye in one particular direction, in an otherwise relatively quiet-looking eye, should raise this diagnosis — the directional, movement-provoked quality of the pain is the key clinical clue.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Inflammation of an extraocular muscle (idiopathic orbital inflammatory syndrome/'orbital pseudotumor' localized to a muscle, or as part of thyroid eye disease, though the classically painful presentation is more typical of the idiopathic form) causes pain that is characteristically reproduced or worsened by attempting to move the eye in the direction that the inflamed muscle would normally act, often with diplopia if motility is restricted, but frequently with a relatively quiet-appearing globe surface.

PainSignificant, specifically worse with eye movement in the direction of the affected muscle's action
VisionUsually preserved unless the process is severe/compressive
Key exam findingPain and possibly restricted movement in one direction of gaze; may have mild proptosis or lid swelling; conjunctiva may show only mild injection over the affected muscle's insertion

Tendon involvement on imaging (versus the tendon-sparing pattern typical of thyroid eye disease, cross-referenced above and in Vol 4) is a useful radiologic distinguishing feature between these two causes of painful extraocular muscle enlargement.

💡

Sinusitis (Referred Pain)

Routine

Pain perceived in or around the eye from adjacent sinus inflammation, with an entirely normal eye exam

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Periorbital pain with a completely normal eye exam, in the setting of nasal congestion and sinus tenderness worse leaning forward, points toward referred sinus pain rather than any primary ocular process.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Inflammation of the paranasal sinuses (particularly the ethmoid or frontal sinuses, given their anatomic proximity to the orbit) can produce pain referred to the periorbital or retro-orbital region via shared trigeminal sensory innervation, despite the eye itself being entirely uninvolved and normal on exam.

Quick Reference

OnsetSubacute, often with a preceding upper respiratory infection
PainPressure-like, often worse leaning forward or with palpation over the affected sinus
VisionNormal
Key exam findingEntirely normal eye exam; sinus tenderness to palpation, nasal congestion/discharge

Always confirm the eye exam is truly normal (vision, pupil, motility, fundus) before attributing periorbital pain to sinusitis — this diagnosis should not be assumed reflexively, particularly if any orbital sign (proptosis, restricted movement) is present, which would instead suggest an orbital complication of the sinus disease itself.

Trigeminal Neuralgia / Herpetic Neuralgia

Routine

Neuropathic, often severe, episodic pain in the V1 (ophthalmic) distribution without any corresponding ocular surface finding

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Severe, brief, electric-shock-like pain triggered by light touch or minor stimuli, in a patient with an entirely normal eye exam, is a distinctive neuropathic pattern that should be recognized as distinct from any ocular surface or intraocular cause.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Trigeminal neuralgia (classically from vascular compression of the trigeminal nerve root, though sometimes idiopathic or demyelinating-disease-related) or post-herpetic neuralgia (following an episode of herpes zoster ophthalmicus, cross-referenced in Vol 3a) produces severe, often brief, electric-shock-like or burning pain in the V1 distribution, with a completely normal eye exam since the pain generator is neuropathic rather than ocular.

Routine

Mechanical outflow obstruction causing true overflow tearing; the adult form that, when infected, becomes dacryocystitis

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Age-related involutional stenosis of the nasolacrimal duct (the most common cause in adults), or less commonly prior trauma or chronic sinus disease, obstructs normal tear drainage into the nasal cavity, causing true overflow tearing that is NOT accompanied by surface irritation symptoms. Punctal Malposition / Ectropion

Routine

The lacrimal punctum is anatomically out of position to collect tears, regardless of normal tear production and duct patency

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A visibly everted lower lid/punctum on simple external inspection, with a fully patent lacrimal system otherwise, identifies a mechanical positioning problem rather than a drainage obstruction or overproduction issue — the fix here is surgical repositioning, not lacrimal probing.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Age-related laxity of the lower lid (involutional ectropion), scarring (cicatricial ectropion, from prior trauma, burns, or chronic skin disease), or facial nerve palsy (paralytic ectropion) causes the lower punctum to turn outward, away from the tear lake, so that it can no longer effectively collect and drain tears even though the nasolacrimal system itself is fully patent.

Quick Reference

OnsetGradual (involutional) or related to the underlying cause's timeline (cicatricial, paralytic)
PainMinimal unless associated corneal exposure develops
VisionNormal unless exposure keratopathy develops from poor lid closure
Key exam findingVisibly everted lower lid margin and punctum, away from the globe
Distinguishing testExternal inspection is usually sufficient; assess for associated lagophthalmos/corneal exposure
💡

Any new facial nerve palsy (Bell's palsy or otherwise) warrants attention to lower lid position and corneal exposure protection, given the risk of paralytic ectropion and secondary exposure keratopathy.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Misdirected eyelashes (trichiasis, from chronic blepharitis, prior trauma, or scarring conditions) or a fully inward-turned lid margin (entropion, most often age-related laxity in older adults) cause chronic mechanical irritation of the cornea and conjunctiva with each blink, triggering reflex tearing alongside foreign body sensation.

Nystagmus

New-onset nystagmus in an adult is a neurologic finding until proven otherwise; congenital nystagmus, by contrast, is a lifelong finding that should never be worked up as an acute new process.

Vestibular systemCerebellumBrainstemVisual pathway

Congenital (Infantile) Nystagmus

Routine

Present since infancy, typically horizontal and conjugate, often with a 'null point' where oscillation dampens

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Lifelong presence since infancy, confirmed by history (and ideally old photos/videos showing the characteristic head turn from a young age), is the reassurance pattern — this is fundamentally different from any new-onset nystagmus in an adult, which always deserves a fresh neurologic workup regardless of how 'benign' the oscillation pattern looks.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A developmental abnormality of ocular motor control circuitry, present from infancy (typically apparent by 2-3 months of age), produces involuntary, usually horizontal, conjugate ocular oscillation. Patients often adopt a compensatory head turn/tilt toward the 'null point,' a specific gaze position where the nystagmus amplitude dampens and vision is best. Can occur in isolation or in association with other ocular conditions (albinism, congenital cataracts, optic nerve hypoplasia) that warrant their own evaluation.

Quick Reference

OnsetPresent since infancy (by definition)
PainNone
VisionVariable, often reduced but relatively stable over time (not progressively worsening)
Key exam findingConjugate, typically horizontal nystagmus, dampening in a specific 'null point' gaze position; often a compensatory head turn
Distinguishing testHistory confirming lifelong presence (old videos/photos showing the head turn from infancy); dilated exam and further workup to exclude associated ocular conditions if not previously done
💡

Don't assume any nystagmus is 'just congenital' without confirming the lifelong history directly — new-onset nystagmus in an adult is never simply relabeled as a congenital variant without that confirmation.

Vestibular Nystagmus (Peripheral)

Urgent

New nystagmus from an inner ear/vestibular nerve problem, classically suppressed by visual fixation

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Nystagmus that is suppressed by visual fixation and improves when the patient is asked to focus on a target is the peripheral (inner ear) pattern — the opposite of central nystagmus, which is often NOT suppressed by fixation and may even be purely vertical or direction-changing.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Peripheral vestibular pathology (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, vestibular neuritis, Meniere disease) causes an imbalance in vestibular input to the ocular motor system, producing nystagmus that is classically unidirectional, horizontal-torsional, and importantly SUPPRESSED by visual fixation (better with eyes open and fixating, worse with fixation removed, as tested with Frenzel goggles or simply in darkness).

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute, often accompanied by severe vertigo, nausea/vomiting
PainNone specific to the eyes; may have associated ear fullness/hearing change (Meniere)
VisionOscillopsia (perceived visual world motion) during active nystagmus
Key exam findingUnidirectional, horizontal-torsional nystagmus, suppressed by visual fixation, with associated positive vestibular exam findings (positional testing, head impulse test)
Distinguishing testHINTS exam (Head Impulse, Nystagmus direction, Test of Skew) to help distinguish peripheral from central causes; formal vestibular testing
💡

The HINTS exam, applied by a trained examiner in the correct clinical context (acute vestibular syndrome), has been shown to outperform early MRI for distinguishing a benign peripheral cause from a dangerous central (stroke) one — a normal head impulse test with direction-changing nystagmus is actually MORE concerning for a central cause, not less, which is a commonly reversed teaching point.

Central (Brainstem/Cerebellar) Nystagmus

Critical

New nystagmus from a posterior fossa stroke, tumor, or demyelinating lesion — NOT suppressed by fixation, and often purely vertical or direction-changing

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Purely vertical nystagmus (upbeat or downbeat) or nystagmus that persists despite visual fixation are patterns that should be treated as a central, potentially stroke-related cause until neuroimaging proves otherwise — these are patterns essentially never produced by peripheral vestibular disease alone.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A lesion of the brainstem or cerebellum (ischemic stroke, hemorrhage, tumor, demyelinating disease) disrupts central ocular motor control pathways, producing nystagmus that is often purely vertical (upbeat or downbeat, patterns essentially never seen with peripheral vestibular disease), direction-changing with gaze, or NOT suppressed by visual fixation — any of these findings should prompt urgent neuroimaging.

Quick Reference

OnsetAcute (stroke) or subacute (tumor, demyelinating disease)
PainMay have associated headache or other focal neurologic symptoms
VisionOscillopsia; may have other associated visual/neurologic deficits
Key exam findingPurely vertical nystagmus, direction-changing nystagmus, or nystagmus NOT suppressed by visual fixation; often accompanied by other cerebellar/brainstem signs
Distinguishing testMRI brain, urgently, given the stroke-pathway implications of a positive central pattern on exam
💡

In the correct acute vestibular syndrome context, a normal (rather than abnormal) head impulse test combined with direction-changing nystagmus is actually the MORE worrisome combination for a central cause — a frequently inverted teaching point worth double-checking, since the intuitive assumption runs the other way.

Drug/Toxin-Induced Nystagmus

Routine

A reversible, dose-related nystagmus from a specific and recognizable list of medications/substances

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Gaze-evoked nystagmus (present or worse only in eccentric gaze, absent in primary position) accompanied by ataxia and a relevant medication/substance history points toward a toxic-metabolic cause rather than a focal structural lesion.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Certain medications (particularly anticonvulsants like phenytoin, carbamazepine, or lamotrigine at supratherapeutic levels, lithium, and sedating agents like benzodiazepines or alcohol) and toxins can produce a typically horizontal, gaze-evoked nystagmus as a dose-related side effect, reversible with dose adjustment or clearance of the causative substance.

Quick Reference

OnsetRelated to medication timing/dose changes or acute intoxication
PainNone; may have associated sedation, ataxia, or dysarthria
VisionOscillopsia, blurred vision
Key exam findingHorizontal, gaze-evoked nystagmus (worse looking to the side), often with associated ataxia/dysarthria supporting a toxic-metabolic rather than focal structural cause
Distinguishing testMedication levels (e.g., phenytoin level), toxicology screen as clinically indicated
💡

Always take a complete, specific medication history (including recent dose changes) in any new nystagmus presentation — this is a readily reversible cause that's easy to miss if the medication list isn't reviewed carefully.

When in doubt about whether post-procedural findings are expected or concerning, the safer default is same-day communication with the operating surgeon/ophthalmology rather than reassurance alone.

Retinoblastoma & Leukocoria Differential (cross-reference)

Critical

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

A white pupillary reflex in a child has a broad differential (congenital cataract, persistent fetal vasculature, Coats disease, advanced ROP, toxocariasis, coloboma), but retinoblastoma is the entity that must be excluded first given the stakes of a missed diagnosis. Full pathophysiology, the complete leukocoria differential, and the EUA-based diagnostic pathway are detailed in Vol 6.

OnsetUsually noticed by a parent, sometimes via flash photography

Congenital Cataract

Urgent

Lens opacity present from birth or early infancy — a leukocoria cause requiring urgent surgery to prevent irreversible amblyopia

Hallmark / Bottom Line

A visible lens opacity confirmed on dilated slit lamp exam (rather than a posterior retinal mass on B-scan) distinguishes this from retinoblastoma, but the urgency of referral is the same given the time-critical amblyopia window for dense congenital cataracts.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Lens opacification present from birth (from genetic causes, intrauterine infection such as congenital rubella, metabolic disease such as galactosemia, or idiopathic causes) blocks formation of a clear retinal image during the critical period of visual development, risking severe, permanent amblyopia if not addressed within a narrow treatment window, particularly for dense, unilateral cataracts.

Quick Reference

OnsetPresent from birth or noticed in early infancy
PainNone
VisionSignificantly impaired if dense and central; amblyopia risk is time-critical
Key exam findingWhite pupillary reflex/absent red reflex; visible lens opacity on exam
Distinguishing testRed reflex testing (Bruckner test) as the pediatric screening standard; urgent ophthalmology referral for confirmation and surgical planning
💡

Surgical timing is genuinely time-critical for dense unilateral congenital cataracts — delays of even weeks can meaningfully worsen the amblyopia outcome, unlike adult cataract surgery.

Neonatal Conjunctivitis (cross-reference)

Critical

Fully detailed in Vol 6 — timing after birth (chemical <24h, gonococcal 2-5 days, chlamydial 5-14 days) is the fastest bedside triage tool

Hallmark / Bottom Line

See the full Vol 6 entry — flagged here because red eye in a neonate is never treated the same way as red eye in an older child or adult, given the unique organisms, systemic disease risks, and treatment requirements specific to this age group.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Conjunctivitis in the first 30 days of life, acquired from maternal genital tract organisms during delivery or from prophylaxis itself, requires urgent recognition given gonococcal disease's capacity to penetrate an intact cornea. Full pathophysiology, organism-specific systemic treatment protocols, and neonatal-specific antibiotic safety considerations are detailed in Vol 6.

Quick Reference

OnsetTiming after birth is diagnostic: <24h (chemical), 2-5 days (gonococcal), 5-14 days (chlamydial)
PainVariable, less reliably reported/assessed in a neonate
VisionAt risk with gonococcal corneal involvement specifically
Key exam findingDischarge severity and timing; corneal involvement raises urgency substantially
Distinguishing testConjunctival Gram stain/culture and chlamydial NAAT/PCR
💡

Topical therapy alone is never adequate for gonococcal or chlamydial neonatal conjunctivitis — both require systemic treatment, per Vol 6.

Congenital Nasolacrimal Duct Obstruction

Routine

The most common cause of a persistently 'goopy,' tearing neonatal eye without true infection

Hallmark / Bottom Line

Chronic tearing and mucoid discharge in an otherwise quiet, non-injected eye (rather than the more inflamed appearance of true conjunctivitis) points toward this diagnosis, which has an excellent spontaneous resolution rate and does not require the urgent workup of neonatal conjunctivitis.

Pathophysiology & Clinical Reasoning

Incomplete canalization of the distal nasolacrimal duct (most often a persistent membrane at the valve of Hasner) at birth, present in a substantial minority of newborns, causes chronic tearing and mucoid discharge from stagnant tears, often mistaken for recurrent conjunctivitis, but distinguished by the chronic, low-grade, non-inflamed nature of the discharge and the high rate of spontaneous resolution within the first year of life.

Quick Reference

OnsetPresent from the first weeks of life, chronic/persistent
PainNone; the eye is not truly red/inflamed, just tearing and mildly mucoid
VisionNormal
Key exam findingChronic tearing and mucoid discharge, WITHOUT significant conjunctival injection; mucoid reflux with gentle pressure over the lacrimal sac
Distinguishing testClinical; the relatively quiet (non-injected) conjunctiva despite chronic discharge is the key distinguishing feature from true conjunctivitis
💡

The large majority of cases resolve spontaneously by 12 months of age with conservative management (lacrimal sac massage); probing is reserved for persistent cases beyond this age.

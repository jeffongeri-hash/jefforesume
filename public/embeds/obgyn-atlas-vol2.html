Stages of Labor & Modern Labor Curves

The single biggest shift in labor management over the past decade: the goalposts for "normal" progress moved, and a meaningful share of unnecessary primary cesareans trace back to providers still practicing off the old curve.

The redefinition that matters most

Friedman's 1950s curves placed the start of active labor at 4cm. Zhang et al.'s contemporary labor curve data (>60,000 deliveries) showed cervical dilation is genuinely slower and more variable until 6cm — the point at which the rate of change reliably accelerates. The 2014 ACOG/SMFM Obstetric Care Consensus on Safe Prevention of the Primary Cesarean Delivery formally moved the active-labor threshold to 6cm. Practically: standards for active-phase progress (and arrest diagnoses) should not be applied before 6cm — a nulliparous patient sitting at 4–5cm for hours with reassuring fetal status is not, by modern definition, "failing to progress."

First Stage — Latent & Active Phase

PhaseDefinitionProlonged when
Latent phaseOnset of regular contractions to 6cm>20h (nullip) / >14h (multip), 95th percentile

Second Stage — Complete Dilation to Delivery

Contemporary ACOG guidance allows longer pushing durations than older teaching before labeling second-stage arrest, particularly with epidural analgesia in place — as a general orientation, allow at least 3 hours (nulliparous) or 2 hours (multiparous) with an epidural before considering second-stage arrest, adding an additional hour without one, always contingent on continued reassuring fetal status and evidence of descent.

Third Stage — Placental Delivery

Active management of the third stage (AMTSL) — prophylactic oxytocin at delivery of the anterior shoulder or immediately after birth, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage — is the single most effective postpartum hemorrhage prevention strategy available and should be routine, not reserved for high-risk patients (Module 6).

Fourth Stage — Immediate Recovery

The first 1–2 hours postpartum, when the overwhelming majority of postpartum hemorrhage occurs. This is the window for frequent fundal checks, vital sign monitoring, and the period where a "the patient looked fine five minutes ago" atony bleed can turn genuinely dangerous fast.

Induction of Labor

Cervical readiness, the mechanics of ripening and induction, and the trial that changed how 39-week induction is counseled.

Bishop Score

A composite of cervical dilation, effacement, station, consistency, and position, scored 0–13. A score ≥8 predicts a vaginal delivery success rate with induction similar to spontaneous labor; lower scores predict a higher likelihood of needing cervical ripening before oxytocin will be effective.

Cervical Ripening & Induction Methods

  • Mechanical — Foley bulb or double-balloon catheter; effective, avoids the tachysystole risk of prostaglandins, and can be combined with oxytocin.
  • Prostaglandins — misoprostol (PGE1, off-label but extensively studied and widely used) or dinoprostone (PGE2); effective for ripening an unfavorable cervix, carry tachysystole risk, and misoprostol is contraindicated with a prior uterine scar due to rupture risk.
  • Oxytocin — the mainstay for active induction/augmentation once the cervix is favorable or ripening is underway; titrated to adequate contraction pattern with continuous FHR monitoring (Module 3).
  • Amniotomy — artificial rupture of membranes, often combined with oxytocin; carries cord prolapse risk if the presenting part isn't engaged (Module 6).

The ARRIVE Trial — Elective 39-Week Induction Tier A

Grobman et al. (NEJM, 2018) randomized 6,106 low-risk nulliparous women to elective induction at 39w0d versus expectant management to 41 weeks.

Fetal Heart Rate Monitoring — NICHD 3-Tier System

The most consequential real-time decision-making tool on labor and delivery, and — per the interobserver reliability literature — one of the least consistently applied. ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 10 (October 2025) is the current consolidated standard, replacing the older Practice Bulletins 106 and 116 and the 2022 oxygen practice advisory.

This is a recently consolidated guideline

CPG No. 10 published October 2025 — recent enough that some institutional protocols may not yet reflect it. Confirm your unit's current FHR management algorithm matches the updated recommendations below, particularly around oxygen supplementation.

The Building Blocks

ComponentDefinitions
BaselineNormal 110–160 bpm; bradycardia <110, tachycardia >160, averaged over a 10-minute window
VariabilityAbsent (undetectable) · Minimal (≤5 bpm) · Moderate (6–25 bpm — the reassuring range) · Marked (>25 bpm)
AccelerationsAbrupt rise ≥15 bpm above baseline for ≥15 sec (≥32 weeks); ≥10bpm/10sec if <32 weeks
Early decelerationsGradual, mirror the contraction (nadir coincides with contraction peak) — reflect fetal head compression, not hypoxia; not concerning in isolation
Late decelerationsGradual, nadir after the contraction peak — reflect uteroplacental insufficiency; recurrent late decelerations are a genuine warning sign
Variable decelerationsAbrupt, variable in timing/shape/depth relative to contractions — reflect cord compression; concerning when recurrent and associated with reduced variability
Prolonged deceleration≥2 minutes but <10 minutes; ≥10 minutes redefines the baseline

The Three Categories

Cat I — ~15%
Normal. Predictive of normal fetal acid-base status. No specific action required.
Cat II — ~80%
Indeterminate — the largest, most heterogeneous bucket, requiring clinical judgment
Cat III — <5%
Abnormal. Predictive of abnormal fetal acid-base status at time of observation.
  • Category I requires all of: baseline 110–160, moderate variability, no late or variable decelerations, early decelerations present or absent, accelerations present or absent.
  • Category II is everything that's neither I nor III — bradycardia without absent variability, tachycardia, minimal or marked variability, absent variability without recurrent decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations with moderate variability, recurrent late decelerations with moderate variability, a prolonged deceleration, or absence of induced accelerations after stimulation.
  • Category III requires either a sinusoidal pattern, or absent variability plus one of: recurrent late decelerations, recurrent variable decelerations, or bradycardia.

Management — What Changed in the 2025 Guideline

1
Category II — initial intrauterine resuscitation before cesarean: maternal position change, amnioinfusion, IV fluid bolus, reduction/cessation of oxytocin or other augmentation agents, and correction of any identified maternal pathophysiology (hypotension, tachysystole).
2
Tachysystole with high-risk Category II/III features persisting despite stopping oxytocin: consider a rapid-acting tocolytic (terbutaline) for uterine relaxation.
3
Category III not responsive to initial resuscitation: expedited delivery — and per the updated guideline, preparation for delivery should begin promptly, in parallel with resuscitative measures, not only after they've failed.
What ACOG now recommends against Tier A
  • Routine maternal oxygen supplementation for Category II/III tracings in a normoxic mother — multiple RCTs and meta-analyses show no improvement in umbilical artery pH or neonatal outcomes; this reverses decades of reflexive practice.
  • Routine intrapartum fetal pulse oximetry for assessing fetal status.
  • Primary reliance on computerized interpretation of the fetal heart rate in labor.

Labor Analgesia

A deliberately concise module — pharmacologic analgesia in depth (dosing, agent selection, complication management) lives in the dedicated Analgesics Atlas. Here: what belongs specifically in a labor-management context.

  • Neuraxial (epidural/CSE) is the most effective labor analgesia available and can be initiated at any point in labor on maternal request — there is no evidence-based cervical dilation cutoff below which it must be withheld.
  • Myth-bust: epidural analgesia does not increase the cesarean delivery rate — this was a genuine concern in older literature, but contemporary randomized data (including early- versus late-epidural trials) has not borne it out. It may modestly lengthen the second stage, which contemporary ACOG second-stage duration guidance already accounts for (Module 1).
  • Nitrous oxide and systemic opioids remain reasonable options for patients declining or not candidates for neuraxial analgesia, with a less complete analgesic effect.
  • Contraindications to neuraxial analgesia: coagulopathy/significant thrombocytopenia, uncorrected hypovolemia, infection at the insertion site, and true local anesthetic allergy (rare) — these are the ones worth knowing cold, even outside anesthesia.

Delivery Mechanics & Operative Delivery

How a fetus actually navigates the pelvis, when instruments belong in the room, and the current evidence on VBAC.

Cardinal Movements of Labor

The sequence a vertex fetus moves through to negotiate the pelvis: engagement → descent → flexion → internal rotation → extension → external rotation (restitution) → expulsion. Understanding where progress is stalling in this sequence is what separates a targeted intervention (repositioning, more time, operative assistance) from a reflexive move to cesarean.

Operative Vaginal Delivery — Forceps & Vacuum

ForcepsVacuum
Key maternal riskHigher rate of significant perineal/vaginal lacerations, OASISLower maternal laceration risk than forceps

Common indications: prolonged/arrested second stage, maternal exhaustion, and a non-reassuring FHR tracing (Module 3) late in the second stage where expedited vaginal delivery is faster and safer than cesarean.

Cesarean Delivery

The low transverse uterine incision is standard for the great majority of cesareans — lower blood loss, easier repair, and (critically for future pregnancies) the lowest uterine rupture risk in a subsequent labor. A classical (vertical) incision is reserved for specific situations (very preterm gestation with an underdeveloped lower segment, transverse lie with ruptured membranes, some anterior placenta previa/accreta cases) and carries a substantially higher rupture risk in any future pregnancy, essentially mandating a scheduled repeat cesarean thereafter.

The most common indications for a primary cesarean, in order of frequency: labor dystocia (arrest disorders), abnormal/Category II-III FHR tracing, fetal malpresentation, multiple gestation, and suspected macrosomia (ACOG Obstetric Care Consensus 1) — which is exactly why the labor-curve redefinition in Module 1 and the FHR management changes in Module 3 are the two highest-leverage tools for safely lowering a unit's primary cesarean rate.

Factors favoring success: prior vaginal delivery (including a prior VBAC), spontaneous labor onset, favorable cervix, and a non-recurrent indication for the original cesarean. Factors reducing success: induced labor, labor dystocia as the original indication, maternal obesity, and advanced gestational age at delivery. Up to two prior low transverse cesareans is generally considered an acceptable TOLAC candidacy per ACOG; three or more has a thinner evidence base and warrants individualized counseling. Classical, inverted-T, or unknown-type prior incisions are contraindications to TOLAC given substantially higher rupture risk.

Labor & Delivery Emergencies

The five presentations that turn a routine delivery into a code. Tap each to expand.

Shoulder Dystocia ▸
Signature findings

The "turtle sign" — the fetal head retracts against the perineum immediately after delivery, with the anterior shoulder impacted behind the maternal pubic symphysis. Complicates roughly 0.1–3% of vaginal deliveries, and — the point worth internalizing — most cases occur with no predictive risk factors. Macrosomia and GDM raise the odds but don't reliably predict who it happens to.

Prevention

Not reliably preventable at the individual-patient level; a history of shoulder dystocia, operative vaginal delivery, or a prior infant with brachial plexus injury raises recurrence risk enough (≥10%) that primary elective cesarean is a reasonable discussion point in a subsequent pregnancy.

Escalate — the HELPERR sequence

Help (call for it immediately) · Evaluate for episiotomy · Legs (McRoberts — hyperflex the maternal thighs to the abdomen) · Pressure (suprapubic, not fundal) · Enter maneuvers (internal rotation — Woods corkscrew, Rubin) · Remove the posterior arm · Roll the patient (Gaskin, all-fours). McRoberts + suprapubic pressure resolves roughly half of cases; posterior arm delivery has the highest single-maneuver success rate (~86%) when escalation is needed. Never apply fundal pressure — it drives the anterior shoulder further into the pubic bone and worsens the impaction. Brachial plexus injury occurs in an estimated 4–15% of shoulder dystocia deliveries regardless of maneuver or operator experience — appropriate technique reduces but does not eliminate this risk, which matters for how the event is documented and discussed with the family afterward.

Umbilical Cord Prolapse ▸
Signature findings

Sudden, severe fetal bradycardia or a Category III tracing immediately after membrane rupture (spontaneous or artificial) is the classic trigger to perform a vaginal exam and rule this out. Incidence roughly 0.1–0.6% of deliveries; risk factors include malpresentation, polyhydramnios, unengaged presenting part, and iatrogenic causes (amniotomy with a high presenting part, ECV with ruptured membranes) — nearly half of cases are iatrogenic.

Prevention

Avoid amniotomy when the presenting part is high and unengaged; consider a controlled, needle-based amniotomy technique in polyhydramnios to slow fluid egress.

Escalate

Manual elevation of the presenting part off the cord (keep your hand there through transport to the OR), maternal repositioning (knee-chest or steep Trendelenburg), and consider bladder filling (500–700mL saline via Foley) to further relieve compression. This is an emergent cesarean in the overwhelming majority of cases — vaginal delivery is only appropriate if it's genuinely imminent (crowning, +3 station or beyond).

Uterine Rupture ▸
Signature findings

In a TOLAC patient, the classic triad is sudden fetal bradycardia or Category III tracing, loss of fetal station on exam, and new-onset abdominal pain or vaginal bleeding — but fetal heart rate abnormality is frequently the only presenting sign, which is exactly why continuous FHR monitoring is non-negotiable throughout a TOLAC.

Prevention

Appropriate TOLAC candidate selection (Module 5) is the primary prevention strategy; avoiding misoprostol for cervical ripening in any patient with a prior uterine scar is a specific, high-yield rule.

Escalate

Emergent cesarean/laparotomy — this is one of the true "minutes matter" obstetric emergencies, since both maternal hemorrhage and fetal hypoxic injury accumulate rapidly once rupture occurs.

Amniotic Fluid Embolism ▸
Signature findings

Sudden, catastrophic cardiovascular collapse — hypotension, hypoxia, and altered mental status — typically during labor, delivery, or shortly postpartum, rapidly followed by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and consumptive coagulopathy. Rare — estimated incidence roughly 2–8 per 100,000 deliveries — but carries a high case-fatality rate, and it's a diagnosis of exclusion with no confirmatory test available in real time.

Prevention

Not preventable in any established sense; the pathophysiology is better understood as an abnormal maternal inflammatory/immunologic response than a mechanical "embolism" of fluid, despite the name.

Escalate

This is a full-team resuscitation from the first suspicion — call for help immediately, aggressive hemodynamic support, early massive transfusion protocol activation given the near-inevitable DIC, and consideration of advanced support (VA-ECMO) at centers with that capability for refractory cardiovascular collapse.

Postpartum Hemorrhage ▸
Signature findings

ACOG (reVITALize) defines PPH as cumulative blood loss ≥1,000mL regardless of delivery route, or any blood loss accompanied by signs/symptoms of hypovolemia, within 24 hours of birth — a deliberate move away from the older, route-dependent 500mL (vaginal) / 1,000mL (cesarean) thresholds, precisely because waiting for 500mL after a vaginal birth to "count" delayed recognition. Quantitative blood loss measurement (weighing pads/drapes) is meaningfully more accurate than visual estimation and is now the ACOG-recommended standard over eyeballing it.

The 4 T's — causes, in order of frequency

Tone (uterine atony — accounts for up to ~80% of cases), Trauma (lacerations, uterine rupture, hematoma), Tissue (retained placenta/products), Thrombin (coagulopathy, whether pre-existing or consumptive from ongoing hemorrhage).

Prevention

Active management of the third stage — prophylactic oxytocin at every delivery — is the single highest-yield prevention measure and should be routine (Module 1).

Escalate — the uterotonic ladder for atony

Oxytocin first-line (routine at delivery and first-line for treatment) → methylergonovine (avoid in hypertension/preeclampsia) → carboprost/15-methyl PGF2α (avoid in asthma) → misoprostol as an additional or alternative agent. No single second-line agent has demonstrated clear superiority — the point is rapid, sequential escalation, not waiting to see if the first dose "kicks in." Tranexamic acid (TXA) 1g IV is increasingly used adjunctively for ongoing hemorrhage. If pharmacologic management fails: uterine tamponade (Bakri balloon), uterine compression sutures (B-Lynch), uterine artery embolization (interventional radiology, if the patient is stable enough to move), and hysterectomy as the definitive last resort when life-threatening hemorrhage persists despite the above.

Partner's Role in Labor & Delivery

What's actually useful to do in the room — and how to recognize when the room has shifted from "normal labor" to something that needs your attention.

During Labor

  • Track contraction timing/spacing so the clinical team gets consistent information, especially in early labor at home.
  • Physical comfort measures (position changes, counter-pressure for back labor, hydration) genuinely help and are something you're positioned to do continuously in a way staff can't.
  • Ask questions on your partner's behalf when she's mid-contraction and can't easily advocate for herself — this is one of the most concretely useful things a support person does.

Recognize When Things Have Escalated

Signs the room's energy has genuinely changed
  • Multiple staff enter quickly and quietly, or someone leaves to "get the doctor" with urgency rather than routine check-in
  • The monitor alarms repeatedly and isn't reset with a simple position change
  • Anyone says the words "OR," "we need to move now," or asks you to step out — these are not the moments to ask detailed questions; step back, let the team work, ask for an update once things stabilize

You are not expected to interpret a fetal monitor or make a clinical call. Your job in a genuine emergency is to get out of the way efficiently and be ready to support afterward — emotionally and logistically (informing family, handling other children, being present for the next decision that does need your input, like emergency consent conversations).

Immediately After Delivery

If your baby is healthy at delivery, ask about immediate skin-to-skin contact and delayed cord clamping (typically 30–60 seconds at minimum, per ACOG) — both have real, evidence-supported benefits and are easy to advocate for in the moment if the birth plan didn't explicitly cover them. If you're offered the choice to cut the cord, that's entirely a personal preference with no clinical downside either way — do what feels meaningful to you.

Tachycardia Differentiation, Interval-by-Interval Anatomy & High-Stakes Pattern Recognition

This volume closes three gaps left after Vols. I–II: (1) a working algorithm for the wide-complex-tachycardia decision at the bedside, (2) a wave/interval-level reference so each abnormality can be pinned to a specific measurement, and (3) the pattern-recognition skills that keep normal variants from being over-called and posterior STEMI from being missed.

A.Narrow vs. Wide Complex Tachycardia

First branch point at the bedside — before any algorithm, before any calipers.
⚠ Step Zero — Before You Analyze Anything
If the patient is hypotensive, has active chest pain, altered sensorium, or signs of shock: synchronized cardioversion first. Wide-complex-tachycardia algorithms are for the stable patient. An unstable patient in a wide-complex tachycardia gets electricity, not calipers.

The First Split: QRS Duration

CategoryQRS DurationWorking Assumption Until Proven Otherwise
Narrow complex< 120 msSupraventricular in origin — impulse used the His-Purkinje system
Wide complex≥ 120 msVentricular tachycardia until proven otherwise — this default protects patients; treating VT as SVT can kill, treating SVT-A as VT rarely does
Fig. A1 — Same paper speed, same box grid. The wide-complex beat is measured onset-to-offset of the QRS, not eyeballed — put calipers on it.

Narrow Complex Tachycardia — Differential by Regularity

Regular

  • Sinus tachycardia
  • AVNRT (typical, slow-fast)
  • AVRT (orthodromic, via accessory pathway)
  • Atrial flutter with fixed-ratio conduction (esp. 2:1)
  • Atrial tachycardia (unifocal)
  • Junctional tachycardia

Irregularly Irregular

  • Atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response
  • Atrial flutter with variable block
  • Multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT) — ≥3 distinct P-wave morphologies
  • Frequent PACs superimposed on sinus tach
Pearl — The 2:1 Flutter Trap
Atrial flutter conducting 2:1 produces a regular narrow-complex tachycardia around 150 bpm that looks maddeningly like sinus tach or AVNRT. Flutter waves are often buried in the preceding T wave. If the rate is persistently and precisely ~150, suspect flutter and look for a second flutter wave hiding in the T wave — vagal maneuvers or adenosine can unmask the sawtooth by transiently increasing the block ratio.

Wide Complex Tachycardia — Differential

EtiologyMechanismConcerning Feature
Ventricular tachycardia (monomorphic)Reentry around scar (post-MI), or idiopathic focusHigh risk can degenerate to VF; treat as VT if any doubt

⚠ Concerning Features That Push Toward VT Regardless of Algorithm Step
  • Age > 35 with any history of structural heart disease or prior MI (VT probability >90% in this population alone)
  • Extreme QRS widths (>160 ms in RBBB morphology, >180 ms in LBBB morphology)
  • Fusion beats or capture beats (pathognomonic for AV dissociation → VT)
  • Positive or negative concordance across the entire precordium (all V1–V6 same direction)
  • Hemodynamic instability disproportionate to the rate
Fig. A2 — A capture (or fusion) beat interrupting an otherwise wide, regular run is near-pathognomonic for VT — it proves the atria and ventricles are running independently.

B.The Brugada Algorithm — VT vs. SVT with Aberrancy

Four sequential yes/no questions. A single "yes" at any step ends the algorithm: it's VT.
🔍 Verify — Performance Data
Brugada et al.'s original 1991 derivation (384 VT, 170 SVT-A) reported 98.7% sensitivity and 96.5% specificity for VT. Real-world validation has not reproduced this. Subsequent studies (Isenhour et al. and others) found meaningfully lower accuracy when applied by working clinicians rather than algorithm derivers, and most published WCT criteria — Brugada included — carry high specificity but comparatively low sensitivity (roughly 20–50% in some series) for VT. Practical translation: a "negative" algorithm (pointing toward SVT-A) does not reliably exclude VT. If in doubt, treat as VT.
Fig. B1 — Brugada 4-step algorithm. Each "yes" is terminal. A full "no" pathway favors SVT-A but does not rule out VT with the same confidence.

Step-by-Step Detail

StepWhat You're MeasuringWhy It Points to VT
1Look across all six precordial leads for an RS complex (any biphasic R-then-S). If NONE exists — every lead is purely monophasic (all R) or purely negative (QS) — that's Step 1 positive.SVT conducted through the normal His-Purkinje system almost always produces an RS somewhere in the precordium. Ventricular myocyte-to-myocyte conduction (VT) is slow and disorganized enough to abolish RS morphology entirely across the precordium.
2In the precordial lead with the clearest RS complex, measure from the onset of R to the nadir of S.Cell-to-cell ventricular conduction is slower than conduction via the His-Purkinje system, so VT produces a longer R-to-S interval. >100 ms is the cutoff.
3Look for P waves marching through the QRS complexes at their own independent rate, unrelated to the QRS rate — plus fusion beats (a QRS that looks like a hybrid of sinus-conducted and ventricular-origin complexes) or capture beats (an occasional narrow, normally-conducted QRS interrupting the wide-complex run).AV dissociation means the atria and ventricles are being driven by two separate pacemakers — only possible if the ventricular rhythm did not originate from a supraventricular source. Fusion/capture beats are the single most specific finding for VT on the entire algorithm.
4Apply BBB-morphology-specific criteria. RBBB-pattern (dominant R in V1): monophasic R, biphasic qR, or "rabbit ears" with left peak taller than right in V1 favor VT; R/S ratio <1 in V6 favors VT. LBBB-pattern (dominant S in V1): broad R wave >30ms in V1, notched/slurred S-wave downstroke in V1, or any Q wave in V6 favor VT.These reproduce the classic bundle-branch-block morphologies as they would appear with a ventricular focus vs. a supraventricular one conducted through a fixed or functional BBB.
Fig. B2 — The four Brugada steps, each shown as the SVT-A finding (blue) vs. the VT finding (red/highlighted) that ends the algorithm.
🚫 Myth: "Regular rate excludes VT"
Monomorphic VT is by definition regular. A perfectly regular wide-complex tachycardia is not reassuring — it is exactly what VT looks like. Regularity alone carries no diagnostic weight in this differential.

Complementary / Alternative Approaches

aVR Algorithm (Vereckei)

A single-lead shortcut using lead aVR alone:

  • Initial R wave in aVR → VT
  • Initial r or q wave >40 ms in aVR → VT
  • Notching on the initial downstroke of a predominantly negative QRS in aVR → VT
  • Ventricular activation velocity ratio (Vi/Vt, comparing voltage change in the first 40ms vs. last 40ms of the QRS): ratio ≤1 → VT; ratio >1 → SVT-A

Faster to apply at the bedside than the full 4-step Brugada sequence; performs comparably in most validation cohorts.

Clinical / Historical Clues (Support, Don't Replace, the ECG)

  • Prior MI or known structural heart disease → VT until proven otherwise (pretest probability alone is >90% in this group)
  • Identical morphology to a documented baseline BBB on a prior ECG → favors SVT-A
  • Documented pre-excitation (short PR, delta wave) on a prior sinus ECG → consider antidromic AVRT
  • History of paroxysmal SVT previously terminated by adenosine/vagal maneuvers → favors SVT-A, but is not proof
⚠ If You Cannot Decide
Treat as VT. The consequence of treating SVT-A as VT (typically just observation or a rate-control approach) is far smaller than the consequence of treating true VT as SVT-A — AV nodal blockers given to VT do nothing helpful and waste time; AV nodal blockers given to a pre-excited tachycardia can precipitate VF.

D.Pseudo-Concerning EKGs — Normal Variants That Mimic Disease

Patterns that are physiologically normal in specific populations but visually overlap with STEMI, cardiomyopathy, or channelopathy.
🔍 Terminology Note
Older literature (including the original International Criteria) labeled this the "Black athlete pattern." Current sports-cardiology consensus explicitly recommends against race-based naming and criteria; this pattern is now framed as a benign repolarization variant that happens to occur at substantially higher frequency in athletes of Black/Afro-Caribbean descent — the content below reflects that framing while flagging the population where clinical suspicion for "abnormal" ECG findings should be tempered.

1. Early Repolarization / Anterior ST-Elevation-with-TWI Pattern

What It Looks Like

  • J-point elevation with convex ("domed") ST-segment elevation in V1–V4
  • Followed by T-wave inversion confined to those same leads
  • Seen in an estimated ~12% of male athletes of African/Afro-Caribbean descent in published cohorts

Why It Gets Mistaken For

  • Anterior STEMI (the ST elevation)
  • ARVC or HCM (the T-wave inversion)
  • Brugada syndrome pattern (J-point/ST elevation in right precordial leads)
Fig. D1 — J-point/ST elevation with a convex, "domed" upstroke immediately preceding T-wave inversion, confined to V1–V4. No reciprocal depression, no pathologic Q.
Pearl — What Keeps It Benign
  • TWI is confined to V1–V4 — extension into the lateral leads (V5–V6) should prompt further workup
  • ST elevation precedes the TWI and has a concave-then-convex "domed" morphology rather than the classic coved Brugada Type 1 pattern
  • No reciprocal ST depression, no pathologic Q waves, no conduction abnormality
  • Asymptomatic, young, athletic — clinical context does the heavy lifting here

2. Persistent Juvenile T-Wave Pattern (PJTWP)

FeatureDetail
DefinitionAsymmetric T-wave inversion in V1–V4 that fails to convert to the normal upright adult pattern; the "juvenile" pattern is expected in children ≤16 years but persists into adulthood in a subset, disproportionately Black women
Key distinguishing featureNo associated ST-segment deviation (unlike the early repolarization variant above)
Reported frequencyRoughly 15% of Black women vs. ~4% of white women showed anterior TWI in one comparative UK cohort; a US cohort of professional football players found ~4.3% of Black players vs. ~1% of white players
MimicsHCM, ARVC, anterior ischemia — the differentiator is the absence of ST changes plus a stable, unchanged appearance on serial tracings
Fig. D2 — Compare to Fig. D1: same location (V1–V4), but no preceding ST elevation at all — the key feature that separates PJTWP from the early-repol variant and from ischemia.

3. Athlete's Heart — The Full Constellation

Normal in Trained Athletes

  • Sinus bradycardia (even into the 30s–40s at rest)
  • Sinus arrhythmia
  • First-degree AV block
  • Mobitz I (Wenckebach) — should resolve with exercise
  • Incomplete RBBB
  • Isolated voltage criteria for LVH (without other markers of pathologic hypertrophy)
  • Early repolarization pattern in inferolateral leads

Needs Further Workup

  • T-wave inversion beyond V1–V4 or extending to lateral leads
  • Any pathologic Q wave
  • Complete LBBB or RBBB
  • QTc >470ms (men) / >480ms (women)
  • Brugada Type 1 pattern (see box below)
  • Profound axis deviation
  • Ventricular pre-excitation

Why the Distinction Matters

Preparticipation ECG screening produces false positives when normal athletic adaptation is misread as pathology — the downside is unnecessary restriction from sport, cost, and anxiety. Missing a true pathologic pattern is the alternative failure mode. The distinguishing features above exist specifically to keep both error rates down.

⚠ Do Not Confuse This Section With True Brugada Syndrome
The benign anterior repolarization variant above is a right-precordial ST-elevation-with-TWI pattern, but it is not Brugada syndrome. True Brugada Type 1 pattern is a distinct, genuinely concerning finding: a coved (not domed) ST-segment elevation ≥2mm in V1–V2 with a gradually descending ST segment directly into an inverted T wave, without a preceding upstroke. It is associated with a channelopathy (typically SCN5A) and risk of sudden cardiac death from polymorphic VT/VF. Any coved morphology in V1–V2 — regardless of the patient's demographics or athletic status — warrants cardiology referral, not reassurance. This atlas volume does not cover the Brugada syndrome diagnostic criteria (Type 1/2/3) or risk stratification in depth; flag for a dedicated channelopathy volume if wanted.
Fig. D3 — The distinguishing shape: a domed (rounded, convex-then-concave) upstroke is the benign variant; a straight, descending "coved" ramp directly into the inverted T is Type 1 Brugada.

E.Repolarization vs. Depolarization Abnormalities, and Spotting Lead Misplacement

Two different troubleshooting questions that get conflated: "is this a real repolarization problem, or an artifact of depolarization" — and separately, "are the leads even on correctly."

Depolarization Abnormality vs. Repolarization Abnormality

The QRS complex represents depolarization; the ST segment and T wave represent repolarization. A large fraction of "abnormal" ST-T changes are not primary repolarization disease — they are the expected, secondary consequence of an abnormal QRS. Recognizing this prevents chasing a repolarization diagnosis (ischemia, pericarditis, electrolyte) when the real driver is a depolarization problem.

If the QRS shows...Expect this secondary ST-T changeRule
LBBBST-T discordant to the terminal QRS deflection (ST elevation where the QRS is negative, depression where it's positive)Appropriate discordance is expected and not ischemic — concordant ST changes (same direction as QRS) or excessively discordant elevation (Sgarbossa/Smith-modified criteria) are what should worry you
RBBBST-T changes typically confined to V1–V3, discordant to the terminal R'ST-T changes outside V1–V3, or any change concordant with a positive QRS, are not explained by the conduction delay alone
LVH (voltage criteria met)"Strain pattern" — downsloping ST depression with asymmetric T-wave inversion in leads with the tallest R waves (typically I, aVL, V5–V6)This is a secondary repolarization change from the hypertrophied myocardium, not primary ischemia — but cannot fully exclude concurrent ischemia in a symptomatic patient
Ventricular pacingDiscordant ST-T changes analogous to LBBB (paced beats are, mechanically, a form of induced LBBB-like conduction)Same Sgarbossa-type discordance rules apply; modified Sgarbossa criteria were validated in this setting too
Pre-excitation (WPW)Secondary ST-T changes wherever the delta wave most distorts the QRSCan mimic inferior or anterior infarct patterns — always check for a short PR and delta wave before reading a Q wave as pathologic
Fig. E1 — Compare the ST segment's direction to the QRS's dominant direction. Same direction (concordant) is the red flag; opposite direction (discordant) is the expected, benign secondary change.
Pearl — The One-Line Rule
Before calling any ST-T abnormality "ischemia," ask: does the QRS itself explain it? If there's a wide QRS, paced rhythm, or voltage-criteria LVH, the ST-T change is very likely secondary — evaluate it against discordance rules, not against standard STEMI criteria.

Recognizing Primary Repolarization Abnormalities

  • Concordant ST elevation or depression with a wide QRS (same direction as the majority QRS deflection) — not explained by the conduction pattern, should be treated as ischemic until proven otherwise
  • Diffuse, concave ST elevation with PR depression across multiple territories, plus PR elevation in aVR — favors pericarditis over infarct
  • Diffuse T-wave changes tracking a lab value (peaked, narrow-based T waves with a normal QRS in hyperkalemia; flattened T waves with U waves in hypokalemia) — primary metabolic repolarization change, QRS is initially preserved until the electrolyte disturbance is severe

Lead Misplacement / Reversal — Recognition Patterns

ReversalClassic Tell
LA/RA (left arm / right arm) reversalNegative P wave, QRS, and T wave in lead I with an otherwise normal-looking rhythm; lead aVR becomes positive (aVR is almost never upright in a true sinus rhythm) — this combination is the single most reliable tell
LA/LL (left arm / left leg) reversalSubtle — lead I resembles the normal lead II, lead II resembles normal lead I; often only caught by comparing to a prior tracing

Fig. E2 — LA/RA reversal: lead I turns entirely negative (P, QRS, and T all inverted) and aVR — almost never upright in sinus rhythm — becomes positive. That combination is the tell.
⚠ When Posterior Leads "Don't Match" the Standard 12-Lead
If V7–V9 show ST elevation but V1–V3 don't show the expected reciprocal depression, recheck electrode positioning before concluding the finding is real — a positioning error is common and should be excluded first. Conversely, ST depression in V1–V3 without posterior leads applied is itself an indication to place them, not a reason to dismiss the finding.

F.Posterior STEMI

The infarct the standard 12-lead ECG is not designed to see directly.
Fig. F1 — Posterior injury produces true ST elevation only in the posterior leads (V7–V9); the standard anterior leads (V1–V3) record a mirror-image reciprocal pattern.

Diagnostic Criteria

LocationFindingThreshold
V1–V3 (anteroseptal, reciprocal)Horizontal ST-segment depression with an upright, often tall T waveAny degree in the right clinical context should trigger posterior lead placement — this is the trigger to look further, not itself diagnostic
V1–V3Tall, broad R wave (>30ms duration, R/S ratio >1 in V2) with abnormal R-wave progression (R in V2–V3 larger than R in V4–V6)Represents a reciprocal, mirror-image "Q wave" of the posterior infarct
V7–V9 (posterior, direct)ST-segment elevation≥0.5mm in a single posterior lead is diagnostic — a deliberately lower threshold than the standard 1mm used elsewhere, because the posterior wall sits far from the recording electrode and produces a smaller signal
V7–V9, men <40ST-segment elevation≥1mm cutoff improves specificity in this subgroup
🔍 Verify — Outcomes Data on Missed Posterior Involvement
A 2021 retrospective study of LCx-territory STEMI found that in roughly 7% of patients, the infarct was detectable only on synthesized posterior leads (not the standard 12-lead) — and that subgroup carried substantially higher cardiac mortality at both 3 months and 1 year compared to those detected on standard leads. This is the outcomes argument for a low threshold to apply V7–V9 whenever V1–V3 show unexplained ST depression with ischemic symptoms.

Lead Placement (Get This Right or the Criteria Don't Apply)

LeadPosition
V7Left posterior axillary line, same horizontal plane as V6
V8Tip of the left scapula, same horizontal plane as V6
V9Left paraspinal region (2–3cm lateral to the spinous processes — not directly over them), same horizontal plane as V6

What Else Is Usually Present

  • Posterior STEMI is very rarely isolated — look hard for concurrent inferior (II, III, aVF) or lateral (I, aVL, V5–V6) ST elevation, since the culprit is most often the left circumflex or a dominant right coronary artery supplying the posterolateral wall
  • In inferior STEMI, ST elevation in III > II suggests RCA occlusion (which can also supply the posterior wall) — a cue to actively look for posterior extension rather than stopping the read at "inferior STEMI"
  • Right ventricular infarction is a separate question requiring right-sided leads (V3R, V4R) — do not assume it's excluded just because posterior leads were checked

Key Mimic / Pitfall

🚫 Pitfall: Right Ventricular Hypertrophy Looks Similar in V1–V3
RVH also produces ST depression with large R waves in the right precordial leads, and can be genuinely difficult to distinguish from posterior injury on the anterior leads alone. Clinical context (acute ischemic symptoms vs. known chronic RVH/pulmonary hypertension), comparison to prior ECGs, and the direct posterior leads themselves are what separate the two — don't rely on V1–V3 morphology alone.
⚠ Bottom Line
Isolated ST depression in V1–V3 with ischemic symptoms should be treated as an active STEMI equivalent until posterior leads prove otherwise — not downgraded to "non-STEMI" by default. This is one of the more common causes of a delayed catheterization lab activation.

Causes

Cardiac

  • Pericardial effusion / tamponade — fluid dampens the signal reaching surface electrodes; look for electrical alternans as a companion clue in large effusions
  • Infiltrative cardiomyopathy (cardiac amyloidosis) — classic, high-yield combination
  • Dilated cardiomyopathy, end-stage
  • Constrictive pericarditis
  • Extensive myocardial fibrosis / prior massive infarction

Extracardiac

  • COPD / hyperinflated lungs (air is a poor conductor — insulates the signal)
  • Obesity (increased tissue distance from heart to electrode)
  • Pleural effusion
  • Pneumothorax
  • Anasarca / generalized volume overload
  • Myxedema (severe hypothyroidism) — often with bradycardia as a companion finding
  • Technical: incorrect gain/calibration setting — always confirm calibration before treating this as a clinical finding
Pearl — The Amyloid Tell
Low QRS voltage on ECG despite a thickened left ventricle on echocardiogram is a discordance that should raise infiltrative cardiomyopathy (amyloidosis) specifically — a normal hypertrophied ventricle should generate more voltage, not less. This voltage/wall-thickness mismatch is one of the highest-yield pattern-recognition cues in cardiac amyloid.

H.RBBB & LBBB Recognition — Subtle Patterns and STEMI-in-LBBB

The classic teaching examples are easy. The ones that actually get missed are borderline.

RBBB — Classic vs. Subtle

Fig. H1 — Complete RBBB is hard to miss. Incomplete/early RBBB is a subtle terminal slur or small second R' that's often skimmed over — it still tells you about right-sided conduction.

LBBB — Classic vs. Subtle

Fig. H2 — Subtle LBBB is easy to read as "just a wide QRS." The absence of any Q wave in I and V6, plus a broad monomorphic R even without an obvious notch, is what should trigger the diagnosis.

STEMI in the Presence of LBBB — Sgarbossa & Modified Sgarbossa Criteria

⚠ Why This Matters
Standard STEMI ST-elevation criteria (Section F reference, Vol. II) do not apply when a LBBB (or a ventricular-paced rhythm) is present, because LBBB itself produces expected, discordant ST-T changes (Section E) that would otherwise mask or mimic infarction. Sgarbossa's criteria — and its more sensitive modified version — exist specifically to read through the LBBB.
Criterion (Original Sgarbossa)FindingPoints
1Concordant ST elevation ≥1mm in any lead with a positive (upright) QRS5
2Concordant ST depression ≥1mm in V1, V2, or V33
3 (original)Excessively discordant ST elevation ≥5mm in a lead with a negative (predominantly S-wave) QRS2

A total score ≥3 is considered specific for infarction. The problem: the original fixed 5mm cutoff for criterion 3 is highly specific but poorly sensitive — a large S wave can require a disproportionately large absolute ST elevation to ever trigger it.

🔍 Modified (Smith) Sgarbossa Criteria — Higher Sensitivity
Criterion 3 is replaced with a proportional measurement: ST-elevation / S-wave depth ratio ≤ −0.25 in any lead with a discordant ST-elevation-to-negative-QRS relationship. This scales the required ST elevation to the size of the preceding S wave rather than using one fixed number, and is meaningfully more sensitive than the original criterion 3 without a large loss of specificity.
Fig. H3 — The three Sgarbossa-family findings. Any one of the three, in the presence of LBBB or ventricular pacing, should prompt activation as an infarct until proven otherwise.

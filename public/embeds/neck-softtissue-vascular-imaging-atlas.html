Thyroid & Parathyroid

10 findings

1. Thyroid nodule, incidental CT

Variable

Focal thyroid lesion identified incidentally on a CT obtained for another indication.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, often hypodense area within the thyroid gland; CT is not the primary modality for thyroid nodule characterization.

Differential
  • Benign colloid nodule (most common)
  • Thyroid adenoma
  • Thyroid carcinoma (less common, cannot be reliably excluded by CT alone)
Next Step

Dedicated thyroid ultrasound for further characterization per incidental thyroid nodule management guidelines.

2. Multinodular goiter, CT

Variable

Enlarged thyroid gland with multiple nodules, common and usually benign.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, heterogeneous thyroid gland with multiple nodules of varying attenuation, sometimes with calcification or cystic change.

Differential
  • Multinodular goiter (benign, most common)
  • Graves disease (diffuse enlargement, less nodular)
Next Step

Ultrasound for nodule characterization; endocrine referral, particularly if compressive symptoms or substernal extension.

3. Substernal/retrosternal goiter extension

Variable

Extension of an enlarged thyroid gland into the superior mediastinum.

Imaging Characteristics

Thyroid tissue contiguous with the cervical gland extending below the thoracic inlet, often with tracheal deviation/compression.

Differential
  • Benign multinodular goiter with substernal extension (most common)
  • Thyroid malignancy (less common)
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral, particularly if causing airway compression.

4. Thyroiditis, CT

Variable

Inflammation of the thyroid gland, from autoimmune, infectious, or subacute causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, heterogeneous, often hypodense thyroid gland with surrounding fat stranding in acute/subacute cases.

Differential
  • Subacute (de Quervain) thyroiditis
  • Acute suppurative thyroiditis (rare, correlate with infection signs)
  • Hashimoto thyroiditis (chronic, autoimmune)
Next Step

Correlate with thyroid function tests; endocrine referral.

5. Thyroid carcinoma, CT features

Concerning

Thyroid malignancy, with CT sometimes showing suspicious features though ultrasound remains the primary modality for nodule characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, infiltrative thyroid mass with possible extrathyroidal extension, suspicious lymphadenopathy, or tracheal/esophageal invasion in locally advanced disease.

Differential
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma (most common)
  • Follicular, medullary, or anaplastic carcinoma (less common subtypes)
Next Step

Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration for tissue diagnosis; endocrine/surgical oncology referral.

6. Parathyroid adenoma, CT (4D-CT)

Variable

Benign parathyroid tumor causing primary hyperparathyroidism, localized with dedicated 4D-CT protocols.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, avidly enhancing nodule posterior to the thyroid gland with washout on delayed phases, distinguishing it from lymph nodes and thyroid tissue on 4D-CT.

Differential
  • Parathyroid adenoma (most common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism)
  • Parathyroid hyperplasia (multiple gland involvement)
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral for parathyroidectomy planning; correlate with calcium and PTH levels.

7. Parathyroid carcinoma, CT

Concerning

Rare malignant parathyroid tumor, often larger and more locally invasive than adenoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, often invasive parathyroid mass with local infiltration into adjacent structures, distinguishing it from the typically smaller, well-defined adenoma.

Differential
  • Parathyroid carcinoma (rare)
  • Large atypical parathyroid adenoma
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral; correlate with markedly elevated calcium and PTH levels.

8. Thyroglossal duct cyst

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the thyroglossal duct, the most common congenital neck mass, classically midline.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, midline or near-midline cystic lesion, typically at or near the hyoid bone, which moves with tongue protrusion/swallowing (though this is a clinical rather than CT finding).

Differential
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (benign, most common congenital neck mass)
  • Infected thyroglossal duct cyst (if wall thickening/surrounding stranding present)
Next Step

Surgical referral (Sistrunk procedure) given recurrent infection risk if untreated.

9. Ectopic thyroid tissue

Benign

Thyroid tissue in an aberrant location, most often lingual, from incomplete descent during embryologic development.

Imaging Characteristics

Tissue with attenuation similar to normal thyroid tissue in an ectopic location (most often the tongue base), which may represent the patient's only functioning thyroid tissue.

Differential
  • Lingual thyroid (most common ectopic location)
Next Step

Correlate with thyroid function and confirm the presence/absence of a normally located thyroid gland before any planned resection.

10. Thyroid hemorrhage/hematoma

Variable

Bleeding into a thyroid nodule or the gland itself, causing acute pain and swelling.

Imaging Characteristics

High-attenuation material within a thyroid nodule or diffusely within the gland, sometimes with adjacent soft-tissue stranding.

Differential
  • Hemorrhage into a benign nodule (most common)
  • Post-procedural hemorrhage (post-biopsy)
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; monitor for airway compromise if extensive.

Salivary Glands

8 findings

1. Sialolithiasis (salivary duct stone)

Variable

Calculus within a salivary duct, most common in the submandibular gland (Wharton duct) given its more viscous, alkaline secretions.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense calculus within the expected course of a salivary duct, often with associated ductal dilation and gland swelling if obstructing.

Differential
  • Submandibular duct stone (most common location)
  • Parotid duct stone (less common)
Next Step

ENT referral; sialendoscopy or surgical removal for symptomatic, non-passing stones.

2. Sialadenitis, CT

Concerning

Inflammation/infection of a salivary gland, often associated with an obstructing stone or dehydration.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, hyperenhancing salivary gland with surrounding fat stranding, sometimes with an identifiable obstructing stone.

Differential
  • Acute suppurative sialadenitis (bacterial, often Staph aureus)
  • Viral sialadenitis (mumps, typically bilateral parotid)
Next Step

Antibiotics for suppurative sialadenitis; identify and address any obstructing stone.

3. Sialadenitis with abscess

Concerning

Salivary gland infection progressing to a discrete abscess collection.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection within or adjacent to the salivary gland, with surrounding inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Salivary gland abscess (complication of sialadenitis)
Next Step

IR or surgical drainage plus antibiotics.

4. Pleomorphic adenoma, salivary gland

Benign

Most common benign salivary gland tumor, most often in the parotid gland.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated mass, most often in the superficial lobe of the parotid, with variable enhancement.

Differential
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (benign, most common parotid tumor)
  • Warthin tumor (second most common benign parotid tumor)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision given growth potential and small but real malignant transformation risk over time.

5. Warthin tumor

Benign

Second most common benign parotid tumor, associated with smoking, often bilateral/multifocal.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often cystic-appearing mass in the tail of the parotid gland, which can be bilateral in a subset of cases.

Differential
  • Warthin tumor (benign, smoking-associated, can be bilateral)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain lesions.

6. Malignant salivary gland tumor, CT features

Concerning

Salivary gland malignancy, with CT features that can suggest but not definitively diagnose malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, infiltrative mass with possible facial nerve involvement (parotid), regional lymphadenopathy, or invasion of adjacent structures.

Differential
  • Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (most common salivary malignancy)
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma (notable perineural spread propensity)
  • Acinic cell carcinoma
Next Step

Surgical oncology/ENT referral for biopsy and staging; MRI often complementary for perineural spread assessment.

7. Sjögren syndrome, salivary gland changes

Variable

Autoimmune condition causing chronic salivary and lacrimal gland inflammation.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small cysts and heterogeneous, punctate calcifications within the parotid glands bilaterally, described as a 'honeycomb' or 'salt and pepper' pattern in more advanced disease.

Differential
  • Sjögren syndrome (correlate with sicca symptoms and autoantibody testing)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; increased lymphoma risk warrants monitoring for new focal masses.

8. Ranula

Benign

Mucous retention cyst or extravasation pseudocyst arising from the sublingual gland, in the floor of the mouth.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, unilocular cystic lesion in the floor of the mouth (simple ranula) or extending through the mylohyoid muscle into the submandibular space (plunging ranula).

Differential
  • Simple ranula (confined to the sublingual space)
  • Plunging ranula (extends through/around the mylohyoid muscle)
Next Step

ENT/oral surgery referral for symptomatic lesions (marsupialization or gland excision).

Larynx & Airway

8 findings

1. Laryngeal carcinoma, CT staging

Concerning

Laryngeal malignancy, most often squamous cell carcinoma, with CT used for staging including cartilage invasion assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with variable extension, assessed for cartilage invasion (sclerosis or frank destruction), paraglottic/pre-epiglottic space involvement, and nodal disease.

Differential
  • Glottic, supraglottic, or subglottic carcinoma (location affects staging and treatment approach)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning (surgery, radiation, or combined therapy).

2. Vocal cord paralysis, CT correlate

Concerning

Immobility of a vocal cord from recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, with CT used to search for a causative lesion along the nerve's course.

Imaging Characteristics

Asymmetric vocal cord position (often medialized/paramedian on the affected side) with a dilated ipsilateral piriform sinus and laryngeal ventricle; CT is obtained to search the nerve's course from skull base to mediastinum for a causative mass.

Differential
  • Malignancy along the recurrent laryngeal nerve course (lung apex, mediastinum, thyroid, skull base)
  • Idiopathic (a substantial proportion have no identifiable cause)
  • Post-surgical (thyroidectomy, cardiothoracic surgery)
Next Step

Search the full nerve course (skull base to aortopulmonary window) for a causative lesion; ENT referral.

3. Epiglottitis, CT

Concerning

Inflammation/infection of the epiglottis and supraglottic structures, a potential airway emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened, edematous epiglottis and aryepiglottic folds with surrounding fat stranding.

Differential
  • Epiglottitis (bacterial, though also seen with severe irritant/thermal injury)
Next Step

Urgent ENT/anesthesia consultation for airway management; avoid agitating the patient or instrumenting the airway outside a controlled setting given obstruction risk.

4. Laryngeal fracture, CT

Concerning

Fracture of the laryngeal cartilages from blunt or penetrating neck trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line(s) through the thyroid or cricoid cartilage, sometimes with associated laryngeal soft-tissue swelling, hematoma, or free air.

Differential
  • Laryngeal fracture (airway emergency, particularly with displacement)
Next Step

Emergent ENT consultation for airway assessment and possible surgical repair.

5. Subglottic stenosis, CT

Variable

Narrowing of the subglottic airway, from prior intubation, tracheostomy, or (in children) congenital causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential or focal narrowing of the subglottic airway lumen.

Differential
  • Post-intubation/tracheostomy stenosis (acquired, most common in adults)
  • Congenital subglottic stenosis (pediatric)
Next Step

ENT referral for bronchoscopic evaluation and possible intervention (dilation, surgical reconstruction).

6. Laryngocele

Variable

Air- or fluid-filled dilation of the laryngeal saccule, which can be internal, external, or mixed relative to the thyrohyoid membrane.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, air- or fluid-filled cystic structure arising from the laryngeal ventricle, confined within the larynx (internal) or extending through the thyrohyoid membrane into the neck (external/mixed).

Differential
  • Laryngocele (benign)
  • Association with an obstructing laryngeal tumor at the saccule (important to exclude, particularly in adults)
Next Step

ENT referral; exclude an underlying obstructing laryngeal lesion, particularly in adult-onset cases.

7. Foreign body, airway CT

Concerning

Aspirated or ingested foreign body causing airway compromise or obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete foreign material within the airway lumen, with associated distal air trapping or atelectasis if causing bronchial obstruction.

Differential
  • Aspirated foreign body
Next Step

Emergent ENT/pulmonology consultation for bronchoscopic removal.

8. Tracheal stenosis, CT

Concerning

Narrowing of the tracheal lumen, from prior intubation/tracheostomy, malignancy, or inflammatory disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or long-segment narrowing of the tracheal lumen, often at the site of a prior tracheostomy stoma or endotracheal tube cuff.

Differential
  • Post-intubation/tracheostomy stricture (most common acquired cause)
  • Tracheal malignancy
  • Relapsing polychondritis
Next Step

ENT/pulmonology referral for bronchoscopic evaluation and possible intervention.

Pharynx & Oral Cavity

6 findings

1. Oropharyngeal carcinoma, CT staging

Concerning

Malignancy of the oropharynx (tonsil, tongue base, soft palate), increasingly HPV-associated in recent decades.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with variable local extension, assessed for tongue base/deep muscle invasion and nodal disease, which is often cystic in HPV-associated disease.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (most common, HPV-associated subtype increasingly common)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging (including HPV/p16 testing) and treatment planning.

2. Oral cavity carcinoma, CT staging

Concerning

Malignancy of the oral cavity (tongue, floor of mouth, buccal mucosa), most often tobacco/alcohol-associated squamous cell carcinoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of depth of invasion (a key staging parameter), mandibular involvement, and nodal disease.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (most common)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

3. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma, CT

Concerning

Malignancy of the nasopharynx, with a distinct epidemiologic and etiologic profile (EBV-associated) compared to other head and neck cancers.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass in the nasopharynx, often with skull base invasion and bulky retropharyngeal/cervical lymphadenopathy given the region's rich lymphatic drainage.

Differential
  • Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (EBV-associated, more common in certain endemic populations)
  • Lymphoma
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral; MRI often complementary for skull base and perineural assessment.

4. Peritonsillar abscess, CT

Concerning

Collection of pus adjacent to the tonsil, a complication of tonsillitis, with CT used when clinical exam is difficult or diagnosis is uncertain.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection adjacent to the tonsil, with associated tonsillar/soft-tissue swelling and often uvular deviation.

Differential
  • Peritonsillar abscess
  • Peritonsillar cellulitis (phlegmon, without a discrete drainable collection)
Next Step

ENT consultation for drainage (needle aspiration or incision and drainage) plus antibiotics.

5. Retropharyngeal abscess, CT

Concerning

Collection of pus in the retropharyngeal space, more common in young children, with airway compromise risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection in the retropharyngeal space with associated prevertebral soft-tissue thickening, distinguishing a true abscess from reactive edema/phlegmon.

Differential
  • Retropharyngeal abscess (surgical emergency given airway and mediastinal spread risk)
  • Retropharyngeal cellulitis/edema (reactive, without a discrete drainable collection)
Next Step

Emergent ENT consultation for airway assessment and drainage; risk of descent into the mediastinum if untreated.

6. Tonsillar hypertrophy

Benign

Enlarged palatine and/or lingual tonsils, common in children, occasionally causing airway obstruction.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged tonsillar tissue narrowing the oropharyngeal airway, symmetric in typical benign hypertrophy.

Differential
  • Reactive/physiologic tonsillar hypertrophy (most common, especially in children)
  • Asymmetric enlargement (warrants further workup to exclude lymphoma or other malignancy)
Next Step

ENT referral for tonsillectomy if causing significant obstructive symptoms; biopsy for asymmetric enlargement.

Deep Neck Spaces & Infection

8 findings

1. Ludwig angina

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, bilateral cellulitis of the submandibular space, most often odontogenic in origin, with significant airway compromise risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral submandibular and sublingual space swelling with fat stranding, typically without a discrete drainable abscess (a cellulitis rather than abscess process), often elevating and displacing the tongue posteriorly.

Differential
  • Ludwig angina (airway emergency, odontogenic source most common)
Next Step

Emergent ENT/anesthesia consultation for airway management given rapid progression risk; source control (often dental) and IV antibiotics.

2. Deep neck space abscess, general

Concerning

Collection of pus within one of the deep neck fascial spaces (parapharyngeal, retropharyngeal, submandibular, and others).

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection within a deep neck space, with surrounding inflammatory fat stranding.

Differential
  • Deep neck space abscess (location determines specific complication risks)
Next Step

Emergent ENT consultation for airway assessment and drainage plus antibiotics.

3. Descending necrotizing mediastinitis

Concerning

Extension of a deep neck infection into the mediastinum along fascial planes, a life-threatening complication.

Imaging Characteristics

Continuity of infected fluid/fat stranding tracking from the neck into the mediastinum along the danger space or other fascial planes.

Differential
  • Descending necrotizing mediastinitis (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent cardiothoracic and ENT surgical consultation for combined neck and mediastinal drainage plus broad-spectrum antibiotics.

4. Internal jugular vein thrombosis (Lemierre syndrome)

Concerning

Septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, classically following oropharyngeal infection (Fusobacterium necrophorum), with septic embolization risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the internal jugular vein with surrounding inflammatory change, often with an adjacent primary infectious source (peritonsillar/pharyngeal infection).

Differential
  • Lemierre syndrome (septic IJ thrombophlebitis, classic association with Fusobacterium)
Next Step

Anticoagulation and prolonged antibiotics; screen for septic pulmonary emboli given the propensity for hematogenous spread.

5. Sialadenitis with deep space extension

Concerning

Salivary gland infection extending into adjacent deep neck spaces.

Imaging Characteristics

Inflammatory change extending from an infected salivary gland into the adjacent parapharyngeal or submandibular space.

Differential
  • Complicated sialadenitis with deep space extension
Next Step

ENT consultation; antibiotics and drainage of any discrete abscess.

6. Cervical necrotizing fasciitis

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening necrotizing soft-tissue infection of the neck.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive fascial plane fluid/gas tracking with muscle edema, sometimes with frank soft-tissue gas (though its absence does not exclude the diagnosis).

Differential
  • Cervical necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation if clinically suspected.

7. Odontogenic infection with spread

Concerning

Dental infection extending into adjacent deep neck spaces (buccal, masticator, submandibular).

Imaging Characteristics

Periapical lucency at the causative tooth with contiguous inflammatory change/abscess extending into adjacent soft-tissue spaces.

Differential
  • Odontogenic abscess with deep space extension
Next Step

Dental/oral surgery and ENT consultation for source control and drainage.

8. Masticator space infection/abscess

Concerning

Infection within the masticator space, most often odontogenic, which can cause trismus from muscle involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection within the masticator space (containing the muscles of mastication and ramus of the mandible), often with an odontogenic source.

Differential
  • Masticator space abscess (odontogenic source most common)
Next Step

Dental/oral surgery and ENT consultation for source control and drainage.

Cervical Lymph Nodes

8 findings

1. Reactive cervical lymphadenopathy

Benign

Mildly enlarged lymph nodes from a reactive process, extremely common, particularly in children and young adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Oval nodes with preserved fatty hilum, generally <1-1.5 cm short axis (varies by nodal station), without necrosis or extranodal extension.

Differential
  • Reactive to upper respiratory/dental infection (most common)
  • Reactive to a more distant inflammatory process
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; typically no isolated workup needed for classic reactive nodes.

2. Suppurative/infected cervical lymphadenitis

Concerning

Infected lymph node, which can progress to abscess formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged node with central low attenuation (necrosis) and peripheral rim enhancement, with surrounding inflammatory fat stranding.

Differential
  • Bacterial lymphadenitis (Staph, Strep most common in children)
  • Suppurative node progressing to a drainable abscess
Next Step

Antibiotics; IR or surgical drainage if a discrete, drainable abscess forms.

3. Metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy

Concerning

Nodal metastasis, most often from a head and neck squamous cell carcinoma primary, occasionally from an infraclavicular primary.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, rounded node with loss of fatty hilum, central necrosis, and/or extranodal extension (irregular margins with infiltration into surrounding fat).

Differential
  • Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma (most common, search the mucosal surfaces for a primary)
  • Metastatic thyroid carcinoma
  • Metastatic disease from an infraclavicular primary (particularly with left-sided supraclavicular/Virchow node involvement)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for primary tumor search (often via PET-CT and panendoscopy) and biopsy.

4. Cervical lymphoma

Concerning

Lymphomatous cervical lymph node involvement, Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple enlarged, often homogeneous (non-necrotic) nodes, sometimes matted together, without the extranodal extension typical of squamous cell carcinoma metastases.

Differential
  • Hodgkin lymphoma (classic cervical presentation, especially in young adults)
  • Non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Next Step

Oncology referral for biopsy (excisional biopsy often preferred over FNA for lymphoma diagnosis) and staging.

5. Tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis (scrofula)

Concerning

Mycobacterial cervical lymph node infection, with a characteristic necrotic appearance and often a more indolent course than pyogenic infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged nodes with central necrosis and peripheral rim enhancement, often matted together, sometimes with a cold abscess and sinus tract formation in chronic disease.

Differential
  • Tuberculous lymphadenitis (endemic area exposure history important)
  • Nontuberculous mycobacterial infection (more common cause in young children in non-endemic areas)
  • Necrotic metastatic node
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation.

6. Kawasaki disease, cervical lymphadenopathy

Concerning

Cervical lymphadenopathy as one component of Kawasaki disease, a systemic vasculitis in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Unilateral, often single, enlarged cervical lymph node (>1.5 cm), typically without central necrosis, as one of the diagnostic criteria alongside fever and mucocutaneous findings.

Differential
  • Kawasaki disease-associated lymphadenopathy (correlate with the full clinical criteria)
Next Step

Pediatric cardiology/rheumatology referral; timely IVIG treatment is important given coronary artery aneurysm risk if untreated.

7. Castleman disease, cervical

Variable

Lymphoproliferative disorder, unicentric or multicentric, that can mimic other causes of lymphadenopathy.

Imaging Characteristics

Unicentric type: solitary, markedly enhancing (hypervascular) node. Multicentric type: widespread lymphadenopathy.

Differential
  • Castleman disease
  • Lymphoma
  • Metastatic lymphadenopathy
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral for biopsy and subtype-specific management.

8. Kimura disease / cervical eosinophilic lymphadenopathy

Variable

Rare, chronic inflammatory condition causing cervical lymphadenopathy and subcutaneous masses, more common in Asian populations, associated with peripheral eosinophilia.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, often matted cervical lymph nodes with associated subcutaneous soft-tissue masses, typically in the periauricular/submandibular region.

Differential
  • Kimura disease (correlate with peripheral eosinophilia and elevated IgE)
Next Step

Correlate with CBC/eosinophil count; biopsy for confirmation given overlap with other lymphoproliferative conditions.

Neck Masses - Congenital

6 findings

1. Branchial cleft cyst

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the branchial apparatus, most often second branchial cleft origin, presenting as a lateral neck mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, cystic lesion along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (second branchial cleft cyst, most common type), which can become infected/enlarged during upper respiratory infections.

Differential
  • Second branchial cleft cyst (most common type)
  • First branchial cleft cyst (periauricular/parotid location)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision, particularly given recurrent infection risk and the rare but important need to exclude cystic nodal metastasis in older adults with a new lateral neck cyst.

2. Cystic hygroma / lymphatic malformation, neck

Benign

Congenital lymphatic malformation, most often in the posterior neck, which can be identified prenatally or in infancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiloculated, thin-walled cystic mass, often extending across fascial planes and multiple neck spaces without respecting normal anatomic boundaries.

Differential
  • Macrocystic lymphatic malformation (cystic hygroma)
  • Microcystic or mixed lymphatic malformation
Next Step

Surgical/IR referral for sclerotherapy or resection, particularly if causing airway compromise or significant cosmetic concern.

3. Dermoid/epidermoid cyst, neck

Benign

Congenital inclusion cyst, often midline in the neck (submental region).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic lesion, sometimes containing fat or calcification (dermoid) or purely fluid-density (epidermoid), typically midline.

Differential
  • Dermoid cyst (may contain fat/calcification)
  • Epidermoid cyst (simple fluid density)
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (key differential if near the hyoid, moves with swallowing)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision.

4. Vascular malformation, neck (venous/lymphatic)

Benign

Congenital vascular malformation of the neck, venous, lymphatic, or mixed.

Imaging Characteristics

Venous malformation: compressible, low-flow lesion, sometimes with phleboliths. Lymphatic malformation: multiloculated cystic spaces.

Differential
  • Venous malformation
  • Lymphatic malformation
  • Mixed venolymphatic malformation
Next Step

IR/surgical referral for symptomatic lesions (sclerotherapy or resection).

5. Thymic cyst, cervical

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant along the path of thymic descent, which can present as a cervical mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often unilocular cystic lesion along the expected course of thymic descent (anterolateral neck to superior mediastinum).

Differential
  • Cervical thymic cyst (uncommon)
  • Branchial cleft cyst (key differential based on location)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic or diagnosis uncertain.

6. Torticollis, congenital muscular

Benign

Fibrosis and shortening of the sternocleidomastoid muscle in infants, sometimes with a palpable 'tumor.'

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform enlargement/thickening of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, sometimes with heterogeneous internal signal reflecting fibrosis, in an infant with head tilt.

Differential
  • Congenital muscular torticollis (sternocleidomastoid 'tumor of infancy,' benign and self-limited in most cases)
Next Step

Pediatric physical therapy referral (stretching exercises); most cases resolve with conservative management.

Neck Masses - Neoplastic

8 findings

1. Carotid body tumor (paraganglioma)

Variable

Vascular tumor arising at the carotid bifurcation, from paraganglion cells, classically splaying the internal and external carotid arteries.

Imaging Characteristics

Avidly enhancing, hypervascular mass at the carotid bifurcation, characteristically splaying the internal and external carotid arteries apart ('lyre sign').

Differential
  • Carotid body tumor (paraganglioma, usually benign but locally growing)
  • Other carotid space paraganglioma (vagal, jugular)
Next Step

ENT/vascular surgery referral; catheter angiography with possible preoperative embolization for surgical planning.

2. Schwannoma, neck (vagal/sympathetic chain)

Variable

Benign nerve sheath tumor arising from a cervical nerve, most often the vagus nerve or cervical sympathetic chain.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, fusiform mass along the expected course of the nerve of origin, with variable enhancement; vagal schwannomas displace (rather than splay) the carotid vessels, distinguishing them from carotid body tumors.

Differential
  • Vagal schwannoma (displaces rather than splays the carotid vessels)
  • Sympathetic chain schwannoma
  • Carotid body tumor (key differential, splays rather than displaces the vessels)
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgery referral for symptomatic or growing lesions.

3. Lipoma, neck

Benign

Benign fat-containing soft-tissue tumor of the neck.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneous, fat-attenuation mass with a thin, imperceptible capsule and no enhancing soft-tissue components.

Differential
  • Simple lipoma (benign)
  • Liposarcoma (rare, larger, more heterogeneous - key differential for atypical features)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous lipoma.

4. Soft tissue sarcoma, neck

Concerning

Malignant soft-tissue tumor of the neck, uncommon but important to recognize.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, heterogeneous, deep soft-tissue mass with variable enhancement and necrosis.

Differential
  • Soft tissue sarcoma
  • Other aggressive neck mass
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

5. Cervical spine/paraspinal mass, neck CT

Concerning

Mass arising from or adjacent to the cervical spine, presenting as a posterior neck mass (see also the neuro atlas for detailed spine tumor characterization).

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue mass with variable bony involvement/destruction, correlate with the neuro atlas series for detailed spinal tumor characterization.

Differential
  • Vertebral metastasis
  • Primary spinal tumor
  • Paraspinal abscess (infectious)
Next Step

Correlate with the neuro atlas series; spine surgery/oncology referral as indicated.

6. Plunging ranula

Variable

Extravasated mucous cyst extending from the sublingual gland through or around the mylohyoid muscle into the submandibular space.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic lesion extending from the floor of the mouth through/around the mylohyoid muscle into the submandibular space, distinct from a simple ranula confined above the mylohyoid.

Differential
  • Plunging ranula
Next Step

ENT/oral surgery referral for definitive management (typically sublingual gland excision).

7. Cervical thymoma / ectopic thymic tissue

Variable

Rare occurrence of thymic tissue or thymoma in the neck, from an ectopic location along the path of thymic descent.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue mass along the expected course of thymic descent, with imaging features similar to a mediastinal thymoma if neoplastic.

Differential
  • Ectopic cervical thymic tissue (benign)
  • Cervical thymoma (rare)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain lesions.

8. Metastasis to neck soft tissue (non-nodal)

Concerning

Metastatic deposit within the neck soft tissues outside of lymph nodes, uncommon.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue mass with variable enhancement, correlate with a known primary malignancy.

Differential
  • Soft tissue metastasis
  • Primary soft-tissue neoplasm (if no known primary)
Next Step

Oncology referral; biopsy if diagnosis is uncertain or would change management.

Vascular - Aorta & Great Vessels

8 findings

1. Aortic arch anomaly

Benign

Congenital variant of the aortic arch branching pattern or laterality, usually incidental but relevant to surgical/interventional planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Variant branching (bovine arch, aberrant subclavian artery) or arch laterality (right-sided aortic arch), best characterized on CT angiography.

Differential
  • Bovine arch (common left brachiocephalic-left common carotid shared origin, normal variant)
  • Aberrant right subclavian artery (arising distal to the left subclavian, can cause a vascular ring/dysphagia lusoria)
  • Right-sided aortic arch
Next Step

Usually incidental; note for any planned central line, surgical, or interventional procedure in the region. See also the chest atlas for detailed thoracic aortic pathology.

2. Carotid artery stenosis, CT angiography

Concerning

Narrowing of the carotid artery, quantified on CTA, relevant to stroke risk stratification (see also the neuro atlas for ultrasound-based grading).

Imaging Characteristics

Luminal narrowing from atherosclerotic plaque, quantified by percent stenosis (NASCET or similar criteria) on CT angiography.

Differential
  • Mild, moderate, or severe stenosis (grading determines management)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/neurology referral for revascularization discussion based on stenosis degree and symptom status; see also the neuro atlas for Doppler-based grading criteria.

3. Carotid or vertebral artery dissection, CTA

Concerning

Arterial wall tear, from trauma or spontaneous causes, a significant cause of stroke, particularly in younger patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic intramural hematoma narrowing the true lumen, or an intimal flap, best seen on CT angiography with appropriate windowing.

Differential
  • Traumatic dissection
  • Spontaneous dissection (connective tissue disorder, minor trauma, cervical manipulation)
Next Step

Neurology/vascular referral; antithrombotic therapy to reduce embolic stroke risk. See also the neuro atlas for detailed dissection characterization.

4. Blunt cerebrovascular injury, CTA grading

Concerning

Traumatic injury to the carotid or vertebral arteries from blunt neck trauma, graded by the Biffl scale.

Imaging Characteristics

Spectrum from intimal irregularity (grade I) to luminal narrowing/dissection (grades II-III) to occlusion (grade IV) to transection (grade V).

Differential
  • Biffl grade I-V blunt cerebrovascular injury (grade correlates with stroke risk and guides antithrombotic therapy intensity)
Next Step

Vascular/neurology consultation; antithrombotic therapy per current blunt cerebrovascular injury management guidelines.

5. Penetrating neck vascular injury, CTA

Concerning

Vascular injury from penetrating neck trauma, assessed by CTA in hemodynamically stable patients per zone-based management.

Imaging Characteristics

Active contrast extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, or vessel occlusion/dissection at or near the wound tract.

Differential
  • Penetrating vascular injury (specific vessel and injury type determine management)
Next Step

Vascular surgery consultation; CTA-based triage has largely replaced mandatory surgical exploration for stable patients with penetrating neck trauma.

6. Innominate artery aneurysm/injury

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation or traumatic injury of the innominate (brachiocephalic) artery, uncommon but significant given its critical location.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation, irregular contour, or active extravasation involving the innominate artery.

Differential
  • Innominate artery aneurysm
  • Traumatic innominate artery injury
Next Step

Emergent vascular/cardiothoracic surgery consultation for traumatic injury; vascular surgery referral for aneurysmal disease.

7. Thoracic outlet vascular compression, CTA

Variable

Compression of the subclavian artery or vein at the thoracic outlet, from an anomalous first rib, cervical rib, or muscular anomaly.

Imaging Characteristics

Positional narrowing or occlusion of the subclavian vessels on provocative (arm-abduction) imaging, sometimes with an associated cervical rib or other bony anomaly.

Differential
  • Arterial thoracic outlet syndrome
  • Venous thoracic outlet syndrome (Paget-Schroetter syndrome if associated with subclavian vein thrombosis)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for symptomatic cases; provocative positional imaging is key to diagnosis.

8. Aortic arch aneurysm involving great vessel origins

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of the aortic arch extending to involve the origins of the great vessels, relevant to surgical planning given the anatomic complexity.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic arch dilation with the aneurysm extending to or involving the origins of the innominate, left common carotid, and/or left subclavian arteries.

Differential
  • Arch aneurysm (surgical complexity increases with great vessel involvement)
Next Step

Cardiothoracic/vascular surgery referral for repair planning; see also the chest atlas for detailed thoracic aortic aneurysm characterization.

Vascular - Peripheral Arterial

8 findings

1. Peripheral arterial disease, CTA (lower extremity)

Concerning

Atherosclerotic narrowing of the lower extremity arteries, causing claudication or critical limb ischemia in advanced disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Luminal narrowing or occlusion from atherosclerotic plaque, often with calcification, assessed for location, length, and severity to guide revascularization planning.

Differential
  • Claudication-level disease (moderate stenosis)
  • Critical limb ischemia (severe multi-level disease, often with poor distal runoff)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for revascularization planning (angioplasty/stenting vs. surgical bypass) based on disease pattern and severity.

2. Acute limb ischemia, CTA

Concerning

Sudden arterial occlusion causing limb-threatening ischemia, from embolism or in situ thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Abrupt arterial cutoff with a filling defect (thrombus/embolus) and absent distal opacification, often with a sharply demarcated occlusion point in embolic disease versus a more tapered occlusion in thrombotic disease on a background of atherosclerosis.

Differential
  • Embolic occlusion (cardiac source, sharp cutoff)
  • In situ thrombosis (underlying atherosclerotic disease, more tapered occlusion)
Next Step

Emergent vascular surgery consultation; time-critical given limb viability concerns.

3. Popliteal artery aneurysm

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of the popliteal artery, the most common peripheral artery aneurysm, with thrombosis/embolization risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation of the popliteal artery ≥1.5-2 cm (or >1.5x the diameter of the adjacent normal artery), often with mural thrombus.

Differential
  • Popliteal artery aneurysm (bilateral in a substantial proportion of cases, and associated with AAA in some patients)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for surveillance or repair based on size; screen for AAA and the contralateral popliteal artery given associated aneurysm risk.

4. Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome, CTA

Variable

Compression of the popliteal artery by an anomalous relationship with the gastrocnemius or popliteus muscle.

Imaging Characteristics

Medial deviation of the popliteal artery relative to an anomalous muscle origin, with dynamic imaging demonstrating positional compression/occlusion.

Differential
  • Popliteal artery entrapment syndrome (consider in young patients with exertional calf claudication)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for consideration of surgical decompression.

5. Peripheral artery pseudoaneurysm

Concerning

Contained vascular injury, most often from prior catheterization/access, trauma, or infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal outpouching from the artery with a 'yin-yang' swirling flow pattern on Doppler (if correlated) or a contained collection of contrast on CTA communicating with the arterial lumen.

Differential
  • Post-catheterization pseudoaneurysm (most common, femoral access site)
  • Traumatic or mycotic pseudoaneurysm
Next Step

Vascular surgery/IR referral for thrombin injection, ultrasound-guided compression, or surgical repair depending on size and location.

6. Arterial dissection, extremity

Concerning

Dissection of a peripheral (non-aortic) artery, uncommon, from trauma or spontaneous causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Intimal flap with true and false lumen, or an intramural hematoma narrowing the true lumen.

Differential
  • Traumatic extremity arterial dissection
  • Spontaneous dissection (rare, consider underlying connective tissue disorder)
Next Step

Vascular surgery consultation; management depends on limb perfusion status.

7. Vasculitis, medium/large vessel CTA findings

Concerning

Inflammatory vessel wall disease, with CTA showing wall thickening and luminal narrowing.

Imaging Characteristics

Circumferential vessel wall thickening with associated luminal narrowing, sometimes with post-stenotic dilation, in a pattern affecting multiple vascular territories.

Differential
  • Takayasu arteritis (younger patients, aorta and major branches)
  • Giant cell arteritis (older patients, cranial and large vessel involvement)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; correlate with inflammatory markers and consider temporal artery ultrasound/biopsy if giant cell arteritis is suspected.

8. Median arcuate ligament syndrome, CTA (celiac)

Variable

Extrinsic compression of the celiac axis by the median arcuate ligament of the diaphragm; see also the abdomen/pelvis atlas for detailed characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, characteristic 'hooked' narrowing at the celiac origin, worse on expiration, often with post-stenotic dilation and prominent collateral vessels.

Differential
  • Median arcuate ligament syndrome
  • Atherosclerotic celiac stenosis (key differential, not positional)
Next Step

See also the abdomen/pelvis atlas; vascular surgery referral if symptomatic.

Vascular - Venous & DVT

8 findings

1. Deep vein thrombosis, CT venography

Concerning

Clot within a deep vein, with CT venography sometimes used (particularly as part of a combined CT-PE protocol) though ultrasound remains first-line for the extremities.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within a deep vein with associated vein enlargement, sometimes with surrounding soft-tissue edema.

Differential
  • Acute DVT (filling defect with vein enlargement)
  • Chronic DVT (vein may be smaller with wall thickening/recanalization)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; ultrasound remains the primary diagnostic modality for extremity DVT in most clinical settings.

2. Superior vena cava thrombosis, CT

Concerning

Clot within the SVC, from central line/pacemaker lead-related thrombosis or malignant compression; see also the chest atlas for detailed SVC syndrome characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the SVC lumen with prominent collateral venous pathways.

Differential
  • Catheter/lead-related thrombosis
  • Malignant compression with secondary thrombosis
Next Step

Anticoagulation for thrombotic causes; see also the chest atlas for full SVC syndrome workup.

3. Internal jugular vein thrombosis, CT

Concerning

Clot within the internal jugular vein, from central line placement, malignant compression, or septic thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome, see Deep Neck Spaces category).

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the internal jugular vein lumen, with or without surrounding inflammatory change depending on the underlying cause.

Differential
  • Catheter-related thrombosis
  • Malignant compression/invasion (e.g., from thyroid or nodal disease)
  • Septic thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; identify and treat the underlying cause.

4. May-Thurner syndrome, CT

Variable

Compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery, a risk factor for left lower extremity DVT.

Imaging Characteristics

Compression/narrowing of the left common iliac vein at its crossing point beneath the right common iliac artery, sometimes with associated venous collaterals or thrombosis.

Differential
  • May-Thurner anatomic compression (may be incidental or associated with left iliofemoral DVT)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/IR referral for stenting if associated with symptomatic DVT or significant compression.

5. Portal vein thrombosis, CT (cross-reference)

Concerning

Clot within the portal venous system; see the abdomen/pelvis atlas for detailed characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the portal vein lumen on contrast-enhanced imaging.

Differential
  • Bland thrombus
  • Tumor thrombus (from HCC)
Next Step

See the abdomen/pelvis atlas for full portal vein thrombosis workup; anticoagulation and hepatology referral as indicated.

6. Venous malformation, extremity CT

Benign

Congenital low-flow venous malformation, which can occur in any extremity soft tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Compressible, low-attenuation soft-tissue lesion, sometimes with phleboliths (a relatively specific finding), without significant arterial-phase enhancement.

Differential
  • Venous malformation (benign)
Next Step

IR referral for sclerotherapy if symptomatic.

7. Superficial venous thrombophlebitis

Variable

Inflammation and thrombosis of a superficial vein, from IV catheter placement, varicose veins, or a hypercoagulable state.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened, non-compressible superficial vein with surrounding soft-tissue inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Superficial thrombophlebitis (usually managed conservatively)
  • Extension into the deep venous system (warrants anticoagulation, particularly if near the saphenofemoral junction)
Next Step

Conservative management (NSAIDs, warm compresses) for isolated superficial disease; anticoagulation if extension toward the deep system.

8. Inferior vena cava anomaly/thrombosis, CT

Variable

Congenital IVC variant or acquired IVC thrombosis, both relevant to venous thromboembolism risk and filter/intervention planning.

Imaging Characteristics

Anomaly: duplicated or left-sided IVC, azygos continuation of the IVC. Thrombosis: filling defect within the IVC lumen.

Differential
  • Congenital IVC variant (important to note before any planned intervention)
  • IVC thrombosis (bland or tumor thrombus)
Next Step

Correlate anomaly with any planned IVC filter placement; anticoagulation and hematology/vascular input for thrombosis.

Vascular Malformations & Aneurysms

6 findings

1. Arteriovenous malformation, extremity/soft tissue

Concerning

High-flow congenital vascular malformation with direct arteriovenous shunting, which can cause pain, bleeding, or high-output cardiac effects if large.

Imaging Characteristics

Tangle of abnormal vessels with early venous filling on CT angiography, reflecting the arteriovenous shunting.

Differential
  • Extremity/soft tissue arteriovenous malformation
Next Step

IR referral for embolization; vascular surgery for combined surgical/endovascular management of complex lesions.

2. Arteriovenous fistula, acquired

Variable

Abnormal direct connection between an artery and vein, from trauma, prior catheterization, or surgical creation (dialysis access).

Imaging Characteristics

Early venous filling at the site of the fistula on CT angiography, with dilated, arterialized draining veins.

Differential
  • Traumatic/iatrogenic AV fistula
  • Surgically created dialysis fistula (expected finding, assessed for patency/complications)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/IR referral for symptomatic acquired fistulas; dialysis access fistulas assessed for maturation/complications per clinical context.

3. Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome

Variable

Congenital syndrome with a combination of capillary malformation, venous/lymphatic malformation, and limb overgrowth.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse venous and/or lymphatic malformation involving an extremity, often with associated limb length/girth discrepancy and superficial venous anomalies (persistent embryonic veins).

Differential
  • Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome (combined vascular malformation syndrome)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary vascular anomaly team referral for comprehensive management.

4. Fibromuscular dysplasia

Variable

Non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory vascular disease causing a characteristic 'string of beads' appearance, most common in the renal and carotid arteries of women.

Imaging Characteristics

Alternating areas of stenosis and mild dilation creating a 'string of beads' appearance, most classically in the renal arteries (secondary hypertension) or carotid arteries.

Differential
  • Fibromuscular dysplasia (correlate with hypertension if renal artery involvement, or stroke/TIA if carotid involvement)
Next Step

Vascular medicine/nephrology referral; screen for aneurysm and dissection given the associated risk with this vasculopathy.

5. Mycotic (infected) aneurysm

Concerning

Aneurysm from infection of the arterial wall, from bacteremia/endocarditis or direct extension of an adjacent infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Saccular, often irregular and rapidly enlarging aneurysm, sometimes with adjacent inflammatory change/fluid, in a patient with bacteremia or endocarditis.

Differential
  • Mycotic aneurysm (high rupture risk, requires urgent management)
Next Step

Emergent vascular surgery/infectious disease consultation given high rupture risk; blood cultures and targeted antibiotics.

6. Renal artery aneurysm

Variable

Focal dilation of the renal artery; see also the abdomen/pelvis atlas for detailed characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded outpouching from the renal artery or a branch, sometimes with a calcified rim.

Differential
  • True aneurysm
  • Pseudoaneurysm (post-biopsy, post-trauma)
Next Step

See the abdomen/pelvis atlas; vascular surgery/IR referral for larger lesions or those in women of childbearing age.

Soft Tissue Infection & Masses

6 findings

1. Cellulitis, CT

Variable

Soft-tissue infection without a drainable collection, with CT primarily useful to exclude a deeper abscess or necrotizing process.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue thickening and stranding without a discrete rim-enhancing fluid collection.

Differential
  • Simple cellulitis (no drainable collection)
  • Underlying abscess (if a discrete collection is identified)
Next Step

Antibiotics for simple cellulitis; drainage if a discrete abscess is identified.

2. Soft tissue abscess, general CT

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the soft tissues, at any body location.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, low-attenuation fluid collection, sometimes with internal gas.

Differential
  • Soft tissue abscess
Next Step

Incision and drainage plus antibiotics.

3. Necrotizing soft tissue infection, general CT

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection with fascial plane involvement, at any body location.

Imaging Characteristics

Fascial thickening and fluid tracking along fascial planes, with soft-tissue gas being a specific but insensitive finding.

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
  • Simple cellulitis/abscess (less extensive)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation for debridement; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation if clinically suspected.

4. Generalized lymphadenopathy, CT

Concerning

Widespread lymph node enlargement across multiple nodal regions, with a broad systemic differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple enlarged lymph nodes across two or more non-contiguous nodal regions.

Differential
  • Systemic infection (viral, HIV)
  • Lymphoma
  • Systemic inflammatory/autoimmune disease (sarcoidosis)
  • Widespread metastatic disease
Next Step

Systemic workup based on clinical context; biopsy of the most accessible/largest node if malignancy is a concern.

5. Panniculitis, CT

Variable

Inflammation of the subcutaneous fat, from a range of infectious, inflammatory, and idiopathic causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased attenuation and stranding within the subcutaneous fat, sometimes with nodularity.

Differential
  • Erythema nodosum-associated panniculitis
  • Infectious panniculitis
  • Idiopathic/other panniculitis subtypes
Next Step

Correlate with clinical/dermatologic exam; skin biopsy often needed for subtype characterization.

6. Calcinosis cutis / soft tissue calcification, general

Variable

Calcium deposition within the skin or subcutaneous tissue, from a range of causes including connective tissue disease, renal failure, and trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete or diffuse calcific densities within the skin or subcutaneous tissue.

Differential
  • Dystrophic calcification (post-traumatic, post-infectious)
  • Metastatic calcification (renal failure, hypercalcemia)
  • Connective tissue disease-associated (scleroderma, dermatomyositis)
Next Step

Correlate with the underlying clinical context; rheumatology or nephrology referral as indicated.

Lateral Neck Soft Tissue & Airway

14 findings

1. Epiglottitis, 'thumbprint sign'

Concerning

Classic lateral neck plain film sign of epiglottic swelling, historically a key diagnostic tool, though clinical suspicion should prompt direct airway evaluation rather than awaiting imaging.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened, rounded epiglottis resembling a thumbprint on the lateral neck view, with loss of the normal thin, curved epiglottic shadow.

Differential
  • Epiglottitis (airway emergency)
  • Normal 'omega epiglottis' variant (can mimic in some patients, clinical correlation essential)
Next Step

Urgent ENT/anesthesia consultation for airway management; avoid agitating the patient or obtaining imaging if it would delay definitive airway management in a clinically toxic-appearing patient.

2. Retropharyngeal abscess, widened prevertebral space

Concerning

Widening of the prevertebral soft tissue space on lateral neck film, suggesting retropharyngeal infection or hematoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Prevertebral soft tissue thickness exceeding expected norms (roughly >7 mm at C2, >22 mm at C6 in adults, with pediatric thresholds lower and techniques/positioning affecting measurement).

Differential
  • Retropharyngeal abscess/cellulitis
  • Retropharyngeal hematoma (trauma or anticoagulation)
  • Technical/positioning artifact (crying, flexed neck position in children - a common false-positive cause)
Next Step

CT with contrast for definitive characterization given the nonspecific nature of this plain film sign; ENT consultation if infection suspected.

3. Croup, 'steeple sign'

Concerning

Subglottic tracheal narrowing from viral laryngotracheobronchitis, classically seen on the AP neck/chest view.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, tapered subglottic narrowing on the AP view, resembling a steeple or pencil point, with normal supraglottic structures (distinguishing it from epiglottitis).

Differential
  • Croup (viral laryngotracheobronchitis, most common cause)
  • Bacterial tracheitis (more toxic-appearing, can mimic)
Next Step

Supportive care (humidified air, corticosteroids, nebulized epinephrine for moderate-severe cases); imaging is often unnecessary with a classic clinical presentation.

4. Bacterial tracheitis, plain film

Concerning

Bacterial infection of the trachea, which can mimic croup but with a more toxic clinical presentation and higher airway obstruction risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular tracheal air column with a 'ragged' or membranous appearance from sloughed mucosal membranes, distinct from the smooth tapering of croup.

Differential
  • Bacterial tracheitis (higher morbidity than croup, may require intubation)
Next Step

Urgent ENT/anesthesia consultation given higher airway compromise risk than typical croup.

5. Foreign body, airway/esophagus plain film

Concerning

Radiopaque foreign body within the airway or esophagus, visible on plain film if sufficiently dense.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete radiopaque object within the airway or esophageal lumen; a coin lodged in the esophagus classically appears round ('en face') on the AP view, while a tracheal coin appears round on the lateral view (opposite orientations due to differing anatomic orientation of the two structures).

Differential
  • Esophageal foreign body (more common location for coins in children)
  • Airway/tracheal foreign body (higher urgency given obstruction risk)
Next Step

Urgent ENT/GI consultation for endoscopic removal, particularly time-sensitive for airway foreign bodies and button batteries in either location.

6. Button battery, esophageal

Concerning

Swallowed button battery lodged in the esophagus, a time-critical emergency due to corrosive mucosal injury risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Round, well-defined density with a characteristic double-density 'halo' or 'step-off' rim on frontal view, distinguishing it from a coin (single uniform density).

Differential
  • Button battery (halo/step-off sign)
  • Coin (uniform density, no halo)
Next Step

Emergent endoscopic removal given rapid corrosive injury risk; do not delay for further workup.

7. Adenotonsillar hypertrophy, lateral neck film

Benign

Enlarged adenoid and/or tonsillar tissue, common in children, assessed for airway narrowing.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged adenoid soft-tissue density at the nasopharyngeal roof and/or enlarged tonsillar tissue narrowing the oropharyngeal airway column.

Differential
  • Reactive/physiologic adenotonsillar hypertrophy (most common, especially in children)
Next Step

ENT referral for adenotonsillectomy if causing significant obstructive symptoms (sleep apnea, feeding difficulty).

8. Prevertebral soft tissue swelling, traumatic

Concerning

Widened prevertebral soft tissue space in the trauma setting, a sensitive but nonspecific sign of underlying cervical spine injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Prevertebral soft tissue thickness exceeding expected norms in the setting of neck trauma.

Differential
  • Underlying cervical spine fracture/ligamentous injury with hematoma
Next Step

CT for further evaluation of the cervical spine given this nonspecific but sensitive trauma sign.

9. Subcutaneous emphysema, neck

Concerning

Air within the subcutaneous soft tissues of the neck, from tracheal/esophageal injury, pneumothorax, or pneumomediastinum tracking superiorly.

Imaging Characteristics

Streaky, lucent tracking through the subcutaneous tissues of the neck.

Differential
  • Tracheal or esophageal injury
  • Pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum with superior tracking
  • Post-procedural (dental, endoscopic)
Next Step

CT to identify the source; correlate with recent trauma or instrumentation.

10. Pneumomediastinum with cervical extension, plain film

Variable

Free mediastinal air tracking into the neck soft tissues, visible on neck or chest films.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent streaks outlining neck soft-tissue planes and fascial layers, continuous with mediastinal free air.

Differential
  • Spontaneous pneumomediastinum (Valsalva-related, usually benign)
  • Esophageal perforation (concerning)
  • Tracheobronchial injury
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; further workup (esophagram/CT) if perforation is suspected.

11. Calcified cervical lymph node, plain film

Benign

Calcification within a cervical lymph node, from prior granulomatous infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, discrete, densely calcified nodule(s) in the expected location of cervical lymph node chains.

Differential
  • Prior histoplasmosis/tuberculosis (most common cause)
  • Prior granulomatous disease of other etiology
Next Step

No follow-up needed for classic calcified granulomatous nodes.

12. Laryngeal cartilage calcification, plain film

Benign

Physiologic ossification/calcification of the laryngeal cartilages, common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Calcific densities within the expected location of the thyroid and/or cricoid cartilage, a normal age-related finding.

Differential
  • Normal age-related laryngeal cartilage calcification/ossification
Next Step

No action needed; recognize as a normal variant that can occasionally obscure or mimic pathology.

13. Cervical spine widening from mass effect, plain film

Variable

Widening of the retropharyngeal or prevertebral space from an adjacent mass lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased prevertebral soft-tissue space from a mass effect, distinct from the more diffuse thickening of infection/edema.

Differential
  • Adjacent mass lesion (nodal, neoplastic)
  • Vertebral body lesion with associated soft-tissue component
Next Step

CT or MRI for further characterization of the underlying mass.

14. Air-fluid level, retropharyngeal space

Concerning

Air-fluid level within the retropharyngeal space, a specific but insensitive sign of abscess (as opposed to simple cellulitis/edema).

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete air-fluid level within the widened prevertebral/retropharyngeal soft-tissue space.

Differential
  • Retropharyngeal abscess (a discrete air-fluid level is a relatively specific sign for a drainable collection)
Next Step

CT for full characterization; ENT consultation for drainage.

Vascular Calcification

8 findings

1. Carotid artery calcification, plain film

Variable

Atherosclerotic calcification of the carotid artery, sometimes incidentally noted on neck, chest, or dental panoramic films.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear calcific density paralleling the expected course of the carotid artery, often near the carotid bifurcation (angle of the mandible on a panoramic dental film).

Differential
  • Carotid atherosclerotic calcification (marker of cardiovascular risk, incidental finding on dental panoramic films is a recognized teaching point)
Next Step

Correlate with cardiovascular risk factors; consider carotid duplex ultrasound if not previously evaluated, particularly for incidentally noted calcification on dental imaging.

2. Aortic wall calcification, plain film

Benign

Atherosclerotic calcification of the aortic wall, common with age, visible on chest or abdominal plain films.

Imaging Characteristics

Curvilinear calcific densities paralleling the expected course of the aorta.

Differential
  • Normal age-related atherosclerosis
  • May suggest aneurysm if calcified walls appear widened
Next Step

Further imaging (ultrasound or CT) for aneurysm evaluation if aortic width appears increased.

3. Peripheral arterial calcification, plain film

Variable

Calcification of peripheral arteries, common with age, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear, tram-track calcification following the course of peripheral arteries (femoral, popliteal, tibial).

Differential
  • Atherosclerotic calcification
  • Medial arterial calcification (Monckeberg sclerosis, associated with diabetes/CKD, does not necessarily indicate luminal stenosis)
Next Step

Correlate with peripheral pulses and symptoms; further vascular workup (ABI, duplex) if clinically indicated.

Benign

Correlate with thoracic outlet syndrome symptoms; vascular surgery referral if symptomatic.

Thyroid & Parathyroid

5 findings

1. Thyroid nodule, MRI characterization

Variable

Focal thyroid lesion, with MRI occasionally used for further characterization though ultrasound remains the primary modality.

Imaging Characteristics

Variable T1/T2 signal depending on nodule composition (cystic, solid, hemorrhagic); MRI is not the primary modality for thyroid nodule risk stratification.

Differential
  • Benign colloid nodule
  • Thyroid adenoma
  • Thyroid carcinoma (cannot be reliably excluded by MRI alone)
Next Step

Dedicated thyroid ultrasound for standardized risk stratification (TI-RADS) and biopsy planning.

2. Thyroid carcinoma, MRI local staging

Concerning

Thyroid malignancy, with MRI used for local staging when extrathyroidal extension or invasion of adjacent structures (trachea, esophagus, recurrent laryngeal nerve) is a concern.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, infiltrative thyroid mass with loss of the normal capsule and, in locally advanced disease, extension into the trachea, esophagus, or great vessels.

Differential
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma (most common)
  • Other thyroid carcinoma subtypes
Next Step

Endocrine/head and neck oncology referral for staging and surgical planning.

3. Parathyroid adenoma, MRI localization

Variable

Benign parathyroid tumor, occasionally localized with MRI when 4D-CT or ultrasound/sestamibi are inconclusive.

Imaging Characteristics

Small nodule posterior to the thyroid gland, typically T2-hyperintense and enhancing, distinguishing it from adjacent lymph nodes and thyroid tissue.

Differential
  • Parathyroid adenoma
  • Parathyroid hyperplasia (multiple gland involvement)
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral for parathyroidectomy planning.

4. Riedel thyroiditis / fibrosing thyroiditis

Concerning

Rare, fibrosing form of thyroiditis that can extend beyond the thyroid capsule into adjacent neck structures, associated with IgG4-related disease in some cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, markedly hypointense (fibrotic) thyroid gland on T2, with extension beyond the capsule into adjacent strap muscles and structures.

Differential
  • Riedel thyroiditis (IgG4-related disease association in a subset)
  • Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (key differential given similarly invasive appearance, biopsy needed to distinguish)
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral; biopsy needed to exclude anaplastic carcinoma given overlapping imaging appearance.

5. Thyroglossal duct cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the thyroglossal duct.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense midline cystic lesion at or near the hyoid bone, without a solid enhancing component (unless infected or, rarely, harboring malignancy).

Differential
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (benign)
  • Infected thyroglossal duct cyst (wall thickening/enhancement)
  • Rare malignant transformation (papillary carcinoma arising within the cyst, uncommon)
Next Step

Surgical referral (Sistrunk procedure).

Salivary Gland Tumors

8 findings

1. Pleomorphic adenoma, MRI characterization

Benign

Most common benign salivary gland tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated mass, typically markedly T2-hyperintense with progressive enhancement, most often in the superficial parotid lobe.

Differential
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (benign, characteristic marked T2 hyperintensity)
  • Warthin tumor
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision given growth potential and small malignant transformation risk over time.

2. Warthin tumor, MRI characterization

Benign

Second most common benign parotid tumor, associated with smoking.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined mass, often with cystic components and lower T2 signal than pleomorphic adenoma, most often in the parotid tail, can be bilateral.

Differential
  • Warthin tumor (benign, smoking-associated)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain lesions.

3. Malignant parotid tumor, MRI features

Concerning

Parotid malignancy, with MRI features suggesting aggressive behavior.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, infiltrative margins, low T2 signal (reflecting high cellularity in some subtypes), restricted diffusion, and possible facial nerve involvement (asymmetric enhancement/enlargement of the nerve).

Differential
  • Mucoepidermoid carcinoma (most common salivary malignancy)
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma (notable perineural spread propensity, assess along the facial nerve)
  • Acinic cell carcinoma
Next Step

Surgical oncology/ENT referral; assess the facial nerve course for perineural spread, particularly with adenoid cystic carcinoma.

4. Adenoid cystic carcinoma, perineural spread MRI

Concerning

Salivary gland malignancy with a notable propensity for perineural spread, even in early-stage disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlargement, abnormal enhancement, or effacement of the fat surrounding the facial nerve (or other cranial nerves) tracking proximally toward the skull base.

Differential
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma with perineural spread (assess the full nerve course to the skull base)
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral; MRI skull base protocol to assess the full extent of perineural extension.

5. Submandibular gland tumor, MRI

Variable

Tumor of the submandibular gland, with a higher proportion malignant compared to parotid tumors.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass within the submandibular gland, characterized similarly to parotid tumors (T1/T2 signal, enhancement pattern, margins) though with a higher pretest probability of malignancy.

Differential
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (benign, still most common overall)
  • Malignant submandibular tumor (higher relative proportion than parotid)
Next Step

ENT/surgical oncology referral; biopsy for tissue diagnosis.

6. Sjögren syndrome, MRI parotid changes

Variable

Autoimmune condition causing chronic salivary gland inflammation.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small cysts and heterogeneous signal within the parotid glands bilaterally, described as a 'salt and pepper' or honeycomb pattern.

Differential
  • Sjögren syndrome (correlate with sicca symptoms and autoantibody testing)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; increased lymphoma risk warrants monitoring for a new focal, dominant mass.

7. MALT lymphoma, salivary gland

Concerning

Marginal zone (MALT) lymphoma arising within a salivary gland, with an increased incidence in Sjögren syndrome.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or focal gland enlargement with homogeneous, relatively low T2 signal (reflecting high cellularity) and restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • MALT lymphoma (particularly in a patient with known Sjögren syndrome)
  • Reactive/inflammatory change
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

8. First branchial cleft anomaly, periparotid MRI

Benign

Congenital cystic anomaly involving the periauricular/parotid region, related to first branchial cleft embryology.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic lesion in the periauricular or parotid region, sometimes with a sinus tract toward the external auditory canal.

Differential
  • First branchial cleft cyst/sinus (Type I or Type II based on relationship to the facial nerve and external auditory canal)
Next Step

ENT/surgical referral for excision, with careful attention to the facial nerve course given anatomic proximity.

Larynx & Hypopharynx Cancer Staging

6 findings

1. Laryngeal carcinoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Laryngeal malignancy, with MRI providing superior soft-tissue contrast for cartilage invasion and paraglottic space assessment compared to CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of paraglottic/pre-epiglottic fat invasion, cartilage invasion (best assessed by combining T1/T2 signal changes with CT sclerosis/destruction), and nodal disease.

Differential
  • Glottic, supraglottic, or subglottic carcinoma
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

2. Cartilage invasion assessment, laryngeal cancer MRI

Concerning

Assessment of thyroid/cricoid/arytenoid cartilage invasion by laryngeal cancer, a key factor affecting resectability and treatment approach (larynx preservation vs. laryngectomy).

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal cartilage signal with extension of tumor signal through the cartilage, though non-ossified cartilage can be a diagnostic pitfall (MRI has high sensitivity but variable specificity for this assessment).

Differential
  • Cartilage invasion (T4 disease, affects resectability)
  • Peritumoral inflammation/edema without true invasion (key differential, a recognized cause of false-positive MRI findings)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary head and neck oncology review, correlating MRI with CT and clinical/endoscopic findings.

3. Hypopharyngeal carcinoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Malignancy of the hypopharynx (piriform sinus, postcricoid region, posterior pharyngeal wall), often presenting at an advanced stage given the region's silent symptomatology.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of local extension into the larynx, esophagus, and prevertebral fascia, along with nodal disease (often bilateral given rich lymphatic drainage).

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (most common, often presents late)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

4. Post-treatment larynx, recurrence vs. change

Variable

Distinguishing expected post-treatment (radiation/chemoradiation) change from tumor recurrence in the larynx, a common diagnostic challenge.

Imaging Characteristics

Post-treatment change: diffuse edema/fibrosis without a discrete mass, gradual improvement on serial imaging. Recurrence: new or enlarging discrete mass with restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Expected post-treatment change (edema, fibrosis)
  • Local tumor recurrence
Next Step

Serial imaging and correlation with endoscopic findings; PET-CT often complementary for recurrence assessment.

5. Laryngocele, MRI

Variable

Air- or fluid-filled dilation of the laryngeal saccule.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic/air-filled structure arising from the laryngeal ventricle, internal or extending externally through the thyrohyoid membrane.

Differential
  • Laryngocele (benign)
  • Association with an obstructing laryngeal tumor (important to exclude in adults)
Next Step

ENT referral; exclude an underlying obstructing lesion, particularly in adult-onset cases.

6. Vocal cord/laryngeal cyst or polyp, MRI

Benign

Benign vocal cord lesion, from chronic voice strain or reactive change.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, well-defined lesion on the vocal cord, with signal characteristics depending on cystic vs. more solid/fibrotic composition.

Differential
  • Vocal cord cyst
  • Vocal cord polyp/nodule (often bilateral with chronic voice abuse)
Next Step

ENT referral for laryngoscopic evaluation and voice therapy or surgical management as indicated.

6 findings

1. Oral tongue carcinoma, MRI depth of invasion

Concerning

Oral cavity malignancy, with MRI used to measure depth of invasion, a key staging parameter that has replaced tumor thickness in current staging systems.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based tongue mass with MRI measurement of depth of invasion from the adjacent normal mucosal surface, along with assessment of midline/contralateral extension and mandibular involvement.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (most common oral cavity malignancy)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging (depth of invasion affects nodal staging and treatment) and treatment planning.

2. Floor of mouth carcinoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Oral cavity malignancy of the floor of mouth, assessed for extension into the tongue, mandible, and sublingual/submandibular spaces.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of extension into adjacent structures and mandibular cortical/marrow invasion.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

3. Mandibular invasion, oral cavity cancer MRI

Concerning

Assessment of mandibular bone invasion by an adjacent oral cavity malignancy, affecting surgical approach (marginal vs. segmental mandibulectomy).

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal low-signal cortical margin with abnormal marrow signal extending into the mandible from an adjacent soft-tissue mass.

Differential
  • Mandibular invasion (affects surgical planning)
  • Reactive periosteal change without true invasion
Next Step

Multidisciplinary head and neck oncology review for surgical planning.

4. Oropharyngeal (tonsil/tongue base) carcinoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Oropharyngeal malignancy, increasingly HPV-associated, with MRI assessing local extent and characteristic cystic nodal metastases.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of deep muscle (tongue base) invasion; HPV-associated disease often shows small primary tumors with disproportionately large, often cystic nodal metastases.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma (HPV-associated subtype increasingly common, generally better prognosis)
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging (including p16/HPV testing) and treatment planning.

5. Buccal/gingival carcinoma, MRI staging

Concerning

Oral cavity malignancy of the buccal mucosa or gingiva.

Imaging Characteristics

Mucosal-based mass with assessment of local extension, including into the buccal fat pad, mandible/maxilla, and masticator space.

Differential
  • Squamous cell carcinoma
Next Step

ENT/head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

6. Minor salivary gland tumor, oral cavity/palate

Variable

Tumor arising from minor salivary gland tissue, most often in the hard/soft palate, with a higher proportion malignant than major salivary gland tumors.

Imaging Characteristics

Submucosal mass, most often at the palate, with imaging characteristics that overlap between benign and malignant subtypes.

Differential
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (benign, still relatively common at this site)
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma or mucoepidermoid carcinoma (malignant, relatively higher proportion than major gland tumors)
Next Step

ENT/oral surgery referral for biopsy and staging.

Deep Neck Infection

6 findings

1. Deep neck space abscess, MRI

Concerning

Collection of pus within a deep neck fascial space, with MRI (particularly DWI) improving distinction between a true drainable abscess and phlegmon/cellulitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing collection with restricted diffusion, distinguishing a true abscess from the more diffuse, non-restricting enhancement of phlegmon/cellulitis.

Differential
  • Deep neck space abscess (drainable)
  • Phlegmon/cellulitis (non-drainable, managed with antibiotics alone)
Next Step

Emergent ENT consultation for airway assessment and drainage of a confirmed abscess.

2. Osteomyelitis, mandible/skull base MRI

Concerning

Bone infection of the mandible or skull base, often from contiguous odontogenic or otogenic spread.

Imaging Characteristics

Marrow edema with enhancement, often adjacent to a soft-tissue infectious source, with cortical destruction in more advanced disease.

Differential
  • Mandibular osteomyelitis (odontogenic source)
  • Skull base osteomyelitis (otogenic source, classically in diabetic or immunocompromised patients, 'malignant otitis externa')
Next Step

ENT/infectious disease referral; prolonged antibiotics and source control.

3. Skull base osteomyelitis (malignant otitis externa)

Concerning

Severe, invasive infection of the skull base, classically arising from external otitis in diabetic or immunocompromised patients, historically termed 'malignant' otitis externa despite being infectious rather than neoplastic.

Imaging Characteristics

Marrow edema and enhancement at the skull base, often with extension along the course of adjacent cranial nerves (particularly the facial nerve), correlate with external auditory canal soft-tissue changes.

Differential
  • Skull base osteomyelitis (Pseudomonas aeruginosa most common organism)
Next Step

Emergent ENT/infectious disease consultation; prolonged targeted antibiotics given high morbidity if inadequately treated.

4. Cervical necrotizing fasciitis, MRI

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening necrotizing soft-tissue infection of the neck.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive fascial plane T2-hyperintense signal with fluid tracking, muscle edema, and variable soft-tissue gas (susceptibility artifact).

Differential
  • Cervical necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent surgical debridement; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation.

5. Sialadenitis with abscess, MRI

Concerning

Salivary gland infection with abscess formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, restricted-diffusion fluid collection within or adjacent to an inflamed salivary gland.

Differential
  • Salivary gland abscess
Next Step

IR or surgical drainage plus antibiotics.

6. Lemierre syndrome, MRI/MRV

Concerning

Septic thrombophlebitis of the internal jugular vein, classically following oropharyngeal infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the internal jugular vein on MR venography, with surrounding inflammatory change and often an adjacent primary infectious source.

Differential
  • Lemierre syndrome (septic IJ thrombophlebitis)
Next Step

Anticoagulation and prolonged antibiotics; screen for septic pulmonary emboli.

Cervical Lymph Node Characterization

6 findings

1. Reactive lymph node, MRI features

Benign

Benign, reactive lymph node enlargement.

Imaging Characteristics

Oval shape, preserved fatty hilum, homogeneous signal, and smooth margins without restricted diffusion or necrosis.

Differential
  • Reactive lymphadenopathy
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; typically no isolated workup needed.

2. Metastatic lymph node, MRI features

Concerning

Nodal metastasis, most often from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded shape, loss of fatty hilum, central necrosis (non-enhancing, often restricted diffusion at the periphery), and/or extranodal extension (irregular margins infiltrating surrounding fat).

Differential
  • Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma
  • Metastatic thyroid or other primary
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral for primary tumor search and biopsy.

3. Extranodal extension, MRI assessment

Concerning

Tumor spread beyond the lymph node capsule into surrounding soft tissue, an important adverse prognostic factor affecting treatment intensity.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, spiculated nodal margins with infiltration into adjacent fat/muscle, loss of the normal smooth nodal capsule.

Differential
  • Extranodal extension (upstages disease and often prompts more aggressive adjuvant treatment)
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral; affects treatment planning (often warrants adjuvant chemoradiation).

4. Cervical lymphoma, MRI features

Concerning

Lymphomatous cervical lymph node involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple enlarged, often homogeneous nodes without the necrosis typical of squamous cell carcinoma metastases, sometimes matted together with restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Next Step

Oncology referral for biopsy (excisional preferred) and staging.

5. Tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis, MRI

Concerning

Mycobacterial cervical lymph node infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged nodes with central necrosis (T2-hyperintense, non-enhancing) and peripheral rim enhancement, often matted, with surrounding soft-tissue edema.

Differential
  • Tuberculous lymphadenitis
  • Nontuberculous mycobacterial infection
  • Necrotic metastatic node
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation.

6. Unknown primary with cervical nodal metastasis, MRI search

Concerning

Metastatic cervical lymphadenopathy without an identified primary tumor on initial workup, prompting a systematic imaging and endoscopic search.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodal metastasis with imaging survey of the likely mucosal primary sites (nasopharynx, tonsil, tongue base, piriform sinus) for a subtle or occult primary lesion.

Differential
  • Occult mucosal primary (often tonsil or tongue base, particularly if HPV-associated)
  • True unknown primary (a minority remain unidentified despite thorough workup)
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral for panendoscopy with directed biopsies (including tonsillectomy) and PET-CT.

Neck Masses - Congenital

6 findings

1. Branchial cleft cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the branchial apparatus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense cystic lesion along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (second branchial cleft cyst, most common), without a solid enhancing component unless infected.

Differential
  • Second branchial cleft cyst (most common)
  • Cystic nodal metastasis (important differential in an older adult with a new lateral neck cyst, particularly from HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision; exclude cystic metastasis in older adults given overlapping imaging appearance.

2. Lymphatic malformation, neck MRI

Benign

Congenital lymphatic malformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiloculated, T2-hyperintense cystic mass extending across fascial planes and multiple neck spaces, with fluid-fluid levels if there has been internal hemorrhage.

Differential
  • Macrocystic lymphatic malformation
  • Microcystic or mixed lymphatic malformation
Next Step

Surgical/IR referral for sclerotherapy or resection, particularly if causing airway compromise.

3. Dermoid/epidermoid cyst, neck MRI

Benign

Congenital inclusion cyst, often midline.

Imaging Characteristics

Dermoid: heterogeneous signal, may contain fat. Epidermoid: more homogeneous fluid signal with restricted diffusion on DWI (key distinguishing feature from a simple cyst).

Differential
  • Dermoid cyst
  • Epidermoid cyst (restricted diffusion)
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (key differential if near the hyoid)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision.

4. Torticollis, congenital muscular MRI

Benign

Fibrosis and shortening of the sternocleidomastoid muscle in infants.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform enlargement of the sternocleidomastoid muscle with heterogeneous signal reflecting fibrous replacement.

Differential
  • Congenital muscular torticollis (benign, self-limited in most cases)
Next Step

Pediatric physical therapy referral; most cases resolve with conservative management.

5. Cervical thymic cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant along the path of thymic descent.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense cystic lesion along the expected course of thymic descent.

Differential
  • Cervical thymic cyst
  • Branchial cleft cyst (key differential based on location)
Next Step

Surgical referral if symptomatic or diagnosis uncertain.

6. Fibromatosis colli

Benign

Alternate term for the sternocleidomastoid pseudotumor of infancy associated with congenital muscular torticollis.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform sternocleidomastoid muscle enlargement in an infant, typically presenting in the first few weeks of life, with signal characteristics reflecting fibrous tissue.

Differential
  • Fibromatosis colli (benign, self-limited)
Next Step

Pediatric physical therapy referral; excellent prognosis with conservative stretching therapy.

Skull Base & Perineural Spread

5 findings

1. Perineural tumor spread, general MRI assessment

Concerning

Tumor extension along a cranial nerve, a critical staging consideration for several head and neck malignancies, particularly adenoid cystic carcinoma and cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Nerve enlargement, abnormal enhancement, and effacement of the normal perineural fat, tracked along the nerve's course to the skull base foramen of exit/entry.

Differential
  • Perineural spread from adenoid cystic carcinoma
  • Perineural spread from cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (particularly along the trigeminal or facial nerve)
  • Perineural spread from other head and neck malignancies
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral; assess the full nerve course to the skull base and beyond for staging.

2. Jugular foramen mass, MRI

Variable

Mass arising within or near the jugular foramen, from paraganglioma, schwannoma, or meningioma.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass centered on the jugular foramen with characteristic features depending on histology: paraganglioma shows a 'salt and pepper' appearance from flow voids, schwannoma shows a more homogeneous, well-defined enhancing mass.

Differential
  • Glomus jugulare paraganglioma (most common)
  • Jugular foramen schwannoma
  • Meningioma
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgery referral for further characterization and treatment planning.

3. Skull base invasion by nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Concerning

Direct extension of nasopharyngeal carcinoma into the skull base, common given the tumor's proximity to this region.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal marrow signal replacing the normal fatty marrow of the skull base, with extension through neural foramina in more advanced disease.

Differential
  • Nasopharyngeal carcinoma with skull base invasion (affects staging)
Next Step

Head and neck oncology referral for staging and treatment planning (typically radiation-based given the anatomic location).

4. Meckel cave mass, MRI

Variable

Mass involving Meckel cave, at the trigeminal nerve root entry zone, from trigeminal schwannoma, meningioma, or perineural tumor spread.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass centered on or extending into Meckel cave, with characteristics depending on the underlying histology.

Differential
  • Trigeminal schwannoma
  • Meningioma
  • Perineural spread from a distal head/neck malignancy
Next Step

Neurosurgery/ENT referral for further characterization.

5. Glomus tympanicum/jugulare paraganglioma

Variable

Paraganglioma arising in the middle ear (tympanicum) or at the jugular foramen (jugulare), presenting with pulsatile tinnitus.

Imaging Characteristics

Avidly enhancing mass with a characteristic 'salt and pepper' appearance from internal flow voids, centered on the middle ear promontory (tympanicum) or jugular foramen (jugulare).

Differential
  • Glomus tympanicum (confined to the middle ear)
  • Glomus jugulare (centered on the jugular foramen, can extend into the middle ear)
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgery referral; catheter angiography with possible preoperative embolization for surgical planning.

Vascular - Carotid & Vertebral

7 findings

1. Carotid/vertebral artery dissection, MRI/MRA

Concerning

Arterial wall tear, well characterized by MRI given the ability to directly visualize the intramural hematoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic, T1-hyperintense (on fat-saturated sequences) intramural hematoma narrowing the true lumen, best seen on axial imaging through the neck.

Differential
  • Traumatic dissection
  • Spontaneous dissection (connective tissue disorder, minor trauma, cervical manipulation)
Next Step

Neurology/vascular referral; antithrombotic therapy to reduce embolic stroke risk. See also the neuro atlas for detailed characterization.

2. Carotid vessel wall imaging, plaque characterization

Variable

Advanced MRI technique characterizing carotid plaque composition, which can add information beyond luminal stenosis degree for stroke risk stratification.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of plaque components including a lipid-rich necrotic core, intraplaque hemorrhage (T1-hyperintense), and a thin/ruptured fibrous cap, features associated with plaque vulnerability.

Differential
  • Stable plaque (thick fibrous cap, no hemorrhage)
  • Vulnerable/high-risk plaque (intraplaque hemorrhage, thin/ruptured cap)
Next Step

Vascular neurology referral; plaque characteristics can inform management even in the setting of only moderate luminal stenosis.

3. Vertebral artery hypoplasia

Benign

Congenital small caliber of one vertebral artery, a common normal variant.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely reduced caliber of one vertebral artery compared to the contralateral side, without focal stenosis or an abrupt transition.

Differential
  • Vertebral artery hypoplasia (normal variant, usually the left vertebral artery is dominant)
Next Step

No action needed for an isolated, asymptomatic finding.

4. Fibromuscular dysplasia, carotid MRA

Variable

Non-atherosclerotic vasculopathy causing a characteristic 'string of beads' appearance in the carotid arteries.

Imaging Characteristics

Alternating areas of stenosis and mild dilation creating a 'string of beads' appearance, typically in the mid-to-distal cervical internal carotid artery.

Differential
  • Fibromuscular dysplasia (correlate with stroke/TIA symptoms)
Next Step

Vascular medicine/neurology referral; screen for associated aneurysm and dissection.

5. Carotid body tumor, MRI/MRA

Variable

Vascular tumor at the carotid bifurcation.

Imaging Characteristics

Avidly enhancing, hypervascular mass with characteristic flow voids ('salt and pepper' appearance), splaying the internal and external carotid arteries.

Differential
  • Carotid body tumor (paraganglioma)
Next Step

ENT/vascular surgery referral; catheter angiography with possible preoperative embolization for surgical planning.

6. Carotid or vertebral artery occlusion, MRA

Concerning

Complete occlusion of the carotid or vertebral artery, from thrombosis, embolism, or dissection.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent flow signal within the vessel on MRA, with the extent and collateral pattern relevant to stroke risk and management.

Differential
  • Chronic occlusion (may be well-tolerated with adequate collateral flow)
  • Acute occlusion (higher stroke risk, time-critical)
Next Step

Correlate with acuity and stroke symptoms; neurology/vascular consultation.

7. Cervical carotid artery aneurysm

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of the extracranial carotid artery, uncommon.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation of the carotid artery, from atherosclerotic, traumatic, mycotic, or dissection-related causes.

Differential
  • Atherosclerotic aneurysm
  • Post-traumatic pseudoaneurysm
  • Mycotic aneurysm
  • Post-dissection aneurysmal dilation
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for surveillance or repair based on size and etiology.

Vascular Malformations, Soft Tissue

6 findings

1. Venous malformation, soft tissue MRI

Benign

Congenital low-flow venous malformation, which can occur in any soft-tissue location including the head/neck and extremities.

Imaging Characteristics

Lobulated, markedly T2-hyperintense (fluid-like, slow-flow) lesion, compressible, often with phleboliths (low-signal foci) as a relatively specific finding.

Differential
  • Venous malformation (benign)
Next Step

IR referral for sclerotherapy if symptomatic.

2. Lymphatic malformation, soft tissue MRI

Benign

Congenital lymphatic malformation, macrocystic, microcystic, or mixed.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiloculated, T2-hyperintense cystic spaces without significant flow on Doppler correlation, with fluid-fluid levels if internal hemorrhage has occurred.

Differential
  • Macrocystic lymphatic malformation
  • Microcystic lymphatic malformation (less discrete cystic spaces, more infiltrative appearance)
Next Step

IR/surgical referral for sclerotherapy or resection.

3. Arteriovenous malformation, soft tissue MRI

Concerning

High-flow congenital vascular malformation with direct arteriovenous shunting.

Imaging Characteristics

Tangle of flow voids on conventional sequences, with early venous filling and arterialized flow on dynamic contrast-enhanced or MRA sequences.

Differential
  • Soft tissue arteriovenous malformation
Next Step

IR referral for embolization; combined surgical/endovascular management for complex lesions.

4. Hemangioma, infantile MRI

Benign

Benign vascular tumor of infancy, distinct from a congenital vascular malformation, with a characteristic proliferative-then-involuting natural history.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated, intensely enhancing soft-tissue mass with flow voids, most prominent during the proliferative phase (first year of life) before gradual involution.

Differential
  • Infantile hemangioma (benign, characteristic growth pattern distinguishes it from a congenital vascular malformation)
Next Step

Pediatric dermatology/vascular anomaly referral; propranolol is a first-line treatment for problematic infantile hemangiomas.

5. Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome, MRI

Variable

Congenital syndrome combining capillary malformation, venous/lymphatic malformation, and limb overgrowth.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse venous and/or lymphatic malformation involving an extremity, with associated soft-tissue and osseous overgrowth and persistent embryonic veins.

Differential
  • Klippel-Trenaunay syndrome
Next Step

Multidisciplinary vascular anomaly team referral.

6. PHACE syndrome, associated vascular anomalies

Variable

Syndrome associating a large facial infantile hemangioma with posterior fossa malformation, cerebrovascular anomalies, cardiac defects, and eye abnormalities.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, segmental facial infantile hemangioma with associated intracranial arterial anomalies (best assessed with MRA) and posterior fossa malformations.

Differential
  • PHACE syndrome (warrants multi-organ system screening)
Next Step

Multidisciplinary evaluation including neurology, cardiology, and ophthalmology given the multi-system associations.

Brachial Plexus

4 findings

1. Brachial plexus traumatic injury, MRI

Concerning

Traumatic brachial plexus injury, from a range of mechanisms including birth trauma, motorcycle/traction injuries, and penetrating trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Nerve root avulsion (pseudomeningocele formation at the nerve root sleeve), or post-ganglionic injury with nerve swelling/signal change along the plexus trunks/cords.

Differential
  • Preganglionic root avulsion (poor spontaneous recovery potential, may need nerve transfer surgery)
  • Postganglionic injury (better spontaneous recovery potential)
Next Step

Neurosurgery/peripheral nerve surgery referral; the pre- vs. postganglionic distinction is critical for surgical planning and prognosis.

2. Brachial plexus tumor infiltration

Concerning

Tumor involvement of the brachial plexus, from a primary nerve sheath tumor or invasion by an adjacent malignancy (Pancoast tumor, breast cancer recurrence).

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal T2-hyperintense signal and/or mass effect involving the brachial plexus, correlate with an adjacent primary malignancy if present.

Differential
  • Pancoast tumor invasion (see also the chest atlas)
  • Metastatic/recurrent breast cancer involvement (classic in the post-radiation, post-mastectomy setting)
  • Primary nerve sheath tumor
Next Step

Multidisciplinary oncology referral for staging and treatment planning.

3. Radiation-induced brachial plexopathy

Variable

Delayed brachial plexus injury from prior radiation therapy, distinguished from tumor recurrence by its diffuse, symmetric pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, symmetric T2 signal change and fibrotic thickening throughout the plexus within the radiation field, without a discrete enhancing mass.

Differential
  • Radiation fibrosis (benign, expected delayed treatment effect)
  • Tumor recurrence (discrete mass, key differential)
Next Step

Correlate with radiation history and field; serial imaging if a discrete new mass is identified.

4. Thoracic outlet syndrome, brachial plexus MRI

Variable

Compression of the brachial plexus at the thoracic outlet.

Imaging Characteristics

Positional narrowing of the space between the clavicle and first rib on provocative imaging, sometimes with an associated cervical rib or fibrous band compressing the plexus.

Differential
  • Neurogenic thoracic outlet syndrome
Next Step

Vascular/thoracic surgery referral for symptomatic cases; see also the chest atlas.

Imaging Characteristics

Luminal narrowing or occlusion on time-of-flight or contrast-enhanced MRA, though calcification (well seen on CT) is not directly visualized on MRA, which can occasionally overestimate stenosis severity.

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of the popliteal artery.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation of the popliteal artery, often with mural thrombus (which may be underestimated in size on MRA given flow-related signal characteristics compared to CT).

Differential
  • Popliteal artery aneurysm

Vascular surgery referral; screen for bilateral involvement and AAA.

3. Vascular malformation, extremity MRA

Benign

Congenital vascular malformation of an extremity, characterized with combined MRI/MRA for both morphology and flow characteristics.

Imaging Characteristics

Low-flow (venous, lymphatic) or high-flow (arteriovenous) malformation, characterized by combining conventional MRI sequences with dynamic MRA.

Differential
  • Venous malformation
  • Lymphatic malformation
  • Arteriovenous malformation

IR/vascular surgery referral for symptomatic lesions.

4. Deep vein thrombosis, MR venography

Concerning

Clot within a deep vein, occasionally assessed by MR venography for central veins (iliac, IVC) where ultrasound access is limited.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within the vein lumen with associated vein enlargement.

Differential
  • Acute DVT
  • Chronic DVT (recanalized vein with wall thickening)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; ultrasound remains first-line for extremity DVT, with MRV reserved for central/pelvic vein assessment.

4 findings

Variable

Soft-tissue infection without a drainable collection, with MRI used to exclude a deeper abscess or necrotizing process when clinical exam is equivocal.

Imaging Characteristics

Soft-tissue and subcutaneous T2 hyperintensity/edema with skin thickening, without a discrete rim-enhancing fluid collection.

Differential
  • Simple cellulitis (no drainable collection)
  • Underlying abscess (if a discrete collection is identified)
Next Step

Antibiotics for simple cellulitis; drainage if a discrete abscess is identified.

2. Soft tissue abscess, MRI general

Concerning

Localized collection of pus, with MRI showing high sensitivity via restricted diffusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense fluid collection with central restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Soft tissue abscess
Next Step

Incision and drainage plus antibiotics.

3. Necrotizing fasciitis, MRI general

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection with fascial plane involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive fascial thickening and T2-hyperintense signal tracking along fascial planes, with variable soft-tissue gas.

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation.

4. Pyomyositis, MRI general

Concerning

Bacterial infection of skeletal muscle forming an intramuscular abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse muscle edema progressing to a rim-enhancing, centrally necrotic intramuscular collection.

Differential
  • Pyomyositis (Staphylococcus aureus most common)
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage for a discrete abscess plus antibiotics.

Lymphatic Malformations & Lymphedema

5 findings

1. Lymphedema, extremity MRI

Variable

Accumulation of lymphatic fluid in the soft tissues from impaired lymphatic drainage, primary (congenital) or secondary (post-surgical, post-radiation, filarial infection).

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse subcutaneous edema with a characteristic 'honeycomb' pattern of thickened, edematous septae, sparing the deep muscular compartments in typical lymphedema.

Differential
  • Primary lymphedema (congenital lymphatic dysplasia)
  • Secondary lymphedema (post-lymph node dissection, post-radiation, most common cause in developed countries)
Next Step

Lymphedema therapy (compression, manual lymphatic drainage); vascular anomaly/lymphedema specialist referral for refractory cases.

2. Lipedema vs. lymphedema, MRI distinction

Variable

Distinguishing lipedema (symmetric fat deposition, typically sparing the feet) from lymphedema, an important clinical distinction with differing management.

Imaging Characteristics

Lipedema shows symmetric subcutaneous fat deposition without the honeycomb edema pattern or lymphatic dilation seen in lymphedema, and characteristically spares the feet.

Differential
  • Lipedema (fat deposition disorder, feet spared)
  • Lymphedema (true lymphatic fluid accumulation, honeycomb pattern, can involve the feet)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam (foot involvement, tissue texture); referral to a lymphedema specialist for confirmed lymphedema.

3. Lymphangiosarcoma (Stewart-Treves syndrome)

Concerning

Rare, aggressive malignancy arising in the setting of chronic longstanding lymphedema, classically post-mastectomy.

Imaging Characteristics

New, enhancing, infiltrative soft-tissue mass arising within a background of chronic lymphedema, with restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Lymphangiosarcoma (rare but important to recognize given aggressive behavior and poor prognosis)
Next Step

Urgent oncology referral for biopsy given the aggressive nature of this malignancy.

4. Lymphocele, post-surgical MRI

Variable

Localized collection of lymphatic fluid, typically post-surgical (particularly after lymph node dissection or vascular/transplant surgery).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense fluid collection without a discrete enhancing wall, in the expected location of prior surgical dissection.

Differential
  • Post-surgical lymphocele (benign, common after lymph node dissection)
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively (observation); drainage or sclerotherapy for symptomatic or persistently enlarging collections.

5. Filariasis, lymphatic MRI changes

Variable

Parasitic infection (Wuchereria bancrofti and related species) causing chronic lymphatic obstruction and lymphedema, endemic in certain tropical/subtropical regions.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated lymphatic channels with chronic soft-tissue and skin thickening (elephantiasis in advanced disease), most often affecting the lower extremities and genitalia.

Differential
  • Lymphatic filariasis (correlate with endemic area travel/residence history)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; antiparasitic therapy and lymphedema management.

Thyroid Nodules & Disease

14 findings

1. Thyroid nodule, TI-RADS benign features

Benign

Thyroid nodule with sonographic features associated with low malignant risk, categorized using the ACR TI-RADS system.

Imaging Characteristics

Purely cystic or spongiform nodule, isoechoic or hyperechoic to the surrounding parenchyma, smooth margins, no suspicious features (no microcalcifications, no taller-than-wide shape, no extrathyroidal extension).

Differential
  • TI-RADS 1-2 (benign to not suspicious)
Next Step

No biopsy needed for TI-RADS 1-2 nodules regardless of size, or size-based follow-up per ACR TI-RADS guidelines for higher categories.

2. Thyroid nodule, TI-RADS suspicious features

Concerning

Thyroid nodule with sonographic features associated with elevated malignant risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic or very hypoechoic solid nodule with one or more suspicious features: microcalcifications, taller-than-wide shape, irregular/lobulated margins, or extrathyroidal extension.

Differential
  • TI-RADS 4-5 (moderately to highly suspicious)
Next Step

Fine needle aspiration biopsy per ACR TI-RADS size and category-based criteria.

3. Thyroid microcalcifications

Concerning

Punctate echogenic foci within a thyroid nodule, a suspicious sonographic feature associated with papillary thyroid carcinoma (psammoma bodies).

Imaging Characteristics

Tiny (<1 mm), punctate echogenic foci without posterior acoustic shadowing, distinct from the larger, shadowing coarse calcifications of a benign nodule.

Differential
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma (classic association with microcalcifications)
Next Step

Fine needle aspiration biopsy given the suspicious nature of this finding.

4. Multinodular goiter, ultrasound

Variable

Enlarged thyroid gland with multiple nodules, extremely common, usually benign.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, heterogeneous thyroid gland with multiple nodules of varying size and echogenicity.

Differential
  • Multinodular goiter (benign, most common)
Next Step

Risk-stratify and biopsy the most suspicious nodule(s) per TI-RADS criteria; endocrine referral.

5. Thyroid cyst, simple

Benign

Purely cystic thyroid lesion, benign.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, well-circumscribed lesion with posterior acoustic enhancement and no solid component.

Differential
  • Simple colloid cyst (benign)
Next Step

No biopsy needed regardless of size for a purely cystic (TI-RADS 1) lesion.

6. Graves disease, ultrasound

Variable

Autoimmune hyperthyroidism causing diffuse thyroid gland changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, hypoechoic, heterogeneous thyroid gland with markedly increased vascularity on color Doppler ('thyroid inferno').

Differential
  • Graves disease (correlate with hyperthyroid symptoms and TSH receptor antibodies)
Next Step

Endocrine referral; correlate with thyroid function tests and TSH receptor antibody testing.

7. Hashimoto thyroiditis, ultrasound

Variable

Chronic autoimmune thyroiditis, the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient regions.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely heterogeneous, hypoechoic thyroid gland with a coarsened, micronodular echotexture, sometimes with fibrous septations creating a 'giraffe pattern.'

Differential
  • Hashimoto thyroiditis (correlate with anti-TPO antibodies and TSH)
Next Step

Endocrine referral; increased lymphoma risk (though still rare) warrants attention to any new discrete, rapidly growing mass.

8. Subacute (de Quervain) thyroiditis, ultrasound

Variable

Painful, self-limited thyroiditis, often post-viral.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, hypoechoic area(s) within the thyroid gland with reduced vascularity on Doppler, correlating with the painful, tender area on exam.

Differential
  • Subacute (de Quervain) thyroiditis (self-limited, often post-viral)
Next Step

Supportive care (NSAIDs, sometimes corticosteroids for severe cases); typically resolves over weeks to months.

9. Thyroid nodule, spongiform pattern

Benign

Thyroid nodule composed predominantly of small cystic spaces, a pattern strongly associated with benignity.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodule composed of multiple small cystic spaces comprising more than 50% of the nodule volume, giving a 'spongiform' appearance.

Differential
  • Spongiform nodule (benign, TI-RADS 2, very low malignant risk)
Next Step

No biopsy needed regardless of size given the very low malignant risk of this pattern.

10. Papillary thyroid carcinoma, ultrasound features

Concerning

Most common thyroid malignancy, with characteristic sonographic features.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic, solid nodule with irregular margins, microcalcifications, and/or a taller-than-wide shape on transverse imaging; may show suspicious cervical lymphadenopathy.

Differential
  • Papillary thyroid carcinoma (most common thyroid malignancy)
Next Step

Fine needle aspiration biopsy and endocrine/surgical oncology referral.

11. Medullary thyroid carcinoma, ultrasound

Concerning

Thyroid malignancy arising from parafollicular (C) cells, associated with MEN2 syndrome in a subset of cases.

Imaging Characteristics

Solid, hypoechoic nodule, often with coarse calcifications, without imaging features that reliably distinguish it from other malignant nodule types.

Differential
  • Medullary thyroid carcinoma (correlate with calcitonin level and MEN2/RET mutation screening if family history present)
Next Step

Fine needle aspiration biopsy; screen for MEN2 syndrome and pheochromocytoma if confirmed.

12. Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma, ultrasound

Concerning

Highly aggressive, rapidly growing thyroid malignancy, typically in older adults, often with a poor prognosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, rapidly growing, heterogeneous, invasive mass with irregular margins and often extrathyroidal extension at presentation.

Differential
  • Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (rapid growth in an older patient is a red flag)
Next Step

Urgent endocrine/head and neck oncology referral given the aggressive, rapidly progressive nature.

13. Thyroid lymphoma, ultrasound

Concerning

Primary thyroid lymphoma, uncommon, with an increased incidence in patients with underlying Hashimoto thyroiditis.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly hypoechoic, often rapidly enlarging mass within a background of Hashimoto thyroiditis changes.

Differential
  • Thyroid lymphoma (particularly with known Hashimoto thyroiditis and rapid gland enlargement)
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral for biopsy and staging.

14. Ultrasound-guided thyroid fine needle aspiration

Variable

Image-guided procedure for tissue sampling of a thyroid nodule.

Imaging Characteristics

Real-time visualization of needle placement within the target nodule for cytologic sampling.

Differential
  • Procedural guidance rather than a diagnostic finding
Next Step

Cytopathology interpretation per the Bethesda classification system to guide further management.

Parathyroid

4 findings

1. Parathyroid adenoma, ultrasound localization

Variable

Benign parathyroid tumor, localized with ultrasound as a complementary study to sestamibi scintigraphy or 4D-CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, homogeneously hypoechoic, oval nodule posterior to the thyroid gland, typically with a polar feeding vessel on Doppler.

Differential
  • Parathyroid adenoma (most common cause of primary hyperparathyroidism)
  • Thyroid nodule (can be mistaken for a parathyroid adenoma)
  • Cervical lymph node
Next Step

Correlate with sestamibi scan or 4D-CT for concordant localization; endocrine/surgical referral for parathyroidectomy planning.

2. Parathyroid hyperplasia, ultrasound

Variable

Enlargement of multiple parathyroid glands, seen in secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism or multiglandular primary disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple enlarged, hypoechoic parathyroid glands, distinct from the single-gland involvement typical of a solitary adenoma.

Differential
  • Secondary hyperparathyroidism (renal failure-related)
  • Multiglandular primary hyperparathyroidism
Next Step

Endocrine/surgical referral; correlate with renal function and calcium/PTH levels.

3. Parathyroid cyst

Benign

Uncommon benign cystic lesion of the parathyroid gland.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic cystic lesion in the expected parathyroid location, distinct from the typically solid appearance of an adenoma.

Differential
  • Parathyroid cyst (usually non-functional, though functioning cysts can occur)
Next Step

Correlate with calcium/PTH levels; aspiration can be both diagnostic and therapeutic.

4. Parathyroid carcinoma, ultrasound

Concerning

Rare malignant parathyroid tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, often lobulated, heterogeneous parathyroid mass, larger than the typical adenoma, sometimes with local invasion.

Differential
  • Parathyroid carcinoma (rare)
  • Large atypical adenoma
Next Step

Surgical oncology referral; correlate with markedly elevated calcium and PTH levels.

Salivary Glands

8 findings

1. Sialolithiasis, ultrasound

Variable

Salivary duct stone, well detected by ultrasound as a first-line imaging modality.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing within a salivary duct, often with proximal ductal dilation.

Differential
  • Submandibular duct stone (most common)
  • Parotid duct stone
Next Step

ENT referral; sialendoscopy or surgical removal for symptomatic, non-passing stones.

2. Sialadenitis, ultrasound

Concerning

Salivary gland inflammation/infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, hypoechoic, heterogeneous gland with increased vascularity on color Doppler, often with an identifiable obstructing stone.

Differential
  • Acute suppurative sialadenitis
  • Viral sialadenitis (mumps, typically bilateral parotid)
Next Step

Antibiotics for suppurative sialadenitis; identify and address any obstructing stone.

3. Pleomorphic adenoma, ultrasound

Benign

Most common benign salivary gland tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, lobulated, hypoechoic mass with posterior acoustic enhancement, most often in the superficial parotid lobe.

Differential
  • Pleomorphic adenoma (benign)
  • Warthin tumor
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision.

4. Warthin tumor, ultrasound

Benign

Second most common benign parotid tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often heterogeneous/cystic-appearing mass in the parotid tail, with increased vascularity on Doppler.

Differential
  • Warthin tumor (benign, can be bilateral)
Next Step

Surgical referral for symptomatic or diagnostically uncertain lesions.

5. Malignant salivary gland tumor, ultrasound features

Concerning

Salivary gland malignancy, with ultrasound features suggesting aggressive behavior though tissue diagnosis is required.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined margins, irregular shape, and often increased, disorganized vascularity; associated suspicious cervical lymphadenopathy raises further concern.

Differential
  • Mucoepidermoid carcinoma
  • Adenoid cystic carcinoma
  • Other salivary malignancy
Next Step

Ultrasound-guided biopsy and ENT/surgical oncology referral.

6. Sjögren syndrome, ultrasound parotid changes

Variable

Autoimmune condition causing chronic salivary gland changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small hypoechoic areas within the parotid glands bilaterally, giving a heterogeneous, 'honeycomb' appearance.

Differential
  • Sjögren syndrome (correlate with sicca symptoms and autoantibody testing)
Next Step

Rheumatology referral; monitor for a new focal, dominant mass given increased lymphoma risk.

7. Sialocele, ultrasound

Variable

Collection of saliva within the soft tissues, from ductal injury (trauma, surgery).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic to complex fluid collection adjacent to a salivary gland or duct, in the setting of recent trauma or surgery.

Differential
  • Post-traumatic or post-surgical sialocele
Next Step

Conservative management (aspiration, pressure dressing); surgical referral for refractory cases.

8. Parotid abscess, ultrasound

Concerning

Localized collection of pus within the parotid gland, a complication of sialadenitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Complex, hypoechoic to anechoic collection within the gland, with surrounding inflammatory change and increased peripheral vascularity.

Differential
  • Parotid abscess
Next Step

IR or surgical drainage plus antibiotics.

Cervical Lymph Nodes

8 findings

1. Reactive cervical lymph node, ultrasound

Benign

Benign, reactive lymph node enlargement, extremely common.

Imaging Characteristics

Oval shape, preserved echogenic fatty hilum, hilar vascularity on Doppler (vessels entering through the hilum), and a long-axis-to-short-axis ratio >2.

Differential
  • Reactive lymphadenopathy (most common)
Next Step

Correlate with the suspected primary process; typically no isolated workup needed for classic reactive nodes.

2. Metastatic cervical lymph node, ultrasound

Concerning

Nodal metastasis, most often from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma or thyroid carcinoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Rounded shape (long-axis-to-short-axis ratio <2), loss of the echogenic fatty hilum, peripheral or mixed vascularity on Doppler, and sometimes cystic necrosis or microcalcifications (thyroid primary).

Differential
  • Metastatic squamous cell carcinoma
  • Metastatic papillary thyroid carcinoma (classic association with cystic change and microcalcifications)
Next Step

Ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration biopsy; head and neck oncology or endocrine referral.

3. Suppurative cervical lymphadenitis, ultrasound

Concerning

Infected lymph node with central liquefaction/abscess formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged node with central anechoic/hypoechoic areas (necrosis/liquefaction) and surrounding inflammatory change.

Differential
  • Suppurative lymphadenitis with abscess formation
Next Step

Antibiotics; aspiration or drainage for a discrete, drainable abscess.

4. Cervical lymphoma, ultrasound features

Concerning

Lymphomatous lymph node involvement.

Imaging Characteristics

Markedly hypoechoic (pseudocystic appearance), rounded nodes, often with preserved but attenuated hilar vascularity, sometimes matted together.

Differential
  • Hodgkin or non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Next Step

Oncology referral for biopsy (excisional preferred over FNA for lymphoma) and staging.

5. Tuberculous cervical lymphadenitis, ultrasound

Concerning

Mycobacterial cervical lymph node infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged nodes with central necrosis, matting, and sometimes adjacent soft-tissue edema or a cold abscess/sinus tract in chronic disease.

Differential
  • Tuberculous lymphadenitis
  • Nontuberculous mycobacterial infection
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation.

6. Kawasaki disease, cervical lymph node ultrasound

Concerning

Cervical lymphadenopathy as a diagnostic feature of Kawasaki disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Unilateral, often single, enlarged node (>1.5 cm), typically without the necrosis seen in suppurative or malignant nodes.

Differential
  • Kawasaki disease-associated lymphadenopathy (correlate with full clinical criteria)
Next Step

Pediatric cardiology/rheumatology referral; timely treatment given coronary artery aneurysm risk if untreated.

7. Cat scratch disease lymphadenitis, ultrasound

Variable

Regional lymphadenitis from Bartonella henselae infection, classically following a cat scratch/bite.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, often hypoechoic node with variable internal changes ranging from homogeneous enlargement to central necrosis/microabscess formation in more severe cases.

Differential
  • Cat scratch disease (correlate with cat exposure history)
Next Step

Usually self-limited; supportive care, with antibiotics reserved for more severe or systemic disease.

8. Ultrasound-guided cervical lymph node biopsy

Variable

Image-guided procedure for tissue sampling of a cervical lymph node.

Imaging Characteristics

Real-time visualization of needle/core biopsy device placement within the target node.

Differential
  • Procedural guidance rather than a diagnostic finding
Next Step

Cytopathology or histopathology interpretation to guide further management.

Neck Masses - Congenital & Miscellaneous

6 findings

1. Thyroglossal duct cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the thyroglossal duct, the most common congenital neck mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic to hypoechoic (often with low-level internal echoes) midline cystic lesion at or near the hyoid bone, moving with tongue protrusion on real-time imaging.

Differential
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (benign)
  • Infected thyroglossal duct cyst (if complex with surrounding inflammatory change)
Next Step

Surgical referral (Sistrunk procedure); confirm the presence of a normally located thyroid gland before excision.

2. Branchial cleft cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant of the branchial apparatus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic to hypoechoic cystic lesion along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (second branchial cleft cyst, most common).

Differential
  • Second branchial cleft cyst (most common)
  • Cystic nodal metastasis (important differential in an older adult with a new lateral neck cyst)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision; exclude cystic metastasis in older adults.

3. Lymphatic malformation, neck ultrasound

Benign

Congenital lymphatic malformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiloculated, anechoic to complex cystic mass, often with septations, without significant internal flow on Doppler.

Differential
  • Macrocystic lymphatic malformation (cystic hygroma)
  • Microcystic lymphatic malformation (more solid-appearing on ultrasound)
Next Step

Surgical/IR referral for sclerotherapy or resection.

4. Dermoid cyst, neck ultrasound

Benign

Congenital inclusion cyst, often midline.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic lesion, sometimes with internal echogenic foci from fat or debris.

Differential
  • Dermoid cyst
  • Epidermoid cyst
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (key differential based on location and tongue-protrusion mobility)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision.

5. Congenital muscular torticollis, ultrasound

Benign

Fibrosis and shortening of the sternocleidomastoid muscle in infants.

Imaging Characteristics

Fusiform enlargement of the sternocleidomastoid muscle with heterogeneous, often hyperechoic internal texture reflecting fibrous replacement.

Differential
  • Congenital muscular torticollis (benign, self-limited in most cases)
Next Step

Pediatric physical therapy referral; most cases resolve with conservative stretching therapy.

6. Carotid body tumor, ultrasound

Variable

Vascular tumor at the carotid bifurcation.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, hypervascular mass at the carotid bifurcation, characteristically splaying the internal and external carotid arteries apart.

Differential
  • Carotid body tumor (paraganglioma)
Next Step

ENT/vascular surgery referral; CTA or MRA for further characterization and surgical planning.

Deep Vein Thrombosis - Lower Extremity

8 findings

1. Acute DVT, femoral/popliteal vein

Concerning

Clot within the femoral or popliteal vein, the primary target of standard lower extremity DVT ultrasound protocols.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible vein with echogenic material within the lumen (though acute thrombus can be nearly anechoic and easily missed without compression testing), lack of normal flow augmentation with distal compression.

Differential
  • Acute DVT (femoral or popliteal vein)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; the primary diagnostic criterion is lack of full compressibility, not echogenicity, given that acute thrombus can be anechoic.

2. Acute DVT, calf vein (distal)

Variable

Clot confined to the calf veins (posterior tibial, peroneal, or muscular veins), with somewhat different management considerations than proximal DVT.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible calf vein with intraluminal material, assessed with a more technically demanding protocol than proximal veins given smaller vessel caliber.

Differential
  • Isolated distal (calf) DVT (lower embolization risk than proximal DVT, though management approaches vary)
Next Step

Anticoagulation or serial surveillance ultrasound per institutional protocol and risk factors.

3. Chronic DVT / post-thrombotic changes

Variable

Old, organized thrombus with venous wall changes, distinct from acute DVT in both appearance and management implications.

Imaging Characteristics

Echogenic, often smaller, non-compressible or partially compressible vein with wall thickening, sometimes with recanalization and collateral vein formation.

Differential
  • Chronic DVT / post-thrombotic syndrome changes
  • Acute-on-chronic DVT (diagnostically challenging)
Next Step

Correlate with prior imaging if available to assess for interval change; anticoagulation duration/management depends on acuity.

4. Superficial venous thrombosis, lower extremity

Variable

Thrombosis of a superficial vein (great or small saphenous vein, or a tributary), from varicose veins or other risk factors.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible, thickened superficial vein with internal echogenic material, assessed for proximity to the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction.

Differential
  • Superficial thrombophlebitis (lower risk if distant from the deep system junction)
  • Extension toward the deep system (higher risk, may warrant anticoagulation)
Next Step

Conservative management for isolated distal superficial disease; anticoagulation consideration if close to the deep venous junction.

5. May-Thurner syndrome, associated left iliofemoral DVT

Variable

Left iliofemoral DVT associated with compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive left lower extremity DVT extending into the iliac system, with ultrasound limited in directly visualizing the iliac compression itself.

Differential
  • May-Thurner-associated DVT (consider in extensive left-sided DVT, particularly in young women without other clear risk factors)
Next Step

CT or MR venography for iliac vein assessment; vascular surgery/IR referral for possible stenting.

6. Baker cyst with rupture, mimicking DVT

Variable

Ruptured popliteal (Baker) cyst causing calf pain and swelling that can clinically mimic DVT.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, ill-defined fluid collection tracking from the popliteal fossa into the calf, distinct from the tubular, compressible vein appearance of DVT.

Differential
  • Ruptured Baker cyst ('pseudothrombophlebitis')
  • Concurrent DVT (can coexist)
Next Step

Conservative management for isolated ruptured cyst; ensure a thorough deep venous assessment is still performed.

7. Muscle hematoma, mimicking DVT

Variable

Intramuscular hematoma causing calf pain and swelling that can clinically mimic DVT.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, often complex fluid collection within the muscle belly, distinct from the vascular structures.

Differential
  • Muscle hematoma (traumatic or anticoagulation-related)
  • Concurrent DVT (can coexist)
Next Step

Correlate with anticoagulation status and trauma history; ensure a thorough deep venous assessment is still performed.

8. Phlegmasia cerulea dolens

Concerning

Severe, extensive iliofemoral DVT causing venous outflow obstruction severe enough to compromise arterial inflow, a limb-threatening emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive thrombosis throughout the deep venous system with markedly reduced or reversed arterial Doppler flow in advanced cases.

Differential
  • Phlegmasia cerulea dolens (limb-threatening emergency)
Next Step

Emergent vascular surgery consultation for consideration of thrombolysis/thrombectomy given limb viability concerns.

Deep Vein Thrombosis - Upper Extremity

4 findings

1. Acute DVT, upper extremity (subclavian/axillary/brachial)

Concerning

Clot within an upper extremity deep vein, increasingly common given central line and PICC use.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible vein with intraluminal material, lack of normal respiratory phasicity on spectral Doppler.

Differential
  • Catheter/PICC-related DVT (common cause)
  • Paget-Schroetter syndrome
  • Malignancy-related DVT
Next Step

Anticoagulation; correlate with any indwelling catheter and consider its removal versus retention based on clinical context.

2. Catheter-related thrombosis, upper extremity

Concerning

Thrombus associated with an indwelling central venous catheter or PICC line.

Imaging Characteristics

Thrombus surrounding or adjacent to the catheter within the vein lumen, sometimes with a fibrin sheath.

Differential
  • Catheter-associated thrombosis
Next Step

Anticoagulation; catheter removal is not always mandatory and depends on ongoing need, function, and clinical severity.

3. Paget-Schroetter syndrome (effort thrombosis)

Concerning

Subclavian/axillary vein thrombosis from repetitive compression at the thoracic outlet, classically in young, athletic patients after vigorous arm activity.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible subclavian/axillary vein with intraluminal thrombus, in a young patient without other risk factors and a history of vigorous overhead arm activity.

Differential
  • Paget-Schroetter syndrome (venous thoracic outlet syndrome)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral; often requires thrombolysis followed by surgical decompression given the underlying anatomic compression.

4. Internal jugular vein thrombosis, ultrasound

Concerning

Clot within the internal jugular vein, from catheter placement, malignant compression, or septic thrombophlebitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Non-compressible internal jugular vein with intraluminal material.

Differential
  • Catheter-related thrombosis
  • Malignant compression/invasion
  • Septic thrombophlebitis (Lemierre syndrome)
Next Step

Anticoagulation; identify and treat the underlying cause.

Peripheral Arterial Doppler

6 findings

1. Peripheral arterial disease, ankle-brachial index and Doppler

Concerning

Atherosclerotic narrowing of the lower extremity arteries, screened with the ankle-brachial index and characterized with segmental Doppler waveforms.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced ankle-brachial index (normal 1.0-1.4; borderline 0.91-0.99; abnormal ≤0.90), with segmental pressure and waveform analysis localizing the level of disease.

Differential
  • Mild-moderate PAD (ABI 0.7-0.9)
  • Severe PAD (ABI <0.5, or non-compressible vessels with ABI >1.4 in medial calcification)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for revascularization planning; toe-brachial index if vessels are non-compressible.

2. Arterial stenosis, extremity Doppler grading

Concerning

Focal narrowing of a peripheral artery, graded by Doppler velocity criteria.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal velocity increase at the stenosis with post-stenotic turbulence, and a velocity ratio compared to the adjacent normal segment used for percent stenosis grading.

Differential
  • Mild, moderate, or severe stenosis (grading determines management)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for revascularization planning based on stenosis severity and symptoms.

3. Arterial occlusion, extremity Doppler

Concerning

Complete occlusion of a peripheral artery.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent flow signal within the artery on color and spectral Doppler, with collateral vessel flow often visible around the occluded segment.

Differential
  • Chronic occlusion (often with collateral development)
  • Acute occlusion (embolic or thrombotic, higher urgency)
Next Step

Correlate with acuity and limb viability; emergent vascular surgery consultation for acute limb ischemia.

4. Pseudoaneurysm, post-catheterization ultrasound

Concerning

Contained vascular injury at a prior arterial access site, most often femoral.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal outpouching from the artery with a characteristic 'yin-yang' swirling flow pattern on color Doppler and a 'to-and-fro' waveform in the connecting neck.

Differential
  • Post-catheterization pseudoaneurysm
Next Step

Ultrasound-guided thrombin injection or compression for smaller pseudoaneurysms; vascular surgery referral for larger lesions.

5. Arteriovenous fistula, acquired ultrasound

Variable

Abnormal direct connection between an artery and vein, from trauma, prior catheterization, or surgical creation.

Imaging Characteristics

High-velocity, turbulent flow with arterialization of the venous waveform at the fistula site, and a characteristic 'color bruit' artifact on surrounding tissue.

Differential
  • Traumatic/iatrogenic AV fistula
  • Surgically created dialysis fistula (expected finding)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for symptomatic acquired fistulas; dialysis fistulas assessed per clinical context.

6. Popliteal artery aneurysm, ultrasound

Concerning

Aneurysmal dilation of the popliteal artery.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal dilation of the popliteal artery ≥1.5-2 cm, often with mural thrombus reducing the functional lumen.

Differential
  • Popliteal artery aneurysm (screen the contralateral side and for AAA given associated risk)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral for surveillance or repair based on size.

Vascular Access & Miscellaneous

6 findings

1. Dialysis fistula/graft assessment, ultrasound

Variable

Ultrasound evaluation of a dialysis arteriovenous fistula or graft for maturation or dysfunction.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of fistula diameter, flow volume, and depth for maturation adequacy, or identification of a stenosis in a malfunctioning access.

Differential
  • Maturing/functional fistula or graft
  • Stenosis (inflow, outflow, or graft-related)
  • Thrombosed fistula/graft
Next Step

Vascular surgery/IR referral for intervention if stenosis or thrombosis affects dialysis adequacy.

2. Central line/PICC placement, ultrasound guidance

Variable

Ultrasound guidance for central venous access placement, with tip position confirmed by subsequent chest X-ray.

Imaging Characteristics

Real-time visualization of vein cannulation and guidewire placement during ultrasound-guided central line insertion.

Differential
  • Procedural guidance rather than a diagnostic finding
Next Step

Confirm final catheter tip position with chest X-ray or intracavitary ECG technique per institutional protocol.

3. Vascular access complication, ultrasound

Concerning

Complication of a vascular access procedure (central line, arterial line, catheterization).

Imaging Characteristics

Hematoma: heterogeneous fluid collection adjacent to the access site. Pseudoaneurysm: focal outpouching with swirling flow. Arteriovenous fistula: turbulent, arterialized flow.

Differential
  • Access site hematoma
  • Pseudoaneurysm
  • Arteriovenous fistula
  • Vessel thrombosis/occlusion
Next Step

Correlate with the specific complication identified; vascular surgery/IR referral as indicated.

4. Temporal artery ultrasound (giant cell arteritis)

Concerning

Ultrasound assessment of the temporal artery for the 'halo sign,' supporting a diagnosis of giant cell arteritis.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic, concentric wall thickening ('halo sign') surrounding the temporal artery lumen, which may partially resolve with treatment.

Differential
  • Giant cell arteritis (halo sign is supportive; temporal artery biopsy remains complementary)
Next Step

Rheumatology/ophthalmology urgent referral; do not delay corticosteroid treatment for diagnostic testing given vision loss risk.

5. Abdominal aortic aneurysm, screening ultrasound (cross-reference)

Concerning

Focal aortic dilation; see the abdomen/pelvis atlas for detailed characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Aortic diameter ≥3 cm measured outer-wall to outer-wall.

Differential
  • Ectasia (mild diffuse dilation <3 cm)
  • AAA
Next Step

See the abdomen/pelvis atlas; vascular surgery referral for surveillance or repair per size-based guidelines.

6. Splenic/hepatic artery aneurysm, ultrasound (cross-reference)

Concerning

Visceral artery aneurysm; see the abdomen/pelvis atlas for detailed characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, rounded outpouching from a visceral artery.

Differential
  • Splenic artery aneurysm (most common visceral artery aneurysm)
  • Hepatic artery aneurysm
Next Step

See the abdomen/pelvis atlas for full workup and IR referral criteria.

Soft Tissue - General

6 findings

1. Soft tissue abscess, ultrasound general

Concerning

Localized collection of pus, with ultrasound distinguishing a drainable abscess from simple cellulitis, applicable at any body location.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete, complex, hypoechoic-to-anechoic fluid collection, often with internal debris and increased through-transmission, sometimes with surrounding cellulitis 'cobblestoning.'

Differential
  • Drainable abscess
  • Simple cellulitis (no discrete collection)
Next Step

Incision and drainage for a discrete abscess; antibiotics alone for cellulitis without a drainable collection.

2. Cellulitis, ultrasound cobblestoning

Variable

Soft-tissue infection without a drainable collection.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened, hyperechoic subcutaneous tissue with interspersed anechoic fluid tracking between fat lobules, creating a 'cobblestone' appearance, without a discrete fluid collection.

Differential
  • Cellulitis (no drainable collection)
Next Step

Antibiotics; ultrasound helps distinguish from an abscess that would benefit from drainage.

3. Necrotizing soft tissue infection, ultrasound signs

Concerning

Rapidly progressive, life-threatening soft-tissue infection, with ultrasound showing supportive but non-definitive findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened fascia (>2-4 mm) with surrounding fluid, and subcutaneous air causing dirty shadowing artifact if present, though absence does not exclude the diagnosis.

Differential
  • Necrotizing fasciitis (surgical emergency, clinical diagnosis should not be delayed for imaging)
Next Step

Emergent surgical consultation; do not delay surgical exploration for imaging confirmation if clinically suspected.

4. Lipoma, ultrasound characterization general

Benign

Common benign fat-containing soft-tissue tumor, at any body location.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, homogeneous or mildly heterogeneous, typically isoechoic to slightly hyperechoic mass relative to surrounding fat, with a thin capsule and minimal vascularity.

Differential
  • Simple lipoma (benign)
  • Atypical lipomatous tumor/liposarcoma (thicker septa, more heterogeneous - key differential)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a classic, homogeneous, superficial lipoma; further imaging for atypical or deep lesions.

5. Epidermal inclusion cyst, ultrasound

Benign

Common benign cyst arising from the epidermis, typically in the dermis/subcutaneous tissue.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often heterogeneous, hypoechoic to mixed echogenicity lesion within the dermis/subcutaneous tissue, sometimes with a visible central punctum connecting to the skin surface.

Differential
  • Epidermal inclusion cyst (benign, common)
Next Step

No treatment needed if asymptomatic; surgical excision for symptomatic or recurrently infected cysts.

6. Foreign body, soft tissue ultrasound general

Variable

Retained foreign material within the soft tissues at any body location.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperechoic focus with posterior acoustic shadowing or reverberation/comet-tail artifact, often with a surrounding hypoechoic inflammatory halo if retained for some time.

Differential
  • Retained foreign body (wood, glass, metal, plastic)
Next Step

Surgical/wound care referral for removal if symptomatic; ultrasound can guide localization for removal.

Pediatric Neck

5 findings

1. Thyroglossal duct cyst, pediatric ultrasound

Benign

Congenital cystic remnant, the most common pediatric congenital neck mass.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, midline cystic lesion at or near the hyoid bone.

Differential
  • Thyroglossal duct cyst (benign)
Next Step

Surgical referral (Sistrunk procedure); confirm a normally located thyroid gland is present before excision.

2. Branchial cleft cyst, pediatric ultrasound

Benign

Congenital lateral neck mass, common in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined cystic lesion along the anterior sternocleidomastoid border.

Differential
  • Second branchial cleft cyst (most common in children)
Next Step

Surgical referral for excision, particularly given recurrent infection risk.

3. Cervical lymphadenitis, pediatric ultrasound

Variable

Enlarged, often infected cervical lymph node in a child, extremely common.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged node(s), reactive (preserved hilum) or suppurative (central necrosis), assessed for abscess formation warranting drainage.

Differential
  • Reactive lymphadenitis (most common, viral or bacterial upper respiratory source)
  • Suppurative lymphadenitis with abscess
Next Step

Antibiotics for suppurative disease; drainage for a discrete abscess.

4. Nontuberculous mycobacterial lymphadenitis, pediatric ultrasound

Variable

Atypical mycobacterial cervical lymph node infection, a classic cause of chronic, indolent cervical adenitis in young children in non-endemic (for TB) areas.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged node(s) with a characteristic thin, non-inflamed overlying skin despite significant internal necrosis, sometimes progressing to a violaceous skin discoloration.

Differential
  • Nontuberculous mycobacterial lymphadenitis (classic in young children, indolent course)
Next Step

ENT/infectious disease referral; surgical excision is often preferred given relative resistance to standard antibiotics.

5. Pediatric neck mass, general approach

Variable

Systematic ultrasound approach to a pediatric neck mass, given the broad congenital, infectious, and (rarely) neoplastic differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Location (midline vs. lateral), cystic vs. solid composition, and vascularity help narrow the differential and guide further workup.

Differential
  • Congenital (thyroglossal duct cyst, branchial cleft cyst, lymphatic/vascular malformation)
  • Infectious/reactive lymphadenopathy (most common overall)
  • Neoplastic (rare in children, but important not to miss)
Next Step

Correlate with age, location, and clinical trajectory; further imaging (MRI) or biopsy for atypical, persistent, or rapidly growing masses.

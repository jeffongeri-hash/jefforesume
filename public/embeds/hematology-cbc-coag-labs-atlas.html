CBC with Differential — Click a Lab to Expand

Every value in a standard CBC-with-differential: normal range, causes of high/low, critical-value thresholds (flagged in red), and clinical pearls.

High — causes

Polycythemia vera, secondary polycythemia (COPD, OSA, high altitude, EPO doping, renal cell carcinoma/other EPO-secreting tumors), dehydration (hemoconcentration, not a true increase in mass).

Low — causes

Anemia of any cause — iron deficiency, chronic disease, hemolysis, marrow failure, acute blood loss, nutritional (B12/folate).

Critical value / action

Symptomatic anemia or Hgb < 7 g/dL generally triggers transfusion consideration (restrictive threshold per most current RCTs, e.g., TRICC/TRISS); Hgb < 5 g/dL or hemodynamic instability is a critical value requiring urgent transfusion regardless of symptoms in most protocols.

Pearl

Transfusion thresholds are contextual, not a single hard number — active ACS/cardiac ischemia typically uses a higher threshold (~8 g/dL) than a stable asymptomatic patient (~7 g/dL) per current restrictive-strategy trials.

High — causes

Same causes as elevated Hgb; also a marker of hemoconcentration in acute dehydration/burns.

Low — causes

Parallels Hgb in most anemias; disproportionately low relative to Hgb is not typical (they usually move together, roughly Hct ≈ 3 × Hgb).

Critical value / action

Hct < 21% (roughly parallels Hgb < 7) generally prompts the same transfusion consideration as low Hgb.

Pearl

Hct is calculated/estimated on modern analyzers (not directly measured) — can be falsely elevated in severe hyperglycemia/hypernatremia-driven cell swelling artifacts on some instruments.

High — causes

Macrocytic anemia: B12/folate deficiency, alcohol use, liver disease, hypothyroidism, myelodysplastic syndrome, reticulocytosis (retics are larger), certain medications (hydroxyurea, zidovudine, methotrexate).

Low — causes

Microcytic anemia: iron deficiency, thalassemia trait/disease, anemia of chronic disease (can also be normocytic), sideroblastic anemia, lead poisoning.

Critical value / action

Not itself a panic value, but extreme MCV (very high with megaloblastic anemia, e.g. >120) should prompt urgent B12/folate workup given risk of concurrent neurologic injury from B12 deficiency.

Pearl

Mentzer index (MCV ÷ RBC count) <13 favors thalassemia over iron deficiency; >13 favors iron deficiency — quick bedside differentiator before confirmatory testing (ferritin, Hb electrophoresis).

High — causes

Reflects anisocytosis (size variation) — classically elevated in iron deficiency (unlike thalassemia trait, where RDW is typically normal despite similarly low MCV), mixed nutritional deficiencies, recent transfusion or hemolysis (new large reticulocytes mixed with old small cells), myelodysplastic syndrome.

Low — causes

Not clinically significant when low/normal — RDW has no meaningful “low” pathology.

Critical value / action

No panic threshold.

Pearl

Elevated RDW in an otherwise normal CBC (before anemia develops) has been associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in population studies — an underused general prognostic marker.

High — causes

Appropriate marrow response to hemolysis or acute blood loss; also seen with EPO/ESA therapy, recovery from marrow suppression (chemo, B12/iron repletion response).

Low — causes

Inappropriately low for the degree of anemia signals a marrow production problem (aplastic anemia, marrow infiltration, pure red cell aplasia, severe nutritional deficiency, renal failure with EPO deficiency) rather than peripheral destruction.

Critical value / action

Not a panic value, but a low retic count in the setting of severe unexplained anemia should prompt urgent marrow evaluation.

Pearl

This is the single most useful test for classifying an anemia as production-problem vs. destruction/loss-problem — always corrected for the hematocrit (RPI) since a raw % retic count is misleadingly elevated when the total RBC pool is already low.

High — causes

Reactive/secondary thrombocytosis (infection, inflammation, iron deficiency, post-splenectomy, malignancy) is far more common than a primary myeloproliferative process (essential thrombocythemia).

Low — causes

ITP, DIC, TTP/HUS, HIT, hypersplenism, marrow infiltration/failure, medication effect, pseudothrombocytopenia (EDTA-induced clumping artifact — always confirm with a smear or citrate tube before acting).

Critical value / action

<10,000–20,000/µL: spontaneous bleeding risk, prophylactic platelet transfusion often considered even without bleeding. <50,000/µL: holds for many procedures/surgery. >1,000,000/µL: paradoxical bleeding risk from acquired von Willebrand syndrome (large vWF multimers consumed) in addition to thrombosis risk — counterintuitive but important.

Pearl

Always check a peripheral smear before treating an unexpected very low platelet count — EDTA-induced pseudothrombocytopenia (in vitro platelet clumping) is a common, harmless cause of a falsely low automated count.

High — causes

Suggests increased platelet turnover/younger platelet population — ITP (marrow compensating), some myeloproliferative disorders.

Low — causes

Suggests decreased production — aplastic anemia, chemotherapy effect, some inherited thrombocytopenias (e.g., Wiskott-Aldrich has small platelets, unlike Bernard-Soulier which has large platelets).

Critical value / action

No panic threshold.

Pearl

MPV can help distinguish a peripheral destructive thrombocytopenia (high MPV, young large platelets being released) from a central production problem (low/normal MPV) — analogous to how retic count works for anemia.

High — causes

Infection (bacterial > viral typically), inflammation, stress/steroid response (demargination), leukemia (especially if markedly elevated with blasts), smoking (chronic mild elevation), post-splenectomy.

Low — causes

Viral infection, marrow suppression (chemo, radiation, marrow failure/infiltration), sepsis (can consume/sequester neutrophils faster than marrow releases them), medication effect, autoimmune (Felty syndrome, SLE), B12/folate deficiency (also affects WBC production).

Critical value / action

<1,000/µL or ANC <500/µL (severe neutropenia) — neutropenic fever is a medical emergency requiring immediate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics, often within 1 hour. >50,000/µL (leukemoid reaction or leukemia) warrants urgent smear review — hyperviscosity/leukostasis risk climbs sharply above ~100,000/µL, especially with blasts (AML) more than mature cells (CLL).

Pearl

The absolute count (not the percentage) is what drives clinical decisions — always calculate ANC (WBC × [%neutrophils + %bands] ÷ 100) rather than reacting to the neutrophil percentage alone.

High — causes

Bacterial infection, tissue necrosis/inflammation, corticosteroids (demargination, not true production increase), stress response, CML (with basophilia and left shift), smoking.

Low — causes

Viral infection, chemotherapy/radiation nadir, clozapine/other drug-induced agranulocytosis, autoimmune neutropenia, B12/folate deficiency, congenital neutropenia syndromes, benign ethnic neutropenia (common in people of African descent, no increased infection risk).

Critical value / action

ANC <500/µL = severe neutropenia — neutropenic precautions and, if febrile, emergent empiric antibiotics without waiting for cultures. ANC <100/µL = profound neutropenia, highest infection risk.

Pearl

Always distinguish benign ethnic neutropenia (chronically mild-low ANC, no infection risk, common and often unrecognized) from a true pathologic neutropenia — avoid unnecessary workup/anxiety when it's a patient's normal baseline.

High — causes

Viral infections (EBV, CMV, pertussis in children can cause striking counts), CLL (in older adults, look for smudge cells on smear), acute stress response (less pronounced than neutrophilia).

Low — causes

HIV/AIDS (CD4 count is the clinically tracked subset), corticosteroid use, chemotherapy/radiation, autoimmune disease, acute severe illness/sepsis (lymphopenia is a marker of illness severity), congenital immunodeficiency.

Critical value / action

No standard panic threshold, but severe lymphopenia in the context of possible HIV should prompt CD4 count and opportunistic infection risk assessment.

Pearl

New unexplained lymphocytosis with smudge cells in a patient >60 should prompt flow cytometry to evaluate for CLL — often an incidental asymptomatic finding.

High — causes

Chronic infections (TB, endocarditis), autoimmune/granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis), recovery phase post-neutropenia, chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) if persistent in an older adult.

Low — causes

Marrow suppression, hairy cell leukemia (classically causes monocytopenia, a distinguishing feature from other leukemias).

Critical value / action

No panic threshold.

Pearl

Persistent absolute monocytosis (>1,000/µL for ≥3 months) without another explanation in an older adult warrants CMML workup — easy to overlook since monocytes are rarely the focus of CBC review.

High — causes

NAACP mnemonic — Neoplasm, Allergy/Asthma, Addison's disease, Collagen-vascular disease, Parasites. Also drug reactions (DRESS syndrome), hypereosinophilic syndrome.

Low — causes

Acute stress response/Cushing syndrome (cortisol suppresses eosinophils acutely) — rarely clinically significant on its own.

Critical value / action

>1,500/µL (marked eosinophilia) warrants urgent workup, especially with cardiac/pulmonary symptoms — hypereosinophilic syndrome can cause eosinophilic myocarditis/endomyocardial fibrosis (Loeffler endocarditis).

Pearl

DRESS syndrome (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms) can be life-threatening — marked eosinophilia plus rash/fever/organ involvement 2–8 weeks after starting a new drug (classically anticonvulsants, allopurinol, sulfonamides) should prompt immediate drug discontinuation.

High — causes

CML (classic finding, especially with left-shifted myeloid series), myeloproliferative neoplasms, rarely IgE-mediated hypersensitivity, hypothyroidism.

Low — causes

Acute infection/stress, hyperthyroidism — rarely clinically significant alone.

Critical value / action

No panic threshold, but unexplained basophilia should prompt BCR-ABL testing for CML.

Pearl

Basophilia unexplained by allergy is one of the more specific tip-offs to CML among the differential counts.

Coagulation Studies — Click a Test to Expand

PT/INR, PTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer, thrombin time, and anti-Xa — what each test actually measures and when it drives a clinical decision.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

Warfarin therapy, vitamin K deficiency, liver disease/synthetic dysfunction, factor VII deficiency (isolated), DIC, supratherapeutic DOAC levels (variable sensitivity by reagent/drug), massive transfusion (dilutional).

Clinical use

Monitors warfarin therapy; component of MELD score in liver disease (not a bleeding-risk marker in that context); part of DIC scoring; part of the alteplase/tPA eligibility screen in acute stroke.

Critical value / action

INR >1.7 is an FDA-label contraindication to IV alteplase in acute ischemic stroke. Markedly elevated INR (>5–10) without bleeding still warrants urgent vitamin K ± holding warfarin per bleeding-risk protocols; INR with active major bleeding at any level is a critical value requiring immediate reversal.

Pearl

Factor VII has the shortest half-life (~6h) of the vitamin-K-dependent factors — PT/INR rises first with both warfarin initiation and vitamin K deficiency, before PTT changes.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

Unfractionated heparin therapy, hemophilia A/B/C, von Willebrand disease (via secondary factor VIII deficiency), lupus anticoagulant (prolongs in vitro, paradoxically prothrombotic in vivo), factor XII deficiency (prolongs PTT but is NOT associated with bleeding — classic teaching point), DIC, liver disease, massive transfusion.

Clinical use

Monitors UFH infusion; screens the intrinsic pathway; part of the mixing study workup for an unexplained prolongation.

Critical value / action

PTT >2–3x the therapeutic range (supratherapeutic heparin) raises bleeding risk and should prompt infusion hold/rate reduction per nomogram; markedly prolonged PTT with active bleeding is a critical value.

Pearl

A prolonged PTT that does NOT correct on a 1:1 mixing study indicates an inhibitor (factor-specific antibody, or lupus anticoagulant) rather than a simple factor deficiency — changes the entire differential and management pathway.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

Acute phase reactant — elevated in inflammation, infection, pregnancy, malignancy; usually not independently actionable when isolated.

Clinical use

Low fibrinogen is a core component of the ISTH DIC score; cryoprecipitate is dosed specifically to correct fibrinogen; monitored during thrombolytic therapy (fibrinogen depletion is expected and tracked to gauge systemic lytic effect and bleeding risk).

Critical value / action

<100 mg/dL with active bleeding (or before an invasive procedure) generally triggers cryoprecipitate transfusion; <50 mg/dL is a critical value with substantial hemorrhage risk.

Pearl

Fibrinogen is often the last factor to normalize after DIC resolves and among the first to drop during ongoing consumption — trending it serially is more informative than a single value.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

VTE (DVT/PE), DIC, recent surgery/trauma, pregnancy, malignancy, advanced age, infection/inflammation — very sensitive but non-specific.

Clinical use

High negative predictive value to rule OUT VTE in low-to-moderate pretest probability patients (Wells score); not useful to rule in VTE given poor specificity; component of ISTH DIC score.

Critical value / action

No panic threshold — an elevated D-dimer alone is never a critical value; it is a screening tool that requires clinical correlation and confirmatory imaging when VTE is suspected.

Pearl

A D-dimer should generally not be ordered in a patient with high pretest probability for VTE — proceed directly to imaging, since a negative D-dimer doesn't reliably rule out VTE in that population and a positive one adds nothing actionable.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

Heparin contamination/effect (most common cause of an otherwise-unexplained prolonged TT), hypofibrinogenemia/dysfibrinogenemia, direct thrombin inhibitors (dabigatran, argatroban, bivalirudin), FDP/fibrin degradation products (as in DIC).

Clinical use

Useful to detect heparin contamination of a sample (e.g., drawn from a heparinized line) and to screen for direct thrombin inhibitor effect when PT/PTT are ambiguous.

Critical value / action

No standard panic threshold; interpreted alongside the clinical bleeding picture.

Pearl

A prolonged TT that normalizes with a reptilase time (reptilase is not inhibited by heparin) confirms heparin contamination as the cause rather than a true fibrinogen/dysfibrinogenemia problem.

Elevated / abnormal — causes

Overdosing, renal impairment (LMWH accumulation), obesity/dosing weight miscalculation.

Clinical use

Preferred monitoring method for UFH in patients with baseline PTT prolongation (e.g., lupus anticoagulant) where PTT is unreliable; used for LMWH monitoring in renal impairment, pregnancy, and extremes of body weight; can also be calibrated to estimate DOAC drug levels in specific labs (specialized assay, not routinely available everywhere).

Critical value / action

Markedly supratherapeutic levels with active bleeding warrant reversal per agent (protamine partially for LMWH, fully for UFH).

Pearl

In patients with a baseline-prolonged PTT from lupus anticoagulant, PTT cannot be trusted to monitor heparin — anti-Xa is the necessary alternative monitoring strategy.

Clinical Correlation: Stroke, ACS & DIC

How CBC and coagulation studies plug directly into time-critical clinical decisions.

Acute Ischemic Stroke Workup

Platelet count

Count <100,000/µL is an FDA-label contraindication to IV alteplase — must result before treatment decision in most protocols (though treatment should not be delayed awaiting labs if no reason to suspect thrombocytopenia).

INR

INR >1.7 (in a patient on warfarin) is an FDA-label contraindication to IV alteplase. Any use of a DOAC (dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) within the prior 48h (or longer with renal impairment) generally excludes alteplase regardless of standard coag studies, since routine PT/PTT don't reliably reflect DOAC levels.

Glucose

Checked immediately — severe hypoglycemia is a stroke mimic and must be corrected/excluded before attributing a focal deficit to ischemia.

CBC

Severe anemia can exacerbate ischemic injury via reduced oxygen delivery to penumbra tissue; marked thrombocytosis or polycythemia can itself be a stroke risk factor/etiology to investigate after the acute event.

Acute Coronary Syndrome & Hematology-Relevant Labs

CBC/Hgb

Significant anemia can precipitate demand (type 2) ischemia even without plaque rupture — always check Hgb in ACS presentations, especially in older adults or those with GI symptoms.

Platelet count

Baseline count needed before initiating dual antiplatelet therapy/GPIIb-IIIa inhibitors; a falling count after starting heparin should raise concern for HIT (heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) — typically a >50% drop from baseline 5–10 days after exposure.

PTT / anti-Xa

Used to titrate UFH infusion in ACS management per institutional nomogram; anti-Xa preferred if baseline PTT is unreliable (e.g., lupus anticoagulant).

Case: Elevated INR in a Patient on No Medications

A 45-year-old with no medical history, on no medications, presents for pre-op labs before an elective procedure. INR returns at 1.6 (baseline PT/INR never previously checked). No bleeding, no bruising, no family history of bleeding disorders.

In someone NOT on warfarin or a DOAC, an elevated INR always means something real is disrupting extrinsic/common pathway factor synthesis or activity — it is never an incidental finding to ignore, because there's no medication to blame it on.

Differential

Vitamin K deficiency

Poor oral intake, malabsorption (celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, biliary obstruction/cholestasis impairing fat-soluble vitamin absorption), recent broad-spectrum antibiotic use disrupting gut-flora vitamin K synthesis.

Liver synthetic dysfunction

Even in an asymptomatic patient — early cirrhosis, undiagnosed hepatitis, fatty liver disease can present with an isolated INR elevation before other liver enzyme abnormalities or symptoms appear.

Isolated Factor VII deficiency

Rare hereditary condition; would show isolated PT/INR prolongation with normal PTT since factor VII is unique to the extrinsic pathway.

Lupus anticoagulant / antiphospholipid antibodies

More classically prolongs PTT, but some reagent systems and some lupus anticoagulants can affect PT-based assays too — worth considering especially if there's any history of prior clots or pregnancy loss.

Pre-analytical error

Under-filled citrate tube (wrong blood-to-anticoagulant ratio artificially prolongs both PT and PTT), sample drawn above an IV line/heparin flush, or a very high hematocrit reducing plasma volume relative to citrate — always consider a repeat draw before an extensive workup if the finding is unexpected and isolated.

Workup approach

Repeat INR on a fresh, properly-drawn sample first (rule out pre-analytical error — cheapest and fastest step). If confirmed: obtain PTT (isolated PT prolongation narrows the differential toward factor VII deficiency or early vitamin K deficiency; combined PT+PTT prolongation broadens it to liver disease, more advanced vitamin K deficiency, or DIC), liver function panel + synthetic function (albumin), nutritional/malabsorption history, and consider empiric vitamin K trial with recheck in 24–48h (rapid correction supports vitamin K deficiency over liver disease, where correction is slower/incomplete).

This scenario matters clinically because an unexplained elevated INR before a procedure changes bleeding risk and may be the first sign of undiagnosed liver disease or a malabsorptive condition — it should delay elective surgery until characterized, not be dismissed as a lab quirk.

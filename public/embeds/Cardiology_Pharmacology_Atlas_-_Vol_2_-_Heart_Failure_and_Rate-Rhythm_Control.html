01 / Heart Failure & Rate/Rhythm Control

Heart Failure Pillars

Four drug classes, each interrupting a different arm of the neurohormonal vicious cycle from Vol. 1b — modern HFrEF management means initiating all four as early as tolerated rather than sequential trial-and-failure.

Contemporary practice favors rapid sequential initiation of all four pillars (often within days to weeks) at low doses, then uptitrating each in parallel — rather than the older model of fully optimizing one drug before starting the next.
Full RAAS blockade and beta-blocker dosing tables live in Vol. 1

The hyperkalemia stack

A typical optimized HFrEF regimen combines an ARNI/ACEi/ARB (Vol. 1) with an MRA — two potassium-raising mechanisms stacked by design. Check basic metabolic panel within 1 week of MRA initiation or any RAAS-blocker dose increase, then periodically (many programs recheck at 1 week, 1 month, then every 3-6 months). Avoid initiating an MRA if baseline potassium is already elevated or eGFR is severely reduced (thresholds vary by institution, generally avoid if eGFR <30 or K+ >5.0 at baseline).

Pillar 4 — SGLT2 Inhibitors

Originally developed as diabetes medications, SGLT2 inhibitors block glucose (and sodium) reabsorption in the proximal tubule — the cardiovascular and renal benefits turned out to be independent of glucose-lowering, and dapagliflozin/empagliflozin are now guideline-recommended for HFrEF and HFpEF regardless of diabetes status.

Euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis — the class-specific trap

SGLT2 inhibitors can precipitate ketoacidosis with a NORMAL or only mildly elevated glucose — because the mechanism is insulinopenia-driven ketogenesis, not hyperglycemia per se, a normal glucose does not rule out DKA in a patient on this drug class who presents with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or malaise. Check a blood or urine ketone level directly rather than anchoring on the glucose value. Hold the drug for any significant illness, planned surgery, or prolonged fasting.

Genital mycotic infections — common, manageable, rarely a reason to stop

The glucosuria mechanism predisposes to genital yeast infections in both sexes — common enough to counsel proactively at initiation. Standard antifungal treatment usually resolves it without needing to discontinue the SGLT2 inhibitor, though recurrent cases warrant a case-by-case risk-benefit conversation.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/heart failure specialist for any patient not yet on all four pillars, or for guidance sequencing initiation in a patient with borderline hemodynamics or renal function.
Urgent evaluation
Suspected euglycemic DKA in any SGLT2i patient with unexplained nausea/vomiting/malaise, regardless of glucose value; significant hyperkalemia in a patient on combined RAAS blockade + MRA.
Who to call
Heart failure cardiology for GDMT optimization and titration sequencing; endocrinology/toxicology for suspected euglycemic DKA.

Additional HF Medications

Beyond the four pillars — agents for specific populations, refractory symptoms, or as an alternative when the standard pillars aren't tolerated.

Digoxin

MechanismInitial DoseMaintenance / IV LoadingSpecial Considerations
Inhibits Na-K-ATPase -> increased intracellular Na+ -> increased intracellular Ca2+ via Na-Ca exchanger -> positive inotropy. Also increases vagal tone -> AV nodal slowing (the basis for its rate-control use, see Rate Control section) 0.125-0.25mg PO daily (lower end in elderly/renal impairment) IV loading for acute AFib rate control: 0.25mg IV q6h x2-3 doses (max ~1.5mg/24h); maintenance same as oral Narrow therapeutic index — target trough level 0.5-0.9 ng/mL for HF (lower than historically taught; higher levels associated with increased mortality without added benefit, from the DIG trial reanalysis). Renally eliminated — dose-reduce significantly in renal impairment.
Digoxin toxicity — know the presentation

GI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, anorexia), visual disturbances (classically yellow-green halos/xanthopsia), and a wide range of arrhythmias (the classic teaching example is atrial tachycardia with block, but any new arrhythmia in a digoxin patient deserves toxicity consideration) — hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia both potentiate toxicity at a given digoxin level by increasing myocardial sensitivity. Digoxin immune Fab (DigiFab) is the specific antidote for severe toxicity with hemodynamically significant arrhythmia.

Ivabradine

MechanismInitial DoseTitrationSpecial Considerations
Selectively inhibits the If ("funny") current in the SA node — slows heart rate with NO effect on contractility, blood pressure, or AV conduction, unlike beta-blockers or non-DHP CCBs 5mg PO BID Titrate to 7.5mg BID based on resting HR (target 50-60 bpm); reduce to 2.5mg BID if HR too low SHIFT trial indication: HFrEF (EF≤35%) with sinus rhythm and resting HR ≥70 bpm despite maximally tolerated beta-blocker dose — this is an add-on for persistent tachycardia despite GDMT, not a beta-blocker substitute. Only works in sinus rhythm (no effect on AFib rate control). Visual phenomena (phosphenes — transient brightness in the visual field) are a recognized class-specific side effect.

Vericiguat

MechanismInitial DoseTitrationSpecial Considerations
Soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) stimulator — directly sensitizes sGC to nitric oxide and also stimulates it independent of NO, increasing cGMP and downstream vasodilation/anti-remodeling effects, addressing the impaired NO-sGC-cGMP signaling seen in HF 2.5mg PO daily (with food) Double the dose every 2 weeks as tolerated to target 10mg PO daily VICTORIA trial indication: recent HF decompensation (hospitalization or outpatient IV diuretic use within 6 months) with reduced EF, on top of standard GDMT — this is specifically for the high-risk, recently-decompensated HFrEF population, not first-line therapy. Symptomatic hypotension is the main initiation concern, hence the low starting dose and slow titration.

Hydralazine/Isosorbide Dinitrate

The combination (rather than either drug alone) is what carries the HFrEF outcomes evidence — hydralazine's arterial vasodilation and the nitrate's venodilation/preload reduction work complementarily, and the combination also mitigates nitrate tolerance to some degree.

RegimenInitial DoseTitration / MaxSpecial Considerations
Fixed-dose combination (BiDil) 1 tablet (37.5mg hydralazine/20mg isosorbide dinitrate) PO TID Max 2 tablets PO TID A-HeFT trial specifically enrolled self-identified Black patients with HFrEF on standard GDMT — significant mortality benefit in this population; also a reasonable option for any patient with a true contraindication or intolerance to ACEi/ARB/ARNI regardless of race
Individually titrated components Hydralazine 25-50mg PO TID-QID + isosorbide dinitrate 20-40mg PO TID Hydralazine max 300mg/day; ISDN max 120mg/day (with a nitrate-free interval to avoid tolerance) Individually titrating each component allows more flexibility than the fixed-dose combination, at the cost of more pills and more complex adherence
Nitrate tolerance — build in a nitrate-free interval

Continuous nitrate exposure without a drug-free interval leads to tachyphylaxis (loss of efficacy) within days — TID dosing regimens are typically structured to leave several nitrate-free hours overnight specifically to prevent this, and this scheduling detail matters clinically, not just for convenience.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/heart failure specialist for initiating vericiguat or ivabradine — both require specific patient selection criteria and careful titration best done with specialist input.
Urgent evaluation
Suspected digoxin toxicity, especially with any new or hemodynamically significant arrhythmia — check a level and consider DigiFab if severe.
Who to call
Heart failure cardiology for add-on therapy sequencing and selection; toxicology for significant digoxin toxicity requiring DigiFab.
03 / Heart Failure & Rate/Rhythm Control

Rate Control Medications

Three drug classes, all slowing AV nodal conduction — but choosing between them depends heavily on EF, comorbidities, and how quickly control is needed. Full individual dosing tables live in Vol. 1 (beta-blockers, CCBs) and the Additional HF Medications section (digoxin) — this section is the comparative decision framework.

ClassOnset (IV)Preferred WhenAvoid/Caution When
Beta-blockers
(metoprolol, esmolol — see Vol. 1)		 Minutes First-line in most guidelines given the largest evidence base; preferred in concurrent CAD/post-MI, HFrEF (once stable — see HF Pillars section) Caution in acute decompensated HF, reactive airway disease (use cardioselective agents — see Vol. 1's mnemonic), cocaine-associated presentations (see Vol. 4)
Non-DHP CCBs
(diltiazem, verapamil — see Vol. 1)		 Minutes Reactive airway disease where beta-blockade is a concern; often the default first IV agent in many EDs for acute AFib RVR AVOID in HFrEF (significant negative inotropy) — beta-blockers or digoxin are preferred in this population; avoid combining with beta-blockers (additive AV block risk)
Digoxin
(see Additional HF Medications)		 Hours (even with IV loading) HFrEF with hypotension where beta-blockers/CCBs aren't tolerated hemodynamically; add-on when a single agent doesn't achieve adequate control Ineffective for rate control during exertion/high sympathetic tone (vagally-mediated mechanism doesn't overcome catecholamine surge) — a poor monotherapy choice for an active patient; narrow therapeutic index
Avoid all three in WPW-associated AFib

Beta-blockers, non-DHP CCBs, and digoxin are all AV-nodal blocking agents — in pre-excited AFib with an accessory pathway (see Vol. 3), blocking the AV node removes its competition with the accessory pathway and can accelerate conduction toward VF. Procainamide or synchronized cardioversion are the correct choices instead — see Vol. 3's SVT section for the full explanation.

Lenient vs. Strict Rate Control

Current evidence (RACE II trial) supports a lenient rate control target — resting heart rate <110 bpm — as generally sufficient and non-inferior to strict control (<80 bpm) for most AFib patients, with the notable exception of patients who remain symptomatic despite lenient control or those with tachycardia-mediated cardiomyopathy concerns, where tighter control is reasonable.

Combination Rate Control

When a single agent doesn't achieve adequate control, combining a beta-blocker or non-DHP CCB with digoxin (rather than combining beta-blocker + CCB, which risks additive AV block/bradycardia) is a reasonable strategy — digoxin's distinct vagally-mediated mechanism complements rather than compounds the direct AV-nodal blocking effect of the other agent.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for rate control regimens that remain inadequate on combination therapy, or for any patient where the underlying rhythm diagnosis (see Vol. 3) affects agent selection.
Urgent evaluation
Any AFib/flutter with hemodynamic instability — this needs cardioversion consideration, not just rate control (see Vol. 3's Atrial Fibrillation section for the full decision framework).
Who to call
Cardiology/electrophysiology for refractory rate control or when rhythm-control strategy (ablation, antiarrhythmics) is being reconsidered.

Antiarrhythmics: Class I

The Vaughan Williams classification organizes every antiarrhythmic by which phase of the cardiac action potential it targets — this is the master diagram for the rest of this volume's antiarrhythmic sections.

Class I drugs all slow phase 0 (the fast sodium-driven upstroke) — the three subclasses (Ia/Ib/Ic) differ in how strongly they block sodium channels and whether they also affect repolarization.
Class MOA All Class I agents block fast voltage-gated Na+ channels, slowing phase 0 depolarization — this reduces conduction velocity through the myocardium, which is the shared mechanism underlying their antiarrhythmic (and proarrhythmic) effects.

Class Ia — Moderate Na+ Block + K+ Block

Ia agents block sodium channels moderately AND prolong repolarization (K+ channel effect) — this dual mechanism means QT prolongation is a class-defining concern alongside the sodium-blocking effects.

DrugIndicationsInitial DoseIV/Emergency DosingSpecial Considerations
Quinidine AFib/flutter rhythm control, Brugada syndrome (paradoxically suppresses the arrhythmic substrate), certain VT 200-300mg PO q6-8h (formulation-dependent) IV formulation rarely used in current US practice Significant drug interactions (potent CYP2D6 inhibitor); cinchonism (tinnitus, headache, visual changes) with toxicity; largely a niche/second-line agent today given the toxicity profile relative to newer options
Procainamide Stable wide-complex tachycardia (including pre-excited AFib — see Vol. 3), AFib/flutter Not typically initiated orally in current practice IV: load 20-50mg/min (max 17mg/kg total) until arrhythmia terminates, hypotension occurs, or QRS widens >50% -- then infusion 1-4mg/min Drug-induced lupus with long-term use (historically relevant for the oral formulation, less so now that IV-only use predominates); actively useful acutely for pre-excited AFib specifically because it slows the accessory pathway without the AV-nodal-blocking risk of other agents
Disopyramide Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with obstruction (negative inotropy reduces the outflow gradient), some AFib 100-150mg PO q6h No IV formulation in routine US use Significant anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth, urinary retention, constipation, blurred vision) limit tolerability; notable negative inotropy — this is actually therapeutic in obstructive HCM but a liability in reduced-EF patients

Class Ib — Weak Na+ Block, Shortens Repolarization

The mirror image of Class Ia in one respect: Ib agents shorten (rather than prolong) the action potential — this makes them the antiarrhythmics of choice when QT prolongation is already a concern.

DrugIndicationsIV/Emergency DosingSpecial Considerations
Lidocaine VT/VF (especially ischemia-related), an alternative to amiodarone in shockable cardiac arrest IV: 1-1.5mg/kg bolus, may repeat 0.5-0.75mg/kg q5-10min (max 3mg/kg total), then infusion 1-4mg/min Preferentially affects ischemic/depolarized tissue over normal myocardium — theoretically well-suited to ischemia-related VT specifically. CNS toxicity (perioral numbness, tremor, confusion, seizures at high levels) is the dose-limiting concern; reduce dose in hepatic impairment or low cardiac output states (reduced clearance)
Mexiletine Chronic VT suppression (often as an adjunct to another antiarrhythmic), congenital LQT3 (where the mutation causes a gain-of-function late sodium current — mexiletine specifically blocks this) 150-200mg PO q8h (oral only, the oral analog of lidocaine) GI side effects (nausea) are common and often dose-limiting; the LQT3-specific indication is a nice example of matching a drug's mechanism to a specific channelopathy subtype (see Vol. 3's Channelopathies section)

Class Ic — Strong Na+ Block, No Effect on Repolarization

The most potent sodium channel blockade of the three subclasses, with no significant effect on repolarization — this potency is exactly why Class Ic use is restricted to structurally normal hearts.

DrugIndicationsInitial DoseTitrationSpecial Considerations
Flecainide AFib "pill-in-the-pocket" rhythm conversion, AFib/flutter maintenance in structurally normal hearts, SVT 50-100mg PO BID (chronic); pill-in-pocket: 200-300mg single dose (weight-based, first dose given under monitored observation) Max 400mg/day (chronic dosing) CONTRAINDICATED in structural heart disease/CAD given the CAST trial finding of increased mortality in post-MI patients — this is one of the most important "know the trial" teaching points in antiarrhythmic pharmacology. Requires a concurrent AV-nodal blocking agent (beta-blocker or non-DHP CCB) when used for AFib, since flecainide can slow atrial rate enough to allow 1:1 AV conduction if flutter develops (paradoxically faster ventricular rate).
Propafenone Same indications as flecainide — AFib pill-in-the-pocket, AFib/flutter maintenance in structurally normal hearts 150mg PO q8h (chronic); pill-in-pocket: 450-600mg single dose (weight-based, first dose monitored) Max 900mg/day Same structural heart disease contraindication as flecainide (CAST trial applies to the whole Ic subclass); has mild intrinsic beta-blocking activity as well, adding a small additional AV-nodal effect
The CAST trial — why structural heart disease is a hard stop for Class Ic (and caution for Ia)

The Cardiac Arrhythmia Suppression Trial found that Class Ic agents, despite effectively suppressing PVCs after MI, increased mortality compared to placebo — the presumed mechanism is that these drugs' potent sodium channel blockade becomes proarrhythmic in scarred, heterogeneous post-infarct myocardium, creating the substrate for reentrant VT rather than preventing it. This single trial fundamentally reshaped how the entire field thinks about antiarrhythmic drug safety, and is the reason structural heart disease/significant CAD is treated as an absolute contraindication for Class Ic agents specifically.

Pill-in-the-pocket requires the first dose to be monitored

Before prescribing flecainide or propafenone for self-administered AFib termination at home, the first dose must be given in a monitored setting to confirm the patient tolerates it without excessive bradycardia, hypotension, or proarrhythmia — this is not a "prescribe and send home" medication on the first exposure.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/electrophysiology before initiating any Class I agent for chronic use — appropriate patient selection (ruling out structural heart disease for Ic, checking QTc for Ia) is essential and not a decision to make without specialist input.
Urgent evaluation
Any new widening of the QRS or QT interval after Class I initiation, or any new arrhythmia in a patient recently started on one of these agents — consider drug-induced proarrhythmia.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for antiarrhythmic selection and monitoring; toxicology for suspected Class I agent overdose (sodium bicarbonate can help reverse sodium-channel-blocker-induced QRS widening, similar to the mechanism used in tricyclic antidepressant overdose).

Antiarrhythmics: Class III

Potassium channel blockers that prolong phase 3 repolarization (see the action potential diagram in the Class I section) — headlined by amiodarone, arguably the single most-used antiarrhythmic in acute cardiology despite a genuinely intimidating toxicity profile.

Class MOA Block potassium channels responsible for phase 3 repolarization — prolonging the action potential duration and the refractory period. This is also exactly why QT prolongation and Torsades risk (see Vol. 3) are the class-defining safety concern.

Amiodarone

IndicationOral Loading/MaintenanceIV/Emergency DosingSpecial Considerations
VT/VF (ACLS), AFib rhythm/rate control, many other atrial and ventricular arrhythmias — the broadest-spectrum antiarrhythmic in use Load 400mg PO BID-TID x1-4 weeks, then 200-400mg PO daily maintenance Cardiac arrest (VF/pulseless VT): 300mg IV push, may repeat 150mg. Stable VT/AFib: load 150mg IV over 10 min, then 1mg/min x6h, then 0.5mg/min x18h Technically a Class III agent but has properties of all four Vaughan Williams classes (Na+ block, beta-blocking activity, Ca2+ channel effects, plus the dominant K+ effect) — this "dirty drug" multi-mechanism profile is part of why it's so broadly effective
Amiodarone's toxicity list is genuinely extensive — know it

Pulmonary: pulmonary fibrosis, potentially irreversible — baseline and periodic PFTs/imaging with chronic use. Thyroid: both hypo- and hyperthyroidism (the iodine content and structural similarity to thyroid hormone drive this) — check TSH at baseline and periodically. Hepatic: transaminitis, rarely progressing to cirrhosis — check LFTs at baseline and periodically. Ocular: corneal microdeposits (nearly universal, usually asymptomatic) and rarely optic neuropathy. Dermatologic: photosensitivity and a slate-gray skin discoloration with long-term use. Cardiac: despite being an antiarrhythmic, still carries QT-prolongation/Torsades risk, though paradoxically lower than other Class III agents at a given degree of QT prolongation.

Drug interactions are extensive too

Amiodarone inhibits multiple CYP enzymes and P-glycoprotein — it significantly raises levels of warfarin (reduce warfarin dose by roughly 30-50% when starting amiodarone and monitor INR closely), digoxin (reduce digoxin dose), and simvastatin (dose-cap simvastatin — see Vol. 1). This interaction burden needs active management, not just passive awareness, any time amiodarone is added to an existing regimen.

Less effective than amiodarone overall — the safety-efficacy tradeoff runs the opposite direction from what the "safer amiodarone" framing might suggest.
Choosing among the Class III agents

Amiodarone remains the most broadly effective and most commonly used given its efficacy across arrhythmia types and relatively lower Torsades risk per degree of QT prolongation — but its extensive extracardiac toxicity makes it a poor choice for long-term use in a young patient who might tolerate an alternative. Dofetilide fills the specific niche of HFrEF patients needing rhythm control without negative inotropy. Dronedarone is for patients specifically wanting to avoid amiodarone's extracardiac toxicity who have preserved EF and non-permanent AFib. Sotalol and ibutilide serve more specific rate/acute-cardioversion roles.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/electrophysiology for initiating any chronic Class III agent — patient selection (renal function, EF, structural disease, baseline QTc) drives which agent is appropriate.
Inpatient admission required
Dofetilide and sotalol initiation both require structured inpatient monitoring per guideline/REMS requirements — this is not negotiable or something to attempt as an outpatient start.
Urgent evaluation
Any significant QTc prolongation or Torsades de pointes in a patient on any Class III agent (see Vol. 3 for acute Torsades management) — the offending drug should generally be held pending evaluation.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for chronic antiarrhythmic selection and monitoring; pharmacy for amiodarone drug-interaction management, especially around warfarin/digoxin/statin co-administration.

Closing this volume with the calcium channel blockers used as antiarrhythmics, and the small set of drugs that don't fit anywhere in the Vaughan Williams framework at all.

Class IV — Non-Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers

Verapamil and diltiazem slow phase 4 depolarization in the SA/AV node (see the action potential diagram in the Class I section) — full dosing tables already live in Vol. 1's Calcium Channel Blockers section; this is the antiarrhythmic-specific context.

Adenosine

MechanismDosingSpecial Considerations
Activates A1 adenosine receptors on AV nodal cells -> opens K+ channels and inhibits the calcium current -> transient, profound AV nodal block. Doesn't fit any Vaughan Williams class — it's often informally called a "Class V" or unclassified agent 6mg rapid IV push (peripheral IV, proximal as possible, followed immediately by a rapid saline flush) -> if ineffective, 12mg rapid IV push, may repeat 12mg once Ultra-short half-life (~10 seconds) — this is both why it works (transient enough to terminate a reentrant SVT circuit without prolonged effects) and why rapid, proximal IV push technique with immediate flush is essential for it to reach the heart at an effective concentration. Transient asystole/flushing/chest discomfort/sense of impending doom are expected, not signs of a complication. Diagnostic AND therapeutic in narrow-complex tachycardia — see Vol. 3's SVT section.
Blunted effect with certain interactions

Caffeine and theophylline are adenosine receptor antagonists — patients with recent significant caffeine intake may need higher doses for the same effect. Dipyridamole blocks adenosine reuptake and potentiates its effect substantially — reduce the dose in patients on this medication. Carbamazepine also potentiates adenosine's AV nodal blocking effect.

Atropine

MechanismDosingSpecial Considerations
Muscarinic (parasympathetic) receptor antagonist — blocks vagal tone on the SA node, increasing heart rate 0.5-1mg IV push, repeat q3-5min, max 3mg total (ACLS bradycardia algorithm) First-line for symptomatic bradycardia — but ineffective for infranodal block (Mobitz II, complete heart block with a wide escape rhythm — see Vol. 3's Bradyarrhythmias section) since the block is below the level vagal tone acts on. Also ineffective in a denervated transplanted heart (see the Cardiothoracic Surgery Atlas's Heart Transplant section) — the vagal pathway atropine blocks doesn't exist in that anatomy.

Isoproterenol

MechanismDosingSpecial Considerations
Non-selective beta-agonist (pure beta-1/beta-2 agonism, no alpha activity) — increases heart rate and contractility, and produces peripheral vasodilation IV infusion 2-10mcg/min, titrate to heart rate response Used for bradycardia-dependent Torsades de pointes (increasing heart rate shortens the QT interval and suppresses the pause-dependent triggering mechanism — see Vol. 3) and for chronotropic support in a denervated transplanted heart where atropine doesn't work (direct beta-agonism bypasses the missing vagal pathway entirely)
Digoxin as an antiarrhythmic — cross-reference, not a new entry

Digoxin's rate-control mechanism (increased vagal tone slowing AV conduction) is covered in the HF/Rate Control sections of this volume — it doesn't fit cleanly into the Vaughan Williams framework either, though it's sometimes informally grouped with adenosine as part of the same "doesn't fit the classic four classes" category.

Quick Reference — Vaughan Williams at a Glance
"Some Block Potassium Channels"

Sodium channel block (Class I) · Beta-blockade (Class II) · Potassium channel block (Class III) · Calcium channel block (Class IV)

The phrase's own words are the memory hook — Sodium, Beta, Potassium, Calcium in class order I through IV. Anything that doesn't fit this pattern (adenosine, digoxin, atropine, isoproterenol) is the "everything else" category worth remembering separately.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Electrophysiology for any arrhythmia requiring escalation beyond first-line acute agents (adenosine, IV rate control), or for chronic rhythm-control strategy planning.
Urgent evaluation
Any bradycardia refractory to atropine — proceed to pacing per the Vol. 3 Bradyarrhythmias framework rather than escalating atropine doses beyond the max.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for complex arrhythmia management; cardiology for acute rate/rhythm control decision-making in the undifferentiated tachyarrhythmia patient.

Mechanism & Classification

Every clinical decision in this file — taper or no taper, IV or oral, which agent for which indication — traces back to two mechanisms: how fast the drug works, and which of two receptors it hits.

MechanismGenomic vs. Non-Genomic Effects

Corticosteroids act through the intracellular glucocorticoid receptor mechanism introduced in Volume 1's Foundational Principles (cytoplasmic receptor, HSP90-chaperoned, translocates to nucleus on ligand binding) — but the clinically relevant point for dosing and timing is that this classic genomic pathway is slow, because it requires new protein transcription and translation.

PathwayOnsetMechanismClinical relevance
GenomicHours (typically 4–8h for measurable clinical effect; full effect over 24–48h)Receptor-glucocorticoid complex → nuclear translocation → transcription of anti-inflammatory genes (annexin A1/lipocortin-1 inhibiting phospholipase A2) and repression of pro-inflammatory genes (NF-κB, AP-1 transrepression)Explains why a single dose of IV methylprednisolone doesn't produce instant bronchodilation in a COPD/asthma exacerbation — the bronchodilators (beta-agonists, anticholinergics) are doing the immediate work while steroids address the underlying inflammatory cascade over the following hours
Non-genomicMinutesDirect membrane-level effects and rapid non-transcriptional receptor signaling at very high dosesClinically minor at conventional doses; more relevant at pulse/mega-doses (e.g., IV methylprednisolone 1g for acute optic neuritis or severe rejection episodes), where rapid non-genomic immunosuppressive effects are part of the theoretical rationale for high-dose pulse therapy
The single most important timing fact in this file
Because the dominant anti-inflammatory mechanism is genomic, steroids given for an acute exacerbation (asthma, COPD, anaphylaxis biphasic-reaction prevention) do not produce their main benefit for several hours — regardless of IV vs. oral route. This is why steroids are given early and empirically at the first sign of a significant exacerbation rather than reserved as a rescue measure, and why they aren't expected to abort a reaction already in progress the way epinephrine or bronchodilators do.

Two ReceptorsGlucocorticoid vs. Mineralocorticoid Receptor

Every corticosteroid drug has some combination of activity at two distinct nuclear receptors — the ratio between them is what separates a "steroid for inflammation" from a "steroid for adrenal replacement," and is covered in full in the Steroid Class Comparison section of this file.

Glucocorticoid receptor (GR) — anti-inflammatory, metabolic, immunosuppressive Mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) — renal Na⁺/K⁺ handling, fluid retention, hypertension risk

Worked ExamplesCommon Conversions

IV Solu-Medrol → oral prednisone at discharge

Methylprednisolone 32 mg IV ≈ prednisone 40 mg oral (ratio ~4:5, following the potency table above) — the actual clinical practice of simply continuing "40mg prednisone" after "125mg methylprednisolone IV" tapers uses this conversion, sometimes rounded pragmatically rather than calculated to the decimal.

Dexamethasone → prednisone equivalence

Prednisone 60 mg ≈ dexamethasone 9 mg. Because dexamethasone's duration of action is so much longer (36–72h vs. 12–36h), converting a multi-day dexamethasone course to prednisone at this exact equivalent dose still usually needs volume/frequency adjustment to match the actual 24-hour exposure profile, not just a straight dose swap.

Physiologic BaselineWhat the Body Normally Makes

Normal daily cortisol production is roughly equivalent to hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day, or prednisone 5 mg/day — this is the number that matters for two very different purposes: it's the target replacement dose in primary/secondary adrenal insufficiency, and it's the rough threshold above which exogenous steroid dosing starts to meaningfully suppress the HPA axis if continued for weeks (see the dedicated Adrenal Suppression section).

IV vs. Oral & Pharmacokinetics

The evidence here corrects a common assumption worth addressing directly: for most indications with an intact gut, IV steroids are not more effective than oral — they're just faster to give when a patient can't swallow.

The EvidenceIs IV Actually Better Than Oral?

Oral prednisone and prednisolone have ~80–100% bioavailability — essentially complete absorption in a patient with functioning gut mucosa and no vomiting. This is not a marginal pharmacokinetic footnote; it's the reason multiple head-to-head trials have found no efficacy difference between routes.

SettingFinding
AECOPD (hospitalized)Multiple RCTs (de Jong et al., and others) and the ATS/ERS guideline found no significant difference in treatment failure, mortality, length of stay, or FEV1 improvement between equivalent-dose IV and oral corticosteroids when oral intake was feasible — oral is now the guideline-preferred route specifically because it's equally effective, cheaper, and avoids IV-line-associated risk
Acute asthma exacerbationMeta-analyses similarly show no difference in pulmonary function improvement or relapse prevention between routes
When IV genuinely mattersImpaired GI absorption (vomiting, ileus, short bowel, severe mucosal edema), inability to swallow/NPO status, shock states with reduced splanchnic perfusion, or need for a very large single bolus dose not practical in oral tablet form (e.g., pulse-dose methylprednisolone 1g for severe rejection or optic neuritis)
Why the "IV is stronger" impression persists anyway
In practice, IV steroids are often given to the sicker subset of patients (more severe exacerbations, unable to tolerate oral intake, ICU-level illness) — creating a confounded clinical impression that the IV route itself drove better outcomes, when it was really illness severity driving both the route choice and the outcome. Controlled trials that hold severity and dose constant consistently fail to show a route-dependent efficacy difference.

Onset TimingHow Long Does Solu-Medrol Actually Take to Work?

Using an AECOPD presentation as the running example: IV methylprednisolone (Solu-Medrol) reaches peak plasma concentration essentially immediately (IV push/infusion), but the genomic anti-inflammatory transcriptional cascade described in the Mechanism section is what actually resolves the underlying airway inflammation — and that takes hours, not minutes.

TimepointWhat's happening
0–30 minPeak plasma concentration reached; negligible genomic effect yet; any early symptomatic improvement in this window is from concurrently-administered bronchodilators, not the steroid
~4–8 hoursDetectable improvement in objective measures (FEV1) begins to emerge in trial data — corresponding to the transcriptional/genomic timeline
24–48 hoursFuller clinical effect established; most trials show the largest incremental FEV1/symptom gains accruing over this window, which is also why "improvement in the first 72 hours" is the standard outcome window cited in AECOPD steroid trials

This timeline is essentially the same regardless of IV or oral route, for the same reason the efficacy is the same — once absorbed, the genomic mechanism doesn't care how the drug got into the bloodstream.

First-Pass & Hepatic HandlingPrednisone → Prednisolone

Prednisone itself is inactive — it requires hepatic 11β-HSD1 conversion (the same enzyme introduced in Volume 1's HPA section) to the active drug, prednisolone. This is clinically relevant in one specific population: patients with severe hepatic impairment may have reduced conversion capacity, which is the rationale for prescribing prednisolone directly (bypassing the conversion step) rather than prednisone in significant liver disease. For most patients with normal hepatic function, prednisone and prednisolone are clinically interchangeable and the distinction is not practically relevant.

Adrenal Suppression

Exogenous glucocorticoid exposure suppresses the same HPA axis mapped in Volume 1 — the practical question is always dose and duration, since suppression is essentially inevitable above a threshold.

MechanismWhy It Happens

Exogenous glucocorticoid provides the same long-loop negative feedback signal to the hypothalamus and pituitary that endogenous cortisol does (see Volume 1, HPA axis) — CRH and ACTH secretion fall, and with sustained suppression, the adrenal cortex itself atrophies from lack of trophic ACTH stimulation. The result is secondary (tertiary, technically — hypothalamic/pituitary-driven) adrenal insufficiency if the exogenous steroid is withdrawn abruptly before the axis has recovered.

ThresholdsDose & Duration That Matter

VariableThreshold for meaningful suppression risk
Dose≥ prednisone 5–7.5 mg/day (or equivalent) — approximately the physiologic replacement dose; above this, exogenous steroid begins to exceed the body's own baseline output
Duration≥ 3–4 weeks at a suppressive dose — current (2024) Endocrine Society and multi-society "good clinical practice" guidance both converge on this window as the point where axis recovery time becomes clinically meaningful
Below both thresholds, no taper is needed
A course under 3–4 weeks, at any dose, can generally be stopped abruptly without tapering and without adrenal function testing — this includes short high-dose "bursts" (e.g., a 5-day prednisone 60mg course, or a 3-day methylprednisolone dose pack). The dose threshold and duration threshold both need to be crossed together for suppression risk to become the dominant concern; a short course, even at a high dose, has not been on board long enough to meaningfully atrophy the axis. The one major exception: patients with recent prior chronic steroid exposure (within the past 6–12 months) may have residual, not-yet-recovered suppression and should be treated more cautiously even for a new short course.

Testing RecoveryMorning Cortisol & Cosyntropin Testing

Morning cortisol levelInterpretation
> 300 nmol/L (~11 mcg/dL)HPA axis has probably recovered — reasonable to continue taper toward discontinuation
150–300 nmol/L (~5–11 mcg/dL)Indeterminate — consider more definitive testing (cosyntropin stimulation) or a slower taper with retesting in several weeks
< 150 nmol/L (~5.4 mcg/dL)Suggestive of ongoing suppression — continue physiologic-dose glucocorticoid coverage and retest in a few months

Definitive confirmation of full recovery uses the low-dose (1 mcg) cosyntropin stimulation test: a peak cortisol ≥500 nmol/L (~18 mcg/dL) at 30 minutes, ideally confirmed on two occasions ≥6 weeks apart, indicates recovered HPA axis function and that daily glucocorticoid supplementation can be safely discontinued.

Never check a random cortisol on a patient taking dexamethasone
Dexamethasone's long half-life and potent HPA suppression will produce a falsely low cortisol result that reflects the drug's own suppressive effect, not the patient's true adrenal reserve. If HPA axis testing is needed in a patient on dexamethasone, switch to an equivalent dose of hydrocortisone or prednisolone first and allow adequate washout before testing. Also account for anything that raises cortisol-binding globulin (pregnancy, oral estrogen — see the transport-protein discussion in Volume 1 Foundational Principles) when interpreting a borderline total cortisol result.

Withdrawal SyndromeDistinct From True Adrenal Insufficiency

Glucocorticoid withdrawal syndrome — fatigue, myalgia, arthralgia, mood changes, nausea — can occur during a taper even when objective HPA axis testing shows adequate cortisol production. It reflects tissue-level physiologic dependence on supraphysiologic glucocorticoid exposure rather than true cortisol deficiency, and is managed by slowing the taper rate and temporarily returning to the last tolerated dose rather than by increasing steroid dose indefinitely or assuming adrenal insufficiency.

Don't ForgetInhaled & Topical Steroids Count Too

High-dose inhaled corticosteroids (e.g., fluticasone ≥500 mcg/day) and, less commonly, extensive high-potency topical steroid use can contribute meaningfully to systemic HPA suppression — this is covered in depth in the Route-Specific Systemic Absorption section, but it's worth flagging here because a patient can appear to be "only on an inhaler" and still be at real risk, especially when combined with a systemic steroid course.

Burst & Taper Protocols

Practical dosing frameworks built directly on the thresholds established in the Adrenal Suppression section.

The Medrol Dose PackWhy It's Shaped the Way It Is

The classic 6-day methylprednisolone dose pack (21 × 4mg tablets: 6-5-4-3-2-1 taper over 6 days, starting at 24mg day 1) is a built-in rapid taper rather than a flat dose — but given the 3–4 week duration threshold for HPA suppression risk, the taper structure here is not primarily about preventing adrenal insufficiency (a 6-day course is nowhere near that threshold). It's more a legacy dosing convention and a way to front-load a higher anti-inflammatory dose while limiting total steroid exposure and side effects (particularly glycemic and mood effects) over a short course.

A flat-dose short burst works just as well physiologically
For most acute indications (gout flare, acute bronchitis-associated bronchospasm, contact dermatitis, mild asthma exacerbation), a flat dose for 5 days (e.g., prednisone 40mg × 5 days, stopped abruptly) is supported by the same evidence as a tapering dose pack — since neither duration crosses the suppression threshold. Society guidelines for asthma/COPD exacerbations (GOLD, GINA) generally recommend a flat 5-day course of prednisone 40mg without a taper for exactly this reason.

When a Real Taper Is NeededLong-Course Protocols

These rates are general frameworks, not rigid rules — the underlying disease's own flare risk (rheumatologic, pulmonary, GI) has to be balanced against suppression risk, and the pace is individualized accordingly. Below ~5mg prednisone/day, consider checking a morning cortisol before the final discontinuation, per the thresholds in the Adrenal Suppression section.

Stress DosingCovering Illness or Surgery During/After Steroid Use

Patients on chronic steroids, or recently tapered off within the suppression-risk window, need stress-dose coverage during acute illness, surgery, or trauma — their suppressed axis cannot mount the normal 5–10x physiologic cortisol surge the body needs during major physiologic stress. Practical framework: continue the patient's usual dose (or equivalent) for minor procedures/illness; add supplemental hydrocortisone (commonly 50–100mg IV, dosed to stressor severity) for major surgery or critical illness; taper back to baseline over 24–72 hours as the acute stressor resolves.

Special Considerations — CKD & Diabetes

Two populations where steroid choice and monitoring genuinely change based on the mechanisms already covered in this file.

CKDChronic Kidney Disease Considerations

Mineralocorticoid activity matters more here

CKD patients already have impaired renal Na⁺/K⁺/fluid handling — choosing an agent with meaningful mineralocorticoid activity (hydrocortisone, cortisone) compounds fluid retention and hypertension risk in a population that often has limited cardiovascular/volume reserve. Where high-dose or prolonged systemic steroids are needed, agents with minimal MC activity (methylprednisolone, dexamethasone) are often preferred specifically to avoid stacking fluid retention on top of reduced renal clearance capacity.

No renal dose adjustment for most agents

Prednisone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone do not require dose reduction for reduced GFR — hepatic metabolism dominates clearance for these agents. This is a common point of confusion since so many other drug classes do require renal dose adjustment.

Volume/potassium monitoring

Even with low-MC-activity agents, high-dose or prolonged glucocorticoid therapy in CKD warrants closer monitoring of volume status, blood pressure, and potassium than in a patient with normal renal reserve — the margin for error is simply smaller.

Additive glucose and bone effects

CKD-associated bone disease and CKD-associated glucose intolerance are both worsened by glucocorticoid exposure — the special considerations below for diabetes apply with less buffer in CKD, and steroid-associated osteoporosis risk (see Volume 1 calcium-phosphate section for baseline CKD-mineral bone disease) is additive rather than independent.

DiabetesSteroid-Induced Hyperglycemia — Mechanism & Pattern

Glucocorticoids raise glucose through three simultaneous mechanisms: hepatic gluconeogenesis (direct transcriptional effect — see Volume 1 HPA function section), peripheral insulin resistance (reduced muscle/fat glucose uptake), and impaired pancreatic beta-cell insulin secretion at higher doses. The combination can unmask previously unrecognized diabetes or acutely worsen known diabetes, sometimes dramatically.

Agent (morning dose)Peak hyperglycemic effectPractical implication
Prednisone~4–8 hours post-dose — afternoon/early evening peak, often normalizing overnightFasting/morning glucose can look deceptively normal — afternoon/evening checks are essential and morning-only monitoring will miss the problem
Dexamethasone~7–9 hours post-dose, but with a much longer tail (12–36h+) given its longer half-lifeHyperglycemia is more sustained and less likely to normalize overnight — often needs longer-acting insulin coverage rather than an NPH-only strategy
Practical insulin-matching strategy
Because morning-dosed prednisone's glucose effect peaks and fades within roughly the same window as NPH insulin's action profile, pairing morning prednisone with morning NPH is a common, mechanistically sound inpatient strategy — the insulin's peak effect lines up with the steroid-driven glucose peak, then both fade toward evening. For dexamethasone or multiple daily steroid dosing, a basal-bolus regimen is generally more appropriate given the flatter, more sustained hyperglycemic profile. In all cases, insulin doses must be tapered down in parallel with the steroid taper — failing to do so is a well-recognized, preventable cause of hypoglycemia as the steroid dose (and its counter-regulatory glucose-raising effect) falls.

Diagnostic threshold for steroid-induced diabetes: random glucose ≥200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) on more than one occasion, or newly elevated HbA1c ≥6.5%, in the temporal context of corticosteroid exposure. Risk factors for a more severe glycemic response include higher steroid dose (particularly prednisone-equivalent ≥50mg/day), pre-existing prediabetes/obesity, and higher baseline HbA1c.

"Local" Steroids That Aren't Entirely Local

Inhaled, intranasal, and topical steroids are prescribed for a targeted local effect — but every one of them has some systemic absorption, and the degree varies enormously by molecule, not just by dose.

What Determines Systemic AbsorptionThree Molecular Properties

PropertyEffect on systemic exposure
Oral bioavailability of the swallowed fractionA large fraction of any inhaled/intranasal dose is swallowed rather than reaching the lung/nasal mucosa — a drug with high first-pass hepatic metabolism (low oral bioavailability) contributes little systemic exposure from this swallowed portion, while one with high oral bioavailability adds meaningfully to systemic load
Lipophilicity / receptor residence timeMore lipophilic molecules partition more effectively into and linger longer in local tissue, which is part of what makes them potent locally, but can also mean slower clearance and more prolonged systemic presence if absorbed
Local vs. systemic metabolismSome molecules are extensively metabolized by lung/nasal/skin tissue itself before ever reaching the bloodstream (a form of pre-systemic clearance beyond the classic hepatic first-pass)

Topical SteroidsAbsorption Depends on Site, Occlusion & Potency Class

Topical corticosteroids are classified into 7 potency classes by vasoconstrictor assay (Class I super-high potency like clobetasol propionate, down to Class VII lowest potency like hydrocortisone 1%) — independent of the systemic equivalency table used for oral/IV agents. Systemic absorption through intact skin is generally low but rises sharply with: thin skin sites (face, genitals, eyelids — absorption can be 10–40x greater than palms/soles), occlusion (dressings, diapers, skin folds), disrupted skin barrier (extensive dermatitis, burns), and large body surface area treated. Clinically significant HPA suppression from topical steroids is uncommon but well-documented, particularly in infants (higher surface-area-to-weight ratio) treated with high-potency agents over large areas or under occlusion.

Steroid Class ComparisonGlucocorticoid vs. Mineralocorticoid Activity Spectrum

This is the same GC/MC potency data from the Potency & Equivalency table, reframed around the clinical question of which agent for which physiologic goal — the two ends of the spectrum are used for essentially opposite reasons.

Fludrocortisone — mineralocorticoid-predominant

GC:MC activity ratio strongly favors MC effect. Used specifically for its mineralocorticoid action — replacement therapy in primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease) where aldosterone production is lost alongside cortisol, and occasionally in refractory orthostatic hypotension. Its glucocorticoid potency is largely incidental to why it's prescribed.

Dexamethasone/betamethasone — glucocorticoid-only, essentially

Negligible mineralocorticoid activity. Preferred specifically when fluid retention must be avoided — cerebral edema (where sodium/water retention would be counterproductive), or in patients with heart failure/hypertension/CKD needing high-dose glucocorticoid effect without compounding volume overload. Also preferred for suppression testing purposes (though never for testing the axis itself, per the Adrenal Suppression section) and antenatal fetal lung maturation, where the lack of mineralocorticoid crossover simplifies the pharmacology.

Hydrocortisone — balanced, physiologic

Retains meaningful mineralocorticoid activity (it's the native hormone, after all) — this is exactly why it's preferred for adrenal insufficiency replacement over synthetic glucocorticoid-only agents: it approximates the body's own combined GC/MC output in one molecule, though primary adrenal insufficiency still typically needs added fludrocortisone for full mineralocorticoid replacement.

Prednisone/methylprednisolone — the everyday middle ground

Low-to-minimal mineralocorticoid activity with strong glucocorticoid potency — the default choice for most anti-inflammatory/immunosuppressive indications (asthma/COPD bursts, autoimmune disease, transplant) specifically because fluid retention is not usually the dominant concern, and the intermediate duration of action is practical for once-daily dosing.

The clinical takeaway, in one line
Choosing a corticosteroid is really choosing a point on the glucocorticoid-to-mineralocorticoid spectrum for the specific physiologic problem at hand — replacement therapy wants both effects (hydrocortisone ± fludrocortisone), most inflammatory/immunosuppressive indications want glucocorticoid effect without fluid retention (prednisone/methylprednisolone), and situations where fluid retention would actively harm the patient want glucocorticoid effect with none of the mineralocorticoid baggage (dexamethasone/betamethasone).

Steroid Selection by Indication

Why one specific agent gets chosen over another for a specific condition — built directly on the potency, route, and GC/MC-spectrum mechanics covered earlier in this file. Organized by outpatient vs. inpatient setting where that distinction actually changes management.

OutpatientAmbulatory Indications

Asthma / COPD exacerbation (mild-moderate, ambulatory)Prednisone

Typical regimen: Prednisone 40–60mg PO daily × 5 days, flat dose, no taper needed (below the 3–4 week suppression threshold).
Why this agent: Oral bioavailability is equivalent to IV (see Routes section) and there's no reason to default to a parenteral route in a patient tolerating oral intake.
Special considerations: Counsel on afternoon/evening glucose monitoring in diabetics (see Special Populations); avoid in active peptic ulcer disease without GI prophylaxis if also on NSAIDs.

Croup (mild-moderate, outpatient/ED discharge)Dexamethasone

Typical regimen: Dexamethasone 0.15–0.6mg/kg PO, single dose (max ~10–12mg) — RCTs show 0.15mg/kg is non-inferior to 0.6mg/kg for mild-moderate croup, though 0.6mg/kg remains the more commonly used dose and may have an edge in more severe presentations.
Why this agent specifically: Long half-life (36–54h) covers the typical symptom duration with a single dose — no other agent's kinetics match the illness course this well with one administration, which is exactly why croup guidelines default to dexamethasone rather than prednisolone (shorter half-life, more return visits in head-to-head trials).
Special considerations: Oral, IM, and IV routes are equally effective — no reason to give an injection if the child can tolerate the oral dose.

Acute gout flare (renal impairment or NSAID-intolerant)Prednisone

Typical regimen: Prednisone 30–40mg PO daily, tapered over 5–10 days.
Why this agent: Effective alternative when NSAIDs are contraindicated (CKD, GI bleeding risk, heart failure) or colchicine isn't tolerated — a straightforward glucocorticoid-potency play with no need for mineralocorticoid-sparing considerations.
Special considerations: Higher-risk population for steroid-induced hyperglycemia given gout's frequent comorbidity with metabolic syndrome/diabetes.

Mild-moderate Crohn's disease (ileal/right colon)Budesonide

Typical regimen: Budesonide 9mg PO daily (controlled ileal-release formulation).
Why this agent specifically: Formulated for targeted release in the terminal ileum/right colon, and undergoes ~90% first-pass hepatic metabolism — meaning strong local anti-inflammatory effect with comparatively minimal systemic glucocorticoid exposure (far less HPA suppression, glycemic, and bone effect than an equivalent systemic dose of prednisone). Only appropriate when disease is anatomically limited to the region the formulation targets — more extensive or colonic Crohn's disease needs systemic therapy instead.
Special considerations: Not a substitute for systemic steroids in severe or extensive disease, or in ulcerative colitis (different anatomic distribution than budesonide's release site).

InpatientHospital-Based Indications

Adult bacterial meningitisDexamethasone

Typical regimen: Dexamethasone 10mg IV, given 15–20 minutes before or with the first dose of antibiotics, then q6h × 4 days.
Why this agent specifically: The landmark European trial (de Gans et al., NEJM 2002) showed reduced unfavorable outcomes and mortality, with the clearest benefit in pneumococcal meningitis — but only when given before or with antibiotics, since the rationale is blunting the inflammatory burst from bacterial lysis, not treating established inflammation. Benefit in meningococcal meningitis is less clearly established.
Special considerations: Dexamethasone can reduce CNS penetration of vancomycin — a real concern with highly resistant pneumococcal strains, requiring closer attention to vancomycin dosing/levels. Never give after antibiotics have already been started — the timing is the entire point.

Septic shock, refractory to fluids + vasopressorsHydrocortisone

Typical regimen: Hydrocortisone 200mg/day IV (50mg q6h or continuous infusion), initiated once norepinephrine/epinephrine requirement reaches ≥0.25 mcg/kg/min for at least 4 hours (Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021, reaffirmed 2026 — weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).
Why this agent specifically: Hydrocortisone's combined glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity is deliberately chosen here — critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency involves relative deficiency of both, and hydrocortisone approximates physiologic replacement rather than pure anti-inflammatory dosing.
Special considerations: Shortens time to shock resolution but has not shown a consistent mortality benefit across trials — a weak recommendation, not a mandate. ICU-only, requires ongoing hemodynamic monitoring.

Adrenal crisisHydrocortisone

Typical regimen: Hydrocortisone 100mg IV bolus, then 200mg/24h (continuous infusion or 50mg q6h), with aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation.
Why this agent specifically: Same logic as septic shock above but even more directly applicable — true adrenal insufficiency needs both GC and MC replacement, and hydrocortisone is the only commonly stocked IV agent that provides meaningful amounts of both in one drug.
Special considerations: Don't delay treatment for confirmatory labs in a clinically suspected crisis — draw a cortisol/ACTH level if feasible without delaying the first dose, but treat empirically regardless.

Severe COVID-19 / pneumonia requiring supplemental oxygenDexamethasone

Typical regimen: Dexamethasone 6mg IV/PO daily × up to 10 days (RECOVERY trial protocol).
Why this agent specifically: The RECOVERY trial showed a mortality benefit specifically in patients requiring supplemental oxygen or invasive ventilation — the same trial found no benefit, and a signal toward harm, in patients who did not require supplemental oxygen. This is a rare, well-documented example of a steroid indication being tightly bound to physiologic severity rather than diagnosis alone.
Special considerations: Do not extrapolate this regimen to patients with mild disease not requiring oxygen — the evidence specifically does not support it there, and early trial-era over-extension of this protocol to milder cases was a recognized practice pitfall.

Malignant epidural spinal cord compressionDexamethasone

Typical regimen: Dexamethasone loading dose (commonly 10mg IV, though practice varies 4–24mg depending on severity/institution), followed by a scheduled taper alongside definitive treatment (radiation/surgery).
Why this agent specifically: No mineralocorticoid activity avoids compounding fluid overload in a bedbound oncology population, and its potency/long duration allows less frequent dosing during an urgent, time-pressured presentation.
Special considerations: A neuro-oncologic emergency — steroids are started empirically on clinical suspicion, before imaging confirmation, given the risk of irreversible cord injury with delay.

Post-extubation stridor prevention (high-risk ventilated patients)Methylprednisolone

Typical regimen: Methylprednisolone given in divided doses over the 12–24 hours preceding a planned extubation in patients identified as high-risk (failed cuff-leak test, prolonged intubation).
Why this agent specifically: A 2023 network meta-analysis found methylprednisolone had a lower risk of post-extubation stridor and reintubation compared to both hydrocortisone and (to a lesser, non-significant degree) dexamethasone — one of the few areas where head-to-head comparative trial data supports one systemic agent over another rather than treating them as interchangeable at equivalent doses.
Special considerations: Timing matters — benefit is tied to dosing in advance of a planned extubation, not as rescue therapy after stridor has already developed.

Verify before relying
Doses, timing windows, and guideline recommendations above reflect the trials and society guidelines cited at the time of writing — confirm current institutional protocols and the latest guideline versions before applying to a specific patient, particularly for rapidly evolving areas like COVID-19 and sepsis management.

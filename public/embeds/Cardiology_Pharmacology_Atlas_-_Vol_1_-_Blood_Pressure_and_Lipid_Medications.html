01 / Blood Pressure & Lipid Medications

Diuretics

Three classes, three nephron segments, three very different potency and electrolyte profiles — the site of action explains almost everything else about how each class behaves clinically.

Potency tracks directly with how much filtered sodium passes through the segment each class blocks — loop diuretics (25% of filtered Na+) are far more potent than thiazides (5-8%) or K-sparing agents (2-3%).
Class MOA All diuretics ultimately work by blocking sodium (and therefore obligate water) reabsorption at a specific nephron segment — the segment determines both potency and the resulting electrolyte disturbance pattern.

Thiazide & Thiazide-Like Diuretics

Block the Na-Cl cotransporter (NCC) in the distal convoluted tubule. Despite modest natriuretic potency, thiazides are uniquely effective long-term antihypertensives — the durable BP-lowering effect outlasts the diuretic effect and is thought to involve a component of direct vasodilation.

Electrolyte signature

Thiazides cause hypokalemia, hyponatremia (can be profound, especially in elderly women), hypercalcemia (increased distal Ca2+ reabsorption — actually useful in patients with osteoporosis or calcium-containing kidney stones), hyperuricemia (can precipitate gout), and can worsen glucose tolerance. Check a basic metabolic panel within 2-4 weeks of initiation or dose increase.

Loop Diuretics

Block the Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2) in the thick ascending limb — the most potent diuretic class, and the mainstay for volume management in heart failure and edematous states rather than primary hypertension.

Ototoxicity with rapid IV administration

Rapid IV push of high-dose loop diuretics (especially furosemide and ethacrynic acid) carries dose- and rate-dependent ototoxicity risk — when giving large IV doses, infusing over 30+ minutes (or as a continuous infusion) rather than rapid push reduces this risk without sacrificing diuretic effect.

Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

Two distinct mechanisms converge on the same collecting-duct segment: aldosterone receptor antagonism (spironolactone, eplerenone — these are also the mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists used as an HF pillar, see Vol. 2) and direct epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) blockade (amiloride, triamterene).

Hyperkalemia is the shared, defining risk

All four K-sparing agents can cause clinically significant hyperkalemia, particularly when combined with an ACEi/ARB/ARNI (see the RAAS Blockade section) or in renal impairment — this combination is extremely common in heart failure regimens specifically because both drug classes are guideline-recommended pillars, making potassium monitoring a routine, ongoing part of HF medication management rather than a one-time check.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Nephrology for diuretic-resistant edema requiring sequential nephron blockade strategies, or any significant electrolyte derangement from diuretic therapy that isn't straightforward to correct.
Urgent evaluation
Significant hyponatremia (especially symptomatic or rapidly developing) in a patient on a thiazide, or significant hyperkalemia in a patient on a K-sparing agent combined with RAAS blockade.
Who to call
Cardiology or nephrology for diuretic regimen optimization in refractory volume overload; pharmacy for complex interaction review in patients on multiple potassium-affecting medications.

RAAS Blockade

Four drug classes hit the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system at four different points — same downstream effect on blood pressure, meaningfully different side effect profiles.

ACE inhibitors block both angiotensin II formation AND bradykinin breakdown (the source of ACEi-specific cough/angioedema); ARBs block only the AT1 receptor, sparing bradykinin metabolism entirely.
Never combine two RAAS blockers

ACEi + ARB, ACEi + direct renin inhibitor, or ARB + direct renin inhibitor combinations increase hyperkalemia, hypotension, and acute kidney injury risk without a clear efficacy benefit — trial data (ONTARGET, ALTITUDE) established this clearly enough that dual RAAS blockade is now avoided as routine practice. The ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan) is a partial exception with its own specific washout requirement (below), not a license to combine it freely with other RAAS agents.

ACE Inhibitors

Block the enzyme converting angiotensin I to angiotensin II — and simultaneously block ACE's other job of breaking down bradykinin, which explains the class-specific cough and angioedema risk.

DrugInitial DoseTitration / MaxSpecial Considerations

Cough — the classic differentiator, mechanistically explained

ACEi-associated dry cough (occurring in a meaningful minority of patients, more common in women and people of East Asian descent) is caused by accumulated bradykinin and substance P in the airway from blocked ACE-mediated breakdown — since ARBs don't touch bradykinin metabolism at all, switching from an ACEi to an ARB reliably resolves the cough and is the standard next step rather than trying a different ACEi (the cough is a class effect, not drug-specific).

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Emergent evaluation
Any angioedema in a patient on an ACEi — airway assessment takes priority; discontinue the ACEi permanently (do not rechallenge, and avoid ARBs too given rare cross-reactivity, though lower than with continued ACEi use).
Urgent evaluation
Significant AKI or hyperkalemia after ACEi/ARB initiation — evaluate for undiagnosed bilateral renal artery stenosis (see Vascular Vol. 1) if the rise is disproportionate.
Who to call
Nephrology for significant renal function changes; allergy/immunology for recurrent angioedema of unclear mechanism after ACEi discontinuation.

Calcium Channel Blockers

One receptor family, two clinically opposite personalities — dihydropyridines are pure vasodilators, non-dihydropyridines are rate-controlling cardiac depressants that happen to also lower blood pressure.

This selectivity difference is the single most important thing to remember about CCBs — it dictates which drugs are safe to combine with beta-blockers and which combination risks dangerous bradycardia.

Dihydropyridines

Named for their shared chemical ring structure — all generic names in this subclass end in "-dipine," making them easy to recognize as a group.

Verapamil in wide-complex tachycardia can be catastrophic

If a wide-complex tachycardia is actually VT rather than SVT with aberrancy (see Vol. 3), giving verapamil can cause profound, hard-to-reverse hypotension and hemodynamic collapse without terminating the arrhythmia — this is a major reason the default teaching for uncertain wide-complex tachycardia is "treat as VT" (see Vol. 3's Wide Complex Tachycardia section) rather than reaching for a calcium channel blocker.

Constipation with verapamil

Verapamil has more pronounced GI smooth muscle effects than diltiazem, making constipation a genuinely common and sometimes dose-limiting side effect — worth proactively counseling patients about, especially the elderly or those already prone to constipation from other medications.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for CCB use in reduced EF heart failure where negative inotropy is a genuine concern, or for complex rate-control regimens combining CCBs with other AV-nodal blocking agents.
Urgent evaluation
Any suspected CCB overdose/toxicity — can produce refractory shock and bradycardia; high-dose insulin euglycemia therapy and lipid emulsion are specific antidotal strategies beyond standard supportive care.
Who to call
Toxicology for suspected significant CCB overdose; cardiology for rate-control regimen optimization in complex arrhythmia patients.

Beta-Blockers

The class with the most clinically important internal variation in all of cardiology pharmacology — selectivity, intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, and alpha-blocking activity all change how an individual agent behaves.

Every beta-blocker's side effect and safety profile traces back to this one distinction: how much beta-2 activity gets blocked alongside the desired beta-1 effect.

Cardioselective (Beta-1-Selective) Agents

These preferentially block beta-1 receptors at standard clinical doses, sparing beta-2-mediated bronchodilation and vasodilation — the preferred choice in patients with reactive airway disease, peripheral arterial disease, or diabetes (where masked hypoglycemia awareness is a concern).

Intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA) — a rarely-tested but real nuance

Pindolol and acebutolol are partial agonists at the beta receptor — they block the receptor from excessive catecholamine stimulation while providing a small amount of baseline stimulation themselves. This produces less resting bradycardia than full antagonists, but ISA agents have NOT shown the same post-MI mortality benefit as non-ISA beta-blockers (metoprolol, carvedilol, bisoprolol) — avoid ISA agents specifically in post-MI and HFrEF patients where the evidence base is built on non-ISA drugs.

Mnemonic — Cardioselective (Beta-1-Selective) Agents
"A BEAM" of selectivity aimed at the heart

Acebutolol · Bisoprolol · Esmolol · Atenolol · Metoprolol

Picture a narrow, focused beam of light aimed exclusively at the heart (beta-1) rather than flooding the whole room (beta-2 effects in lungs/vasculature/liver) — that's the visual for "selective." Nebivolol and betaxolol are also cardioselective but postdate the classic mnemonic, so add them as a mental footnote: "A BEAM, plus Nebivolol and Betaxolol." Everything NOT on this list (propranolol, nadolol, timolol, sotalol, pindolol, carvedilol, labetalol) is non-selective or combined alpha/beta.

Never stop a beta-blocker abruptly

Chronic beta-blockade upregulates beta-receptor density and sensitivity as a compensatory adaptation — abrupt discontinuation can produce a rebound sympathetic surge with tachycardia, hypertension, and in patients with underlying CAD, precipitation of angina or MI. Any planned discontinuation should be tapered, and any patient who has run out of or stopped their beta-blocker unintentionally deserves specific attention to this risk.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for HFrEF beta-blocker titration in a patient with borderline hemodynamics, or for sotalol initiation requiring inpatient QTc monitoring.
Urgent evaluation
Significant bradycardia or hypotension after dose increase; any beta-blocker overdose (glucagon is a specific antidote given its cAMP-generating mechanism independent of the blocked beta receptor).
Who to call
Cardiology for HF-specific titration questions; toxicology for significant beta-blocker overdose/toxicity.

Alpha-Blockers, Central Agents & Vasodilators

Third- and fourth-line agents in most algorithms — reserved for resistant hypertension, specific comorbidities, or hypertensive emergency, and each with a distinctive mechanism worth knowing even if prescribed less often.

Alpha-1 Blockers

Block postsynaptic alpha-1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle, producing vasodilation — largely fallen out of favor as first-line antihypertensives after the ALLHAT trial showed increased heart failure risk compared to a diuretic, but retain a niche role.

Alpha-1 blockers characteristically cause pronounced orthostatic hypotension with the very first dose (before the body's compensatory mechanisms adjust) — this is why the first dose is conventionally given at bedtime, and patients should be counseled to rise slowly and avoid driving until they know how they'll respond.

Act within the CNS (not peripherally) to reduce sympathetic outflow — stimulating presynaptic alpha-2 receptors in the brainstem, which paradoxically reduces peripheral sympathetic tone.

Where these fit in the broader algorithm

None of the agents in this section are first-line for routine hypertension — they enter the picture for resistant hypertension (uncontrolled on 3+ agents including a diuretic), specific comorbidities (BPH for alpha-blockers, HFrEF in specific populations for hydralazine/nitrates), or hypertensive emergency requiring IV titratable therapy. Recognizing when a patient has escalated to needing one of these agents is itself a signal to reassess for secondary hypertension causes (see Vol. 1a) if not already done.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology or hypertension specialist for truly resistant hypertension requiring minoxidil or complex multi-agent regimens; nephrology if secondary hypertension workup hasn't been completed.
ICU admission
Any patient requiring nitroprusside infusion — this needs continuous arterial line monitoring and ICU-level nursing given the drug's potency and narrow margin for overcorrection.
Who to call
Cardiology/critical care for IV vasodilator titration in hypertensive emergency; toxicology if cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity from prolonged nitroprusside is suspected.

Lipid-Lowering Therapy

Statins remain the foundation, but the non-statin armamentarium has expanded substantially — closing out this volume with every major class in current use, cross-referenced to the 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline goals from Vol. 1a.

Statins & Intensity Categories

All statins share the same core mechanism (HMG-CoA reductase inhibition, upregulating hepatic LDL receptors — see Vol. 1a's lipid pathway diagram) — they differ mainly in potency-per-milligram and drug interaction profile.

The 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline goals (Vol. 1a) are hit by matching intensity category to risk category — high-intensity statin is now the default starting point for most ASCVD and very-high-risk patients rather than a later escalation step.

Statin-associated muscle symptoms — approach it systematically

True statin-induced myopathy with significant CK elevation is uncommon; most reported muscle symptoms occur at similar rates to placebo in blinded trial data (the nocebo effect is substantial — see Vol. 1a). Before abandoning statin therapy: check CK, screen for interacting drugs and hypothyroidism, consider a different statin (pravastatin/pitavastatin/rosuvastatin have the least interaction burden) or alternate-day dosing before concluding the patient is truly statin-intolerant. Rhabdomyolysis is rare but is the reason for the interaction cautions above.

The evidence-based escalation order

Statin (matched to risk-appropriate intensity) remains the foundation for essentially everyone. Ezetimibe is the first add-on given its efficacy, tolerability, and cost. PCSK9 inhibitors or inclisiran follow for patients still above goal on maximal statin + ezetimibe, particularly very-high-risk ASCVD patients. Bempedoic acid is a reasonable option specifically for statin-intolerant patients. Icosapent ethyl is added specifically for elevated triglycerides in patients already on a statin, given its unique outcomes evidence among the omega-3 options. Fibrates, bile acid sequestrants, and niacin now occupy narrower, more specific niches (severe hypertriglyceridemia, statin intolerance combined with other intolerances, or add-on triglyceride/LDL management) rather than routine first- or second-line use.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Lipid specialist/cardiology for severe or refractory hypercholesterolemia (consider familial hypercholesterolemia — see Vol. 1a), or for PCSK9i/inclisiran candidacy and insurance authorization navigation.
Urgent evaluation
Severe hypertriglyceridemia (generally >500-1000 mg/dL) given acute pancreatitis risk — this needs prompt triglyceride-lowering therapy, sometimes including temporary fasting/insulin infusion in extreme cases, not routine outpatient follow-up.
Who to call
Lipidology/preventive cardiology for complex or refractory dyslipidemia; genetics for confirmed or suspected familial hypercholesterolemia given cascade family screening implications.

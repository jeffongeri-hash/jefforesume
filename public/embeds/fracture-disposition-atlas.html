Lower Extremity Fracture Disposition Atlas

Fracture pattern → energy → disposition logic, with a focused tibial shaft admit-vs-discharge framework (Sarmiento criteria), Gustilo-Anderson open fracture grading, acute compartment syndrome, and a quick-reference disposition table spanning femur to toe.

Pattern encodes ENERGY, and energy predicts everything that follows — soft tissue injury, stability, compartment syndrome risk, healing, and therefore disposition. Describe every fracture the same way every time: location, pattern, displacement, angulation, shortening, rotation, comminution, open vs. closed, articular involvement.

Transverse

EnergyDirect blow / bending force — moderate energy.
BehaviorPerpendicular to the long axis. Relatively STABLE to shortening once reduced (broad end-on bony contact) but prone to ANGULATION. Broad surface contact favors healing.
Disposition signalThe most cast-amenable tibial pattern if alignment meets criteria — but transverse tibias are also the classic pattern to lose reduction over the first 3 weeks.

Oblique

EnergyAngulated/bending force with an axial component.
BehaviorDiagonal (typically >30° to the axis). UNSTABLE — tends to shorten and translate because the fragments slide along the fracture plane.
Disposition signalInherently unstable to axial load; frequently fails cast management and drifts into shortening. Lower threshold for fixation.

Spiral

EnergyTORSIONAL (twisting) — classically a planted foot with body rotation.
BehaviorHelical fracture line encircling the shaft. Long fracture surface = good healing potential, but ROTATIONALLY UNSTABLE.
Disposition signalMalrotation is the failure mode and it is poorly tolerated and hard to judge on plain films. In adults, spiral tibias commonly get nailed. In a non-ambulatory child, a spiral femur/tibia must trigger a non-accidental trauma assessment.

Butterfly wedge

EnergyHigher-energy bending — a wedge fragment blown off the compression side.
BehaviorA separate triangular fragment. Marks a higher-energy mechanism and less intrinsic stability than the pattern alone suggests.
Disposition signalHigher energy = more soft tissue injury = higher compartment syndrome risk. Usually operative.

Comminuted

EnergyHIGH energy — the energy is what fragmented the bone, and that same energy went through the soft tissues.
Behavior>2 fragments. Intrinsically unstable in every plane. Frequently associated with significant periosteal and soft tissue stripping, which is what actually impairs healing.
Disposition signalOPERATIVE. High compartment syndrome risk — admit and monitor serially even if the initial exam is reassuring.

Segmental

EnergyVery high energy — two complete fracture lines isolating a free bone segment.
BehaviorThe intercalary segment is devascularized (stripped of blood supply at both ends). High nonunion rate.
Disposition signalOPERATIVE, essentially always. Treat as a high-energy injury with maximal compartment vigilance.

Angulated

EnergyDescriptor, not a pattern — applies to any of the above.
BehaviorDescribe the direction by where the APEX points (apex anterior/posterior/varus/valgus) and quantify in degrees on BOTH the AP and lateral.
Disposition signalThis is the number that drives the Sarmiento decision — see the Tibial Shaft tab.

Displaced / translated

EnergyDescriptor — quantify as a % of shaft width.
BehaviorDescribe by the position of the DISTAL fragment relative to the proximal. Note that >50% translation greatly reduces the healing surface.
Disposition signalDisplacement alone is less predictive than angulation/shortening for the tibia, but marked translation predicts cast failure.
Clinical Pearls
  • Describe the DISTAL fragment relative to the PROXIMAL — that is the universal convention and prevents miscommunication with the surgeon on the phone.
  • The pattern tells you the energy; the energy tells you about the SOFT TISSUES; the soft tissues drive the complications. A 'simple-looking' fracture line from a high-energy mechanism is still a high-energy injury.
  • Always image the JOINT ABOVE AND BELOW. For the tibia that means the knee and the ankle — and it is how you catch the Maisonneuve and the ipsilateral femur (floating knee).

The tibial shaft is the most commonly fractured long bone, and it is the one where the ED disposition decision carries the most consequence. The whole question reduces to: (1) is it OPEN? (2) is there COMPARTMENT SYNDROME or vascular injury? (3) does the alignment meet Sarmiento's criteria? (4) can they safely bear weight and be reliably followed? A 'no' to any of the first two, or a fail on the third, means the patient does not go home.

Why the Tibia Is Different

The tibia is subcutaneous along its entire anteromedial border — there is essentially no soft tissue envelope between skin and bone. This is why tibial fractures are the most common OPEN long bone fracture, why even low-energy falls produce open injuries, and why a tiny 'poke hole' over the shin must be assumed to communicate with the fracture.

The blood supply is precarious. The nutrient artery enters proximally and the distal third is watershed — which is why DISTAL third tibial fractures have notably higher nonunion and delayed union rates, and why periosteal stripping from high-energy injury is so damaging.

The leg contains four tight fascial compartments and the tibia is the single most common cause of acute compartment syndrome. Critically, compartment syndrome occurs in CLOSED and OPEN fractures alike — an open wound does NOT decompress the compartment, and believing otherwise is a classic and dangerous error.

The Sarmiento Criteria — Who Can Actually Be Casted

Non-operative management (long-leg cast → functional brace) is appropriate ONLY when ALL of the following hold: (1) LOW-ENERGY mechanism with minimal soft tissue injury (Tscherne grade 0–1); (2) a STABLE pattern meeting acceptable alignment — coronal angulation <5°, sagittal angulation <10°, rotation <5°, shortening <1 cm; and (3) the patient can BEAR WEIGHT in the cast or brace.

If more than one or two reduction attempts are needed to hold alignment, or shortening exceeds ~1.5 cm, non-operative treatment should be ABANDONED — that fracture has told you it is unstable.

Even when criteria are met, 25–40% of cast-managed tibial shaft fractures require cast change/wedging or conversion to surgery, and loss of reduction happens in the FIRST 3 WEEKS. That is precisely why close radiographic follow-up is not optional and why the discharge decision hinges on reliable follow-up.

The honest evidence note: a prospective multicenter comparison of IM nailing vs. casting in STABLE tibial shaft fractures found no clear difference in alignment, union, return to work, or function at 6 months. So for the genuinely stable, low-energy, well-aligned fracture, casting is a legitimate evidence-supported choice — not a compromise. The controversy is real; even trauma experts lack consensus.

Absolute Operative Indications — These Patients Are Admitted

OPEN FRACTURE (any Gustilo grade) — to the OR for debridement; IV antibiotics within 1 hour.

VASCULAR INJURY — emergent. IIIC by definition.

COMPARTMENT SYNDROME (or serious concern for it) — emergent fasciotomy.

UNSTABLE PATTERN / alignment failing Sarmiento criteria, or a fracture that will not hold reduction.

IPSILATERAL FEMUR FRACTURE (floating knee) — mandates fixation.

POLYTRAUMA — fixation permits mobilization and pulmonary toilet.

SEGMENTAL or significantly COMMINUTED patterns.

INABILITY TO BEAR WEIGHT in a cast/brace, or an unreliable follow-up situation.

Intramedullary nailing is the workhorse for operative tibial shafts: it controls length, alignment, and rotation, permits early weight-bearing and knee/ankle motion, and gets people back to work sooner.

The Discharge Decision — What Must Be True

A tibial shaft fracture goes home ONLY if ALL are true: closed, neurovascularly intact, low-energy with an intact soft tissue envelope, alignment within Sarmiento criteria (ideally after a reduction that HELD), no compartment concern after an adequate observation period, adequate analgesia achieved ORALLY, able to mobilize safely (crutches/walker with PT clearance), reliable social situation, and confirmed orthopedic follow-up within about a week with repeat films.

Discharge instructions must be explicit and documented: elevate to heart level, do NOT let the cast get wet, and return IMMEDIATELY for increasing pain despite elevation and analgesia, numbness/tingling, inability to move the toes, or a cast that feels too tight. That last set is a compartment syndrome return precaution and it needs to be in the chart.

The single most important safety point: escalating pain and increasing analgesic requirement in a casted tibia is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise. Never manage that over the phone with more opioid — that patient comes back and the cast comes off.

If ANY doubt exists about compartment syndrome, reliability of follow-up, or the patient's ability to recognize and act on warning signs — ADMIT. The asymmetry of harm is extreme: an unnecessary admission costs a night; a missed compartment syndrome costs the limb.

Clinical Pearls
  • The open-fracture antibiotic clock is the highest-yield modifiable variable you control in the ED: IV antibiotics within 1 hour of injury predicts infection better than time to debridement. Document the administration TIME.
  • An open wound does NOT prevent compartment syndrome. Open tibias develop compartment syndrome too — the fascia is not decompressed by a skin laceration.
  • In any casted tibia, escalating pain + rising opioid requirement = remove the cast and examine the limb. Full stop.
  • Distal-third tibial fractures are watershed and heal poorly — counsel accordingly and have a lower threshold for orthopedic involvement even when alignment looks acceptable.

These are the two findings that convert any lower extremity fracture from a splint-and-street problem into a surgical emergency. Both are diagnosed clinically, and both are missed by clinicians waiting for a confirmatory finding that only appears too late.

Gustilo-Anderson Classification

GRADE I: wound <1 cm, clean, minimal soft tissue damage, simple fracture pattern. Usually an inside-out spike.

GRADE II: wound 1–10 cm, moderate soft tissue damage, no extensive stripping.

GRADE IIIA: extensive soft tissue damage (usually >10 cm) but adequate bone coverage remains; includes any high-energy or segmental/comminuted fracture and any farm/highly contaminated injury REGARDLESS of wound size.

GRADE IIIB: extensive soft tissue loss with PERIOSTEAL STRIPPING and bone exposure — requires FLAP coverage. This is where orthoplastic teams matter: a Chinese Level I series found deep infection 8.0% vs 29% and nonunion 11% vs 2.5% comparing orthoplastic vs. orthopedic-alone approaches for IIIB/C tibias.

GRADE IIIC: ANY open fracture with a vascular injury requiring repair — regardless of wound size. A 0.5 cm wound with an ischemic foot is a IIIC, not a Grade I.

The grade is definitively assigned in the OPERATING ROOM, not in the ED — the wound is always worse than it looks. Your ED job is to recognize it is open, start antibiotics, and call.

Open Fracture: The ED Sequence

1. IV ANTIBIOTICS IMMEDIATELY — target within 1 hour of injury. This is the strongest modifiable predictor of infection, more important than the historical 6-hour debridement rule. Typical: cefazolin for I/II; add gram-negative coverage for III; add penicillin for farm/soil contamination (clostridial risk). Follow your institutional protocol.

2. TETANUS status — update as indicated.

3. Remove gross contamination only. Do NOT scrub, irrigate aggressively, or probe in the ED.

4. Photograph ONCE, then apply a sterile saline-moistened dressing and DO NOT repeatedly uncover the wound — each re-exposure increases nosocomial contamination.

5. Splint, reassess neurovascular status, and get the surgeon.

6. Document the antibiotic administration TIME explicitly — it is a quality metric and a medicolegal anchor.

Acute Compartment Syndrome — Diagnose It Early or Not At All

The tibia is the most common cause. The ANTERIOR compartment is most often involved. It occurs in closed AND open fractures.

THE 5 P's ARE A TRAP. Pallor, Pulselessness, and Paralysis are LATE — by the time they appear, muscle and nerve are already necrosing. Compartment pressure never needs to exceed systolic pressure to cause necrosis, which is exactly why PULSES ARE TYPICALLY PRESENT in a limb that is dying.

THE EARLY FINDINGS: (1) PAIN OUT OF PROPORTION with an escalating analgesic requirement, and (2) PAIN ON PASSIVE STRETCH of the compartment's muscles (passive toe extension for the deep posterior compartment; passive toe flexion for the anterior). Paresthesia — classically first web space numbness from deep peroneal involvement — is the first neurologic sign.

MANAGEMENT: remove all circumferential casts/dressings down to skin (this alone can drop pressure substantially). Keep the limb at HEART LEVEL — elevating it lowers arterial inflow and worsens perfusion pressure. Measure pressures if the patient is obtunded, intoxicated, or otherwise unexaminable: ΔP (diastolic − compartment pressure) <30 mmHg → FASCIOTOMY. In an awake, reliable patient, serial clinical examination is the diagnostic standard — do not wait for a number.

Clinical Pearls
  • The obtunded, intubated, or intoxicated patient cannot report pain out of proportion — that population needs compartment pressure monitoring, because your best diagnostic tool has been removed.
  • Regional anesthesia and patient-controlled analgesia can MASK the cardinal early sign. Coordinate with anesthesia and orthopedics before blocking a high-risk tibia.
  • A 'poke hole' over the subcutaneous tibial border is an OPEN FRACTURE until proven otherwise. Treat it as such — antibiotics and a surgeon.

Quick disposition reference for the rest of the lower extremity. The recurring theme: articular involvement, instability, neurovascular risk, and inability to bear weight push toward admission; isolated, stable, non-articular, weight-bearing injuries generally go home with immobilization and follow-up.

Clinical Pearls
  • The Ottawa Ankle and Foot Rules are ~98–100% sensitive for clinically significant fractures and can reduce imaging substantially — but they apply to the ANKLE AND MIDFOOT ONLY, and require palpating the posterior edge/tip of BOTH malleoli, the navicular, and the base of the 5th metatarsal.
  • The two classic misses in the 'simple ankle sprain': the MAISONNEUVE (palpate the proximal fibula, every time) and the LISFRANC (plantar ecchymosis, weight-bearing views).
  • For every lower extremity fracture: document neurovascular status BEFORE and AFTER reduction and splinting, and give explicit compartment syndrome return precautions in writing.
  • Axial-load mechanisms (fall from height onto the feet) demand you examine the ENTIRE axial skeleton — calcaneus, both, plus the lumbar spine. The calcaneal fracture distracts from the burst fracture.

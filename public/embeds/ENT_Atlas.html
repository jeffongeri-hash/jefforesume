Ear

Full anatomical landmarks, otoscopic exam technique and documentation, and the three ear infections that account for nearly every ear-related ED and outpatient visit: acute otitis media, otitis media with effusion, and acute otitis externa — plus the mastoiditis and cholesteatoma red flags you cannot miss.

Anatomy Ear Landmarks — Outer, Middle & Inner Ear

Full lateral cross-section. The eustachian tube is the anatomic link between middle ear and nasopharynx — why a URI so often precedes AOM/OME, and why children (shorter, more horizontal tubes) get more middle ear disease than adults.

Outer ear

Pinna (auricle) landmarks: helix (outer rim), tragus (cartilage anterior to canal — pain on palpation/traction here is the hallmark of otitis externa), and lobule. The external auditory canal is cartilaginous in its outer third and bony in its inner two-thirds — the bony portion is thin-skinned and exquisitely sensitive, which is why deep canal instrumentation hurts disproportionately.

Middle & inner ear

The ossicular chain (malleus → incus → stapes) transmits sound from the TM to the oval window. The facial nerve (CN VII) runs through the temporal bone in close proximity to the middle ear — the anatomic reason cholesteatoma erosion or severe AOM/mastoiditis can produce facial palsy, and why Ramsay Hunt (CN VII + vesicles) is a "same building" diagnosis. The cochlea and semicircular canals sit posteromedial to the middle ear, explaining why inner-ear structures are ordinarily protected from external/middle ear infection unless there's erosion or spread.

Exam Otoscopic Exam & Documentation

Pneumatic otoscopy (with an insufflation bulb) is the single highest-yield addition to a routine otoscopic exam — TM mobility is more sensitive for effusion than color or position alone. Use the largest speculum that comfortably fits the canal to get a good seal for insufflation.

Document four things every time: position, color, translucency, and mobility of the TM, plus the canal itself.

  • Position: neutral / retracted / bulging
  • Color: pearly-gray (normal) / erythematous / amber or yellow / dull white / blue-gray (hemotympanum)
  • Translucency: translucent (normal) / opacified / visible air-fluid level or bubbles
  • Mobility: normal / reduced / absent (pneumatic otoscopy)
  • Canal: patent / edematous / debris / discharge (color, odor) / foreign body

AOM Acute Otitis Media

⚑ Guideline note

AAO-HNSF's own ear-infection guidelines are otitis media with effusion (OME) and tympanostomy tubes. The primary acute otitis media (AOM) diagnostic/treatment guideline is the AAP 2013 clinical practice guideline (endorsed alongside AAFP), which AAO-HNS references and aligns with. Worth knowing which society owns which guideline when you're asked in a chart review or deposition.

Diagnostic criteria (AAP 2013)

Diagnose AOM with any of the following:

  • Moderate-to-severe bulging of the TM, or new-onset otorrhea not due to otitis externa
  • Mild bulging and recent (<48h) onset of ear pain or intense TM erythema

Erythema or pain alone, without bulging, is not sufficient — a red TM from crying, fever, or URI-associated hyperemia is a classic overdiagnosis trap.

💡 Pearl — watchful waiting is guideline-supported

For children ≥6 months with unilateral, non-severe AOM (mild otalgia <48h, temp <39°C) and reliable follow-up, observation with analgesia and a safety-net antibiotic prescription is an AAP-endorsed option — this mirrors the same antibiotic-stewardship logic AAO-HNS uses for adult sinusitis.

First-line therapy

Amoxicillin 80–90 mg/kg/day divided q12h is first-line. Use amoxicillin-clavulanate if amoxicillin in the past 30 days, concurrent purulent conjunctivitis (suggests H. influenzae), or history of recurrent AOM unresponsive to amoxicillin. Penicillin allergy: cefdinir, cefuroxime, or cefpodoxime (cross-reactivity with penicillin is low); azithromycin as a non-cephalosporin fallback.

OME Otitis Media with Effusion

Middle ear fluid without signs of acute infection — TM neutral or retracted (not bulging), dull/amber/gray, reduced mobility, ± air-fluid level or bubbles visible through the TM, and critically: no otalgia, fever, or bulging.

⚠ Common practice gap

AAO-HNS 2016 guideline: do not treat OME with antihistamines, decongestants, or systemic/topical steroids — none improve resolution and all carry side-effect burden. Most OME resolves spontaneously within 3 months. Tympanostomy tubes are reserved for effusion persisting ≥3 months (bilateral) or sooner in an at-risk child (speech/language delay, craniofacial anomaly, Down syndrome, etc.).

AOE Acute Otitis Externa ("Swimmer's Ear")

Diffuse ear canal inflammation: rapid-onset otalgia, itching, or fullness, with tenderness on tragal pressure or auricle traction, and canal edema/erythema ± otorrhea. Canal edema can be severe enough that the TM is not visualizable — document this explicitly rather than guessing at TM status.

⚑ AAO-HNSF 2014 guideline — topical, not oral, is first-line

  • Topical therapy alone for uncomplicated diffuse AOE — not systemic antibiotics
  • Oral antibiotics reserved for extension beyond the ear canal (cellulitis, adenopathy), or in diabetes/immunocompromise where risk of necrotizing (malignant) otitis externa is higher
  • If TM perforation or tympanostomy tube is known/suspected: use a non-ototoxic topical (e.g. ofloxacin or ciprofloxacin-based) rather than aminoglycoside/acidifying drops
  • Address pain proactively — AOE pain is frequently underestimated
  • Reassess at 48–72h if refractory to initial therapy

🚩 Can't miss — necrotizing (malignant) otitis externa

Consider in diabetics or immunocompromised patients with AOE that is disproportionately painful, refractory to topical therapy, or accompanied by granulation tissue at the bony-cartilaginous junction of the canal floor. This is skull-base osteomyelitis (typically Pseudomonas) — needs imaging and IV therapy, not another course of drops.

Pearls Additional Ear Findings & Can't-Miss Diagnoses

Cholesteatoma

Attic retraction pocket (superior/pars flaccida) with keratin debris, ± foul-smelling chronic otorrhea unresponsive to topical therapy. This is a surgical referral, not a "try another antibiotic" finding — erosive and can cause conductive hearing loss, facial nerve injury, or intracranial spread.

Mastoiditis

Postauricular erythema, swelling, and tenderness with the auricle displaced anteriorly/inferiorly — usually in the setting of untreated or undertreated AOM. This is a same-day imaging and IV-antibiotic diagnosis, not an outpatient one.

Hemotympanum

Blue-gray or dark TM after trauma — think temporal bone fracture. Pair with a exam for CSF otorrhea, hearing loss, facial nerve function, and Battle sign / raccoon eyes.

Bullous myringitis

Hemorrhagic or clear bullae on the TM surface itself, intensely painful. Historically linked to Mycoplasma, though evidence for a distinct etiology is mixed — treat the pain and manage as AOM if effusion/bulging present.

Nose & Sinus

Full sagittal and coronal anatomical landmarks, rhinoscopy exam technique and documentation, the AAO-HNSF 2025 Adult Sinusitis antibiotic-stewardship framework, and the distinguishing factors — bilateral vs. unilateral pain, discharge character — that separate routine viral rhinosinusitis from something that needs a different workup entirely.

Anatomy Nasal Cavity Landmarks — Sagittal View

Sagittal section, lateral wall. All three turbinates and their meatuses stack vertically; the middle meatus (below the middle turbinate) is where the ostiomeatal complex drains.

Septum & external landmarks

Anteriorly cartilaginous (quadrangular cartilage), posteriorly bony (perpendicular plate of ethmoid above, vomer below) — the junction is a common site of both deviation and perforation. Kiesselbach's plexus, the most common anterior epistaxis source, sits on the anteroinferior septum.

Nasopharynx

The adenoid (pharyngeal tonsil) sits on the nasopharyngeal roof — prominent in children, involutes with age, and is a common cause of pediatric nasal obstruction/mouth-breathing when hypertrophied. The eustachian tube opening (torus tubarius) sits on the lateral nasopharyngeal wall — the direct anatomic link explaining why nasal/sinus congestion so often travels with ear symptoms.

Anatomy Sinus Anatomy & Drainage

Coronal schematic. The ostiomeatal complex (middle meatus) is the final common drainage pathway for the frontal, maxillary, and anterior ethmoid sinuses — obstruction here is the final common pathway of most rhinosinusitis, regardless of which sinus started it.

Why the ethmoid matters most in the ED: the ethmoid sinuses are separated from the orbit only by the paper-thin lamina papyracea. Ethmoid sinusitis is the most common source of pediatric orbital and preseptal cellulitis — see the Ocular-ENT Overlap domain.

Exam Rhinoscopy Exam & Documentation

Anterior rhinoscopy: nasal speculum (or the largest otoscope speculum) with a headlamp or otoscope light, tilting the head back slightly. Document:

  • Septum: midline vs. deviated, perforation, active bleeding point (classically Kiesselbach's plexus, anteroinferior septum)
  • Turbinates: size (normal/hypertrophied), color/character — pale, boggy, edematous turbinates point toward allergic rhinitis; erythematous, engorged turbinates point toward infectious/viral rhinitis
  • Discharge: location (anterior vs. posterior/post-nasal), character (clear/mucoid/purulent), and laterality (unilateral vs. bilateral) — see below, this is the single most useful discriminator
  • Mucosa: polyps (pale, mobile, insensate grape-like masses — distinct from turbinates, which are tender and fixed), masses, crusting

AAO-HNSF 2025 Antibiotic Stewardship for Sinusitis

⚑ The core diagnostic question: is this even bacterial?

Most acute rhinosinusitis is viral and self-limited. AAO-HNSF's updated Adult Sinusitis Clinical Practice Guideline (published August 2025, superseding the 2015 version) defines acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (ABRS) only when one of these is present:

  • Purulent nasal drainage + nasal obstruction and/or facial pain-pressure-fullness, persisting ≥10 days beyond onset of upper respiratory symptoms without improvement, or
  • Symptoms worsen within 10 days after an initial period of improvement ("double worsening"), or
  • Severe onset: high fever (≥39°C/102.2°F) with purulent discharge or facial pain for at least 3–4 consecutive days at the start of illness

Radiographic imaging is not recommended for patients who meet these clinical criteria unless a complication or alternative diagnosis is suspected.

First-line antibiotic (when treatment is chosen)

Amoxicillin, with or without clavulanate, for 5–7 days in most adults. Add clavulanate for recent antibiotic exposure, daycare-attending children, recent hospitalization, or regions/patients with higher resistant-pneumococcus prevalence. Penicillin-allergic: doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin/moxifloxacin) as alternatives — reserve fluoroquinolones per antibiotic stewardship given tendon/CNS/QT risk profile.

💡 Pearl — safety numbers worth knowing

Sinusitis affects roughly 1 in 8 US adults yearly (30+ million diagnoses), costs over $11 billion/year to manage, and accounts for more than 1 in 5 antibiotic prescriptions in adults — the fifth most common antibiotic-driving diagnosis in the country. That's the scale of the overprescribing problem this guideline is built to address, and it's a useful number to have on hand for a shared-decision-making conversation with a patient expecting a prescription.

Pearl Bilateral vs. Unilateral Pain — A Key Distinguisher

Bilateral facial pain/pressure with bilateral congestion is the expected pattern for routine viral URI, allergic rhinitis, and most typical ABRS (which is often still bilateral, just meeting the bacterial criteria above).

Unilateral, focal, or asymmetric sinus pain — especially with a unilateral predominance of purulent discharge — should broaden your differential rather than confirm "worse sinusitis." Consider:

Odontogenic sinusitis

Unilateral maxillary pain/pressure with foul odor, worse with chewing/percussion of upper molars. Ask about recent dental work, ask the patient to point to the tooth. CT or dental referral, not another course of amoxicillin.

Invasive fungal sinusitis

Emergency in DKA, neutropenia, or other immunocompromise: unilateral facial pain, black/necrotic turbinate or palate eschar, cranial neuropathy. This is mucormycosis until proven otherwise — surgical and ID emergency, do not delay for outpatient follow-up.

Foreign body (especially pediatric)

Unilateral foul-smelling purulent discharge in a child is a foreign body until proven otherwise — button battery is a same-day removal emergency given rapid mucosal necrosis.

Unilateral mass/polyp/tumor

A unilateral polyp (as opposed to the bilateral polyps typical of CRSwNP) warrants imaging to exclude an inverted papilloma or malignancy before assuming "just sinusitis."

Pearl Discharge Character — What It Does and Doesn't Tell You

⚠ The single most corrected misconception in this atlas

Colored (yellow/green) nasal discharge does not, by itself, indicate bacterial infection. Color comes from neutrophil myeloperoxidase, which shows up in viral URIs too as the immune response ramps up — most people with colored discharge on day 3-4 of a cold have a virus, not a bacterial infection. What actually distinguishes ABRS is the time course (≥10 days, no improvement) and pattern (double worsening, or severe onset) — not the color you see on the tissue.

Clear, thin discharge

Typical of early viral URI or allergic rhinitis. If unilateral and watery after head trauma — think CSF rhinorrhea (halo/ring sign on gauze, send for beta-2 transferrin) before calling it allergies.

Thick, colored discharge

Common in both viral and bacterial disease. Only diagnostic for ABRS in combination with the 10-day/double-worsening/severe-onset criteria above — never in isolation.

Throat / Oropharynx

Full pharynx and larynx anatomical landmarks, oropharyngeal exam documentation, viral-vs-strep distinguishing factors, and the three can't-miss deep-space and airway emergencies that hide behind "just a sore throat."

Anatomy Pharynx & Larynx Landmarks — Sagittal View

Sagittal section showing all three pharyngeal subdivisions plus the larynx. This is the diagram that explains the epiglottitis pearl below: the epiglottis and vocal cords sit well below and posterior to what a tongue depressor and light show you in a routine oropharyngeal exam.

Three pharyngeal subdivisions

  • Nasopharynx: posterior to the nasal cavity, above the soft palate — adenoid, eustachian tube openings
  • Oropharynx: soft palate to hyoid bone — tonsils, tongue base, what a routine tongue-depressor exam actually shows
  • Hypopharynx (laryngopharynx): hyoid to cricoid cartilage — piriform sinuses (classic foreign-body/fish-bone lodging point), not visualized without laryngoscopy

Larynx

The epiglottis guards the airway inlet, with the vallecula (tongue base–epiglottis space) anterior to it. True vocal cords sit below, flanked by the arytenoid cartilages posteriorly. None of this is visible on a standard oropharyngeal exam — direct or video laryngoscopy is required, which is exactly why a "normal-looking throat" cannot exclude epiglottitis.

Exam Oropharynx Exam & Documentation

Tongue depressor technique: press firmly on the mid-posterior tongue (not the tip) to elicit a gag-free view of the oropharynx; have the patient say "ahh" to elevate the soft palate. Document:

  • Tonsils: size (grade 1 = within pillars, to grade 4 = touching midline), color, presence and character of exudate
  • Uvula: midline vs. deviated (and which direction) — deviation is a peritonsillar space finding, not a tonsillitis finding
  • Soft palate: symmetric bulge vs. asymmetric bulge, petechiae (classic for strep or mono, not specific to either)
  • Posterior pharyngeal wall: erythema, cobblestoning (lymphoid hyperplasia from chronic post-nasal drip), exudate/discharge tracking down from the nasopharynx
Normal oropharynx landmarks via tongue depressor exam.

🚩 The exam finding that isn't there — epiglottitis

The single most important pearl in this domain: the standard oropharyngeal exam is often completely normal in epiglottitis, because the epiglottis sits below what a tongue depressor view shows you. A patient with a severe sore throat, muffled voice, and drooling but a "benign-looking" throat on your exam is not reassuring — it's a red flag. See below.

Weak documentation Throat: red, tonsils swollen. Rapid strep negative. Viral, supportive care.
Bulletproof documentation Oropharynx: tonsils 2+ bilaterally, no exudate, uvula midline, no palatal petechiae, no trismus, voice normal, no drooling. Centor criteria 1/4 (fever only; no exudate, no tender anterior cervical adenopathy, cough present). RADT negative. Low pretest probability for GAS pharyngitis; supportive care and return precautions for worsening pain, trismus, drooling, or voice change discussed and documented.

Pharyngitis Viral vs. Strep

Centor / McIsaac criteria

One point each for: fever >38°C, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical adenopathy, absence of cough. McIsaac modification adds age (+1 if 3–14y, -1 if ≥45y). Higher score raises pretest probability of group A strep (GAS) but does not replace RADT/culture for diagnosis.

⚠ Common misconception

Exudate is neither required for strep nor specific to it. EBV (mono) and adenovirus both commonly produce exudative tonsillitis that looks identical to strep on inspection alone — this is exactly why Centor/McIsaac scoring plus RADT/culture matters more than the gestalt "looks strep-y" impression.

💡 Pearl — mono lookalike

Exudative tonsillitis + posterior cervical or diffuse lymphadenopathy + fatigue ± splenomegaly in an adolescent/young adult should raise infectious mononucleosis on your differential. Avoid amoxicillin empirically in this population when mono is a real possibility — the amoxicillin/EBV rash is classic and avoidable.

Can't miss Peritonsillar Abscess

Progression from tonsillitis → cellulitis → abscess. Classic tetrad: severe unilateral sore throat, muffled "hot potato" voice, trismus, and uvular deviation away from the affected side with a bulging soft palate/anterior tonsillar pillar on the affected side. Often with ipsilateral otalgia and tender cervical adenopathy.

Distinguishing from peritonsillar cellulitis

Cellulitis causes diffuse swelling without a discrete, fluctuant bulge or marked uvular deviation. Abscess causes a more discrete, asymmetric bulge — POCUS (intraoral probe) can confirm a drainable fluid collection before you commit to needle aspiration.

Management framework

Airway assessment first, then needle aspiration or I&D, antibiotics covering GAS and oral anaerobes, and analgesia/hydration. Most patients are managed as outpatients after drainage; refer to ENT for recurrent or complicated cases.

Can't miss Epiglottitis & Deep Neck Space Infections

🚩 Epiglottitis

Acute dysphagia, odynophagia, drooling, muffled voice, high fever, and anxious/tripod positioning — progressing to stridor if untreated. The oropharyngeal exam is frequently normal since the epiglottis isn't visualized without laryngoscopy. Avoid agitating the patient or instrumenting the airway/throat outside a controlled setting (OR/resuscitation bay with airway backup) if this is on your differential — direct visualization should happen with anesthesia/ENT present, not with a tongue depressor in a busy exam room.

🚩 Retropharyngeal abscess

More common in young children: neck stiffness/torticollis, refusal to extend the neck, muffled voice, worsening over several days, and inability to swallow secretions. Lateral neck imaging or CT neck with contrast; this is an airway and surgical-consult emergency, not an outpatient antibiotic trial.

Ocular-ENT Overlap

Where sinus and ear infections spill into the orbit. The exam findings that separate a same-day-outpatient problem from a sight- and life-threatening one.

Can't miss Preseptal vs. Orbital Cellulitis

🚩 This distinction is the whole game

Preseptal (periorbital) cellulitis is anterior to the orbital septum and generally an outpatient problem. Orbital (postseptal) cellulitis is posterior to the septum, involves the orbital contents themselves, and is a true emergency — risk of vision loss, cavernous sinus thrombosis, and intracranial spread. A recent or concurrent sinusitis (especially ethmoid) is the classic antecedent for orbital cellulitis, via spread through the lamina papyracea.

💡 Pearl — the bedside exam that separates them

Ask the patient to look in all directions and ask specifically about pain with eye movement, then check visual acuity and pupillary response (RAPD). If eye findings are otherwise entirely normal aside from eyelid swelling, preseptal cellulitis is likely. Any proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, decreased vision, or RAPD changes the diagnosis — and the disposition — immediately.

Weak documentation Eye swollen and red, looks infected. Started antibiotics, f/u with PCP.
Bulletproof documentation L periorbital edema/erythema, no proptosis, EOM full and painless in all directions, visual acuity 20/20 OU, pupils equal/round/reactive, no RAPD, no chemosis. Afebrile. No antecedent sinus symptoms. Findings consistent with preseptal cellulitis; orbital involvement specifically excluded on exam. Oral antibiotics with 24h recheck arranged; explicit return precautions for vision change, eye pain with movement, or new swelling given verbally and in writing.

Overlap Dacryocystitis

Infection of the lacrimal sac: swelling, erythema, and tenderness centered over the medial canthus (distinguishing it from a more diffuse preseptal process), with epiphora (excess tearing) and often purulent discharge expressible from the punctum with gentle sac compression. Usually from nasolacrimal duct obstruction; can extend to preseptal or, less commonly, orbital cellulitis if untreated.

Complications Intracranial & Vascular Spread

Orbital cellulitis and sinusitis share a final common complication pathway worth keeping in mind: subperiosteal abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis (bilateral eye findings, cranial nerve III/IV/VI palsies, rapidly progressive), meningitis, and epidural/subdural/brain abscess. Any orbital cellulitis patient with worsening exam despite therapy, bilateral findings, or new neurologic signs needs escalation and imaging, not a medication change.

Distinguishing features are the highest-yield single clue, not the complete diagnostic picture — always confirm against current literature and your own clinical judgment before applying.

Doctrine The Bulletproof Pattern, Applied to ENT

The same core doctrine applies here as elsewhere in this reference: avoid conclusory verbs like "ruled out" or "no infection" when the exam finding you're citing cannot actually exclude the diagnosis. The defensible pattern: name the diagnosis you're considering, rest on objective exam findings, cite a validated tool or guideline criterion where one exists, use probabilistic language, and close with an explicit safety net. Every documentation example in this atlas follows that structure — scroll back through Ear, Nose/Sinus, Throat, and Ocular for worked examples.

⚠ ENT-specific version of the CXR-can't-exclude-dissection trap

"Oropharynx clear, no epiglottitis" is a conclusory statement your standard exam cannot actually support — you didn't visualize the epiglottis. If epiglottitis is genuinely on your differential, document the actual findings (voice quality, drooling, positioning, willingness to lie flat) and your reasoning, rather than a diagnosis-by-negation you didn't have the view to make.

Myths Common ENT Misconceptions — Public & Clinician-Facing

“Green or yellow nasal discharge means it's bacterial and needs an antibiotic.”
What's actually true: Discharge color reflects neutrophil myeloperoxidase activity, not the organism. Viral URIs routinely turn colored by day 3-4 as the immune response ramps up.
To a patient: “Color tells us your immune system is working, not what's causing the infection. What matters is how long it's lasted and whether it's getting better or worse.” To a colleague: lead with the AAO-HNSF ABRS criteria (≥10 days no improvement, double worsening, or severe onset) rather than debating discharge color.
“Any ear infection needs antibiotics right away.”
What's actually true: OME (fluid without acute signs) does not benefit from antibiotics, antihistamines, decongestants, or steroids per AAO-HNSF. Even AOM has an AAP-endorsed watchful-waiting pathway for non-severe, unilateral cases with reliable follow-up.
The distinguishing exam finding is bulging — OME is retracted or neutral, not bulging, and has no acute otalgia or fever. That's the difference between an antibiotic conversation and a reassurance conversation.
“Strep throat always has white patches; if there's no exudate, it's viral.”
What's actually true: Exudate is neither required for strep pharyngitis nor specific to it — EBV and adenovirus commonly produce exudative tonsillitis indistinguishable from strep on inspection alone.
This is exactly why Centor/McIsaac scoring plus RADT/culture exists — gestalt “looks strep-y” assessment is not reliable enough on its own, in either direction.
“Swimmer's ear needs an oral antibiotic.”
What's actually true: AAO-HNSF's guideline is explicit: topical therapy alone is first-line for uncomplicated diffuse otitis externa. Oral antibiotics are reserved for infection extending beyond the canal or specific risk factors (diabetes, immunocompromise).
Oral antibiotics for routine AOE add systemic side-effect risk without added benefit over topical drops for the localized canal infection most patients actually have.
“If sinus symptoms last more than a week, that means it's bacterial.”
What's actually true: The guideline threshold is ≥10 days without improvement, or a double-worsening pattern, or severe onset — not a flat one-week cutoff. And even once ABRS is diagnosed, watchful waiting is the preferred initial approach for most uncomplicated cases per the 2025 update.
“Bacterial” and “needs antibiotics right now” are two different questions — the guideline separates them deliberately.
“A normal-looking throat exam rules out epiglottitis.”
What's actually true: The oropharyngeal exam frequently looks entirely normal in epiglottitis, because the epiglottis sits below what a tongue depressor view shows. Muffled voice, drooling, and tripod positioning with a benign-looking oropharynx is a red flag, not reassurance.
This is the single most dangerous documentation trap in this atlas — charting “oropharynx clear, no exudate, reassuring” in a patient with drooling and stridor risk is itself a liability if the diagnosis is missed.
“You need a CT scan to diagnose a sinus infection.”
What's actually true: Acute rhinosinusitis meeting clinical diagnostic criteria should NOT be imaged per AAO-HNSF, unless a complication (orbital/intracranial extension) or an alternative diagnosis is suspected. Imaging is reserved for those specific scenarios, and for objectively confirming chronic rhinosinusitis.
Over-imaging exposes patients to radiation and cost without changing management in uncomplicated cases — the clinical criteria are the diagnostic test here.
“Unilateral sinus pain is just a worse version of regular sinusitis.”
What's actually true: Unilateral predominance of pain or discharge should broaden, not narrow, your differential: odontogenic source, invasive or allergic fungal sinusitis, foreign body (pediatric), or a unilateral mass/polyp.
Bilateral symmetric pain fits the routine viral/bacterial rhinosinusitis pattern; asymmetry is the signal that something else may be going on.

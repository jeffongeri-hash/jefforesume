CBC

Complete Blood Count (with differential)

Marrow output, oxygen carriage, and the inflammatory census in one draw.

Reference See individual components below (WBC, Hgb/Hct, Plt, indices, diff).

▲ Why it rises

  • WBC ↑ (leukocytosis). Demargination/release from marrow: infection, inflammation, steroids, catecholamine stress (seizure, exercise), tissue necrosis, myeloproliferative disease. Left shift (bands) implies bacterial demand-driven granulopoiesis.
  • Hgb/Hct ↑ (erythrocytosis). True: polycythemia vera (low EPO), or secondary from chronic hypoxia/OSA/high altitude/EPO-secreting tumor (high EPO). Relative: plasma-volume contraction (dehydration, diuretics) — the RBC mass is normal, the denominator shrank.
  • Platelets ↑ (thrombocytosis). Reactive (90%): iron deficiency, infection, inflammation, post-splenectomy, malignancy, bleeding. Clonal: essential thrombocythemia, other MPN.

▼ Why it falls

  • WBC ↓ (leukopenia/neutropenia). Decreased production (marrow suppression: chemo, B12/folate, infiltration), increased destruction/margination (sepsis — an ominous sign, viral, autoimmune), sequestration (hypersplenism). ANC <500 = severe neutropenia = emergency.
  • Hgb/Hct ↓ (anemia). Categorize by MCV: microcytic (iron def, thalassemia, chronic disease), normocytic (acute blood loss, hemolysis, marrow, early chronic disease), macrocytic (B12/folate, alcohol, hypothyroid, MDS, reticulocytosis).
  • Platelets ↓ (thrombocytopenia). Production (marrow), destruction (ITP, TTP/HUS, DIC, HIT, drug), sequestration (splenomegaly), dilution (massive transfusion). <10–20k = spontaneous bleeding risk.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Leukemoid reaction (WBC >50k, mature cells) vs CML — check LAP score / molecular. Blasts on smear = call heme now.
  • Pseudothrombocytopenia: EDTA-induced clumping. Repeat in citrate tube before treating a lonely low platelet count in a well patient.
  • Anemia + thrombocytopenia + schistocytes → think MAHA (TTP/HUS, DIC). LDH, haptoglobin, retic, coags, smear immediately.
  • Nucleated RBCs / teardrop cells → marrow stress or infiltration; in a sick adult, a poor-prognosis sign.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Neonates/infants. Physiologic polycythemia at birth (Hct up to ~60%), then a physiologic nadir of Hgb ~9–11 g/dL around 2 months. Neonatal WBC runs high (up to ~30k day 1). Don't over-call.
  • Pediatrics. Lymphocyte predominance is normal until ~4–5 yrs (the 'crossover'), opposite the adult neutrophil-predominant differential.
  • Pregnancy. Dilutional anemia (plasma volume expands more than red cell mass); mild leukocytosis is physiologic and can reach 15k, intrapartum higher — do not reflexively treat.
  • Older adults. New anemia is never 'just age.' Always evaluate — occult GI loss and MDS rise with age. A left shift may be blunted; sepsis can present with a normal or low WBC.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Corrected WBC for nucleated RBCs. Automated counters miscount nRBCs as WBCs. Corrected WBC = uncorrected WBC × 100 / (100 + nRBC per 100 WBC).
  • Absolute neutrophil count (ANC). ANC = WBC(×10³) × (%neutrophils + %bands) / 100. Drives febrile-neutropenia decisions (<500, or <1000 and falling).
  • Reticulocyte production index. RPI = retic% × (Hct/45) / maturation factor. Separates hypoproliferative from appropriate marrow response in anemia.

ED lens

First-pass triage lab. Left shift, bandemia, or neutropenia reframes an undifferentiated febrile patient. Baseline Hgb anchors transfusion thresholds in bleeding.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

A downtrending Hgb near end of life is often expected and not transfused unless symptomatic (dyspnea, angina). Serial CBCs rarely change a comfort-focused plan — order only if the result would change management.

BMP/CMP

Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻ / CO₂ content)

The metabolic half of acid-base, read together with the anion gap.

Reference 22–28 mmol/L

▲ Why it rises

  • Metabolic alkalosis. Chloride-responsive (vomiting/NG loss, diuretics, contraction — urine Cl <20) or chloride-resistant (hyperaldosteronism, severe hypokalemia, Cushing — urine Cl >20). Also compensatory for chronic respiratory acidosis (COPD).

▼ Why it falls

  • Metabolic acidosis. Split by anion gap. High-gap (MUDPILES/GOLDMARK: lactate, ketones, uremia, toxic alcohols, salicylate). Normal-gap/hyperchloremic (GI bicarb loss — diarrhea; renal — RTA; saline dilution).

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Always compute the anion gap when bicarb is low. A normal bicarb does not exclude a mixed disorder (a coexisting gap acidosis + metabolic alkalosis can leave HCO₃ deceptively normal — the gap reveals it).
  • Delta-delta: compare rise in anion gap to fall in bicarb to detect a superimposed non-gap acidosis or metabolic alkalosis.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Pediatrics. Lower baseline bicarb than adults (as low as ~18–22 in young children).
  • Chronic respiratory acidosis. Expected renal compensation raises bicarb ~3.5 per 10 mmHg chronic PaCO₂ rise — a 'high' bicarb in a COPD patient may be appropriate compensation, not primary alkalosis.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Anion gap (see Calculators). AG = Na − (Cl + HCO₃). Normal ~8–12.
  • Albumin-corrected gap. Add ~2.5 to the gap for every 1 g/dL albumin below 4. Hypoalbuminemia hides a gap acidosis — critical in the malnourished/critically ill.

ED lens

The pivot point for the acid-base workup. Low bicarb → compute gap → build the differential → treat the cause.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Reflects the acid-base consequences of the dying process (e.g., lactic acidosis of hypoperfusion). Rarely acted upon; comfort-directed care instead.

BMP/CMP

Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN)

A volume and protein-catabolism signal as much as a renal one.

Reference 7–20 mg/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated BUN. Prerenal (volume depletion, low cardiac output — ADH-driven urea reabsorption), renal (GFR loss), postrenal (obstruction). Non-renal drivers: GI bleed (protein load), high-protein diet, catabolism, steroids, tetracyclines.

▼ Why it falls

  • Low BUN. Low protein intake/malnutrition, advanced liver disease (failure of urea synthesis), volume overload/dilution (SIADH), pregnancy.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Low<77–20>20 elevatedmg/dL

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • BUN:Cr ratio >20:1 suggests prerenal azotemia or GI bleed; ~10–15:1 suggests intrinsic renal.
  • An isolated BUN rise with normal Cr in an upper-GI-bleed presentation is a supportive clue (digested-blood protein load).

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Older adults. Lower muscle/protein mass and lower baseline GFR make interpretation reliant on the paired creatinine and trend.
  • Cirrhosis. Low BUN can falsely reassure — the failing liver undersynthesizes urea despite renal impairment (hepatorenal risk).

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Interpret with creatinine. BUN alone is nonspecific; the BUN:Cr ratio and trend carry the signal.

ED lens

Best read as part of the prerenal-vs-intrinsic story alongside creatinine, urine studies, and volume exam.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Rising BUN is common in the volume-contracted dying patient; not corrected unless reversal serves comfort.

BMP/CMP

Creatinine (Cr)

A GFR estimate distorted by muscle mass — context is everything.

Reference M 0.7–1.3 mg/dL · F 0.6–1.1 mg/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated creatinine. Reduced GFR: prerenal, intrinsic (ATN, GN, interstitial), or postrenal. Also increased production (rhabdomyolysis) and secretion blockers (cimetidine, trimethoprim raise Cr without a true GFR change).

▼ Why it falls

  • Low creatinine. Low muscle mass — the key teaching point. Cachexia, advanced age, amputees, prolonged ICU stay, malnutrition, some liver disease. A 'normal' Cr in a frail elder may hide substantial renal impairment.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Below range (low muscle mass — see modifiers)<0.60.6–1.3>1.3 elevatedmg/dL
>4.0 markedly elevatedmg/dL

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Rhabdomyolysis raises Cr disproportionately (muscle breakdown releases creatinine precursor); the Cr may overstate true GFR loss early.
  • Creatinine lags GFR. In evolving AKI, the Cr is a rear-view mirror — a 'normal' value hours into an insult can be falsely reassuring.
  • Drug clearance: dose renally-cleared and QT-prolonging drugs off an accurate GFR, not the raw Cr.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Low-muscle-mass patients (your teaching point). In the elderly, cachectic, cirrhotic, or amputee patient, creatinine is generated at a lower rate, so a 'normal-looking' Cr can correspond to a substantially reduced GFR. Estimate GFR (CKD-EPI) and consider cystatin C when muscle mass is atypical, and reduce reliance on Cr for drug dosing.
  • Pediatrics. Age/size-dependent norms; use a pediatric estimating equation (e.g., bedside CKiD) — adult cutoffs will misclassify.
  • Pregnancy. GFR rises; a 'normal' adult Cr (e.g., 1.0) may be abnormally high in pregnancy.
  • Sex/race. Men run higher than women (muscle mass). Race coefficients have been removed from current CKD-EPI equations.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Estimated GFR. Use CKD-EPI 2021 (race-free). Cockcroft–Gault (uses weight) still appears in drug-dosing references — see Calculators.
  • Cystatin C. Muscle-independent filtration marker; preferred when creatinine is unreliable (cachexia, extremes of muscle mass).

ED lens

Anchor of the AKI workup and renal drug dosing. Always pair with baseline (trend > snapshot) and muscle-mass context.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Rising creatinine is expected in the actively dying and is not itself treated. Deprescribe renally-cleared agents to the estimated GFR to avoid accumulation and toxicity.

BMP/CMP

Glucose

Hypoglycemia kills faster than hyperglycemia — check it first.

Reference Fasting 70–99 mg/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Hyperglycemia. Diabetes, stress/counter-regulatory surge (illness, steroids, catecholamines), DKA/HHS. Marked hyperglycemia drives osmotic diuresis and factitious hyponatremia.

▼ Why it falls

  • Hypoglycemia. Insulin/sulfonylurea excess, sepsis, adrenal insufficiency, liver failure, alcohol (impaired gluconeogenesis), insulinoma, malnutrition. In any altered patient, glucose is a reflex check.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Severe hypo<4070–99>250 hyperglycemicmg/dL
Hypo40–69>600 HHS rangemg/dL

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • DKA typically glucose >250 with gap acidosis + ketones; euglycemic DKA (SGLT2 inhibitors, pregnancy, starvation) has near-normal glucose — don't be falsely reassured.
  • HHS: glucose often >600, minimal ketosis, profound hyperosmolarity, older type-2 patients.
  • Sulfonylurea/long-acting-insulin hypoglycemia recurs — admit and consider octreotide for sulfonylureas.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Neonates. Hypoglycemia threshold and consequences differ; neonatal hypoglycemia is a distinct emergency with its own cutoffs.
  • Elderly on agents. Hypoglycemia unawareness and renal drug accumulation raise risk; sulfonylureas are high-risk in this group.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Corrected sodium. See Sodium — correct Na up for hyperglycemia.
  • Effective osmolality. 2×Na + glucose/18 (omit BUN for 'effective' osm). Drives HHS assessment.

ED lens

First reflex in altered mental status, seizure, or focal deficit (hypoglycemia mimics stroke). Point-of-care first, confirm on serum.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Tight glycemic control is abandoned near end of life; the aim is to avoid symptomatic hypo/hyperglycemia. Deprescribe sulfonylureas and simplify insulin.

BMP/CMP

Potassium (K⁺)

The number you never ignore — narrow window, lethal tails.

Reference 3.5–5.0 mmol/L

▲ Why it rises

  • Hyperkalemia. Decreased excretion (AKI/CKD, K-sparing diuretics, ACEi/ARB, aldosterone deficiency), transcellular shift out of cells (acidosis, insulin deficiency, cell lysis — rhabdo, tumor lysis, hemolysis), or increased intake in the setting of impaired excretion.

▼ Why it falls

  • Hypokalemia. GI loss (vomiting, diarrhea), renal loss (diuretics, hyperaldosteronism, hypomagnesemia driving renal K wasting), transcellular shift in (insulin, beta-agonists, alkalosis, refeeding).

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Severe low<2.53.5–5.0>6.5 severe highmmol/L
Moderate low2.5–2.96.0–6.5mmol/L
Mild low3.0–3.45.1–5.9mmol/L

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • ECG is the vital sign for K⁺: peaked T waves → PR/QRS widening → sine wave → arrest. Treat the ECG, not just the number.
  • Hypomagnesemia makes hypokalemia refractory — you cannot repvlete K without repleting Mg.
  • Pseudohyperkalemia: hemolyzed specimen, fist-clenching during draw, marked thrombocytosis/leukocytosis. Confirm before treating a well patient.
  • Order in hyperkalemia treatment: stabilize membrane (calcium) → shift (insulin/glucose, albuterol, bicarb if acidotic) → remove (dialysis, diuresis, GI binders).

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Neonates. Higher normal upper limit (up to ~6.0) and specimens hemolyze easily — heel-stick hemolysis is a classic false hyperkalemia.
  • CKD/dialysis. Chronic tolerance to higher levels, but still at risk. Missed dialysis + dietary indiscretion is a recurring ED presentation.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • No formal correction. But interpret alongside pH: acidemia shifts K out (0.1 pH drop ≈ ~0.6 mmol/L rise, highly variable). A 'normal' K in DKA masks a large total-body deficit that unmasks with insulin.

ED lens

The single most time-critical electrolyte. Any peaked-T or wide-QRS in a dialysis/AKI patient = empiric calcium before the level returns.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Generally not corrected unless causing symptomatic arrhythmia distressing the patient. Deprescribe QT/K-affecting agents as goals shift.

BMP/CMP

Sodium (Na⁺)

A water problem masquerading as a salt number.

Reference 135–145 mmol/L

▲ Why it rises

  • Hypernatremia. Almost always free-water deficit relative to sodium: impaired thirst/access to water (elderly, intubated, infants), renal losses (osmotic diuresis, DI), GI/insensible losses. Rarely salt gain (hypertonic saline, salt ingestion).

▼ Why it falls

  • Hyponatremia. Assess volume + serum/urine osms + urine Na. Hypovolemic (GI/renal loss), euvolemic (SIADH, hypothyroid, adrenal, psychogenic polydipsia, beer potomania), hypervolemic (CHF, cirrhosis, nephrosis). It's a water-handling defect, not usually a sodium-deficiency.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Severe low<120135–145>160 severe highmmol/L
Moderate low120–129155–160mmol/L
Mild low130–134146–154mmol/L

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Correction rate discipline: raise Na no faster than ~8 mmol/L per 24h in chronic hyponatremia to avoid osmotic demyelination (central pontine myelinolysis).
  • Acute symptomatic hyponatremia (seizure, obtundation): 3% saline bolus is appropriate — the risk calculus flips.
  • Pseudohyponatremia: severe hyperlipidemia/hyperproteinemia (older assays). True measured osm is normal.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Infants/elderly. Highest hypernatremia risk — cannot independently access water. Elderly also have blunted thirst and concentrating ability.
  • Beer potomania / tea-and-toast. Low solute intake limits free-water excretion → dilutional hyponatremia at surprisingly modest fluid intake.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Glucose-corrected sodium. Add ~1.6 mmol/L (some use 2.4) to measured Na for every 100 mg/dL glucose above 100. Hyperglycemia pulls water intravascularly and dilutes Na — the 'true' Na is higher than measured.

ED lens

Sodium interpretation is inseparable from volume status and rate-of-change. The number without the timeline is a trap.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Asymptomatic derangements at end of life generally not corrected. Focus on symptomatic relief (e.g., delirium from acute change) rather than the number.

Calcium (total) + Ionized

Report a total calcium only after you've accounted for albumin.

Reference Total 8.5–10.5 mg/dL · Ionized 1.10–1.30 mmol/L

▲ Why it rises

  • Hypercalcemia. 'CHIMPANZEES' — most commonly primary hyperparathyroidism (outpatient) and malignancy (inpatient: PTHrP, bony mets, myeloma). Also thiazides, vitamin D toxicity, granulomatous disease, milk-alkali, immobilization.

▼ Why it falls

  • Hypocalcemia. Hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy), vitamin D deficiency, CKD, hyperphosphatemia (tumor lysis), pancreatitis, hypomagnesemia, massive transfusion (citrate chelation), rhabdo (early).

Low7.0–8.410.6–13.9 highmg/dL

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Malignant hypercalcemia: aggressive IV isotonic fluids first, then calcitonin (fast, tachyphylaxis) + bisphosphonate/denosumab (slow, durable).
  • Symptomatic hypocalcemia (tetany, Chvostek/Trousseau, QT prolongation, seizure) → IV calcium; check and replace magnesium.
  • Citrate toxicity in massive transfusion → ionized hypocalcemia; monitor ionized Ca, not total.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Critically ill / transfusion. Use ionized calcium — total calcium is unreliable when albumin and pH are shifting.
  • CKD. Chronic mineral-bone disease alters Ca/PO₄/PTH interplay; interpret as a system.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Albumin-corrected calcium. Corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin g/dL). Hypoalbuminemia lowers total calcium without lowering the physiologically active ionized fraction — correcting prevents over-treating a pseudo-hypocalcemia. When in doubt, measure ionized Ca.

ED lens

Always correct for albumin (or get ionized). QT effects make calcium an arrhythmia concern at both extremes.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Hypercalcemia of malignancy is a common, treatable cause of end-of-life delirium, nausea, and constipation — hydration ± calcitonin can meaningfully improve comfort even in a comfort-focused plan.

CMP

Liver Panel (AST/ALT, Alk Phos, Bilirubin, Albumin)

Pattern recognition: hepatocellular vs cholestatic, and is the liver still working?

Reference AST 10–40 · ALT 7–56 U/L · ALP 44–147 U/L · Total bili 0.1–1.2 mg/dL · Albumin 3.5–5.0 g/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Transaminases (AST/ALT) ↑. Hepatocellular injury. ALT >1000s: ischemic ('shock liver'), acetaminophen, viral, autoimmune. AST:ALT >2:1 with modest elevations suggests alcoholic hepatitis.
  • Alk phos ↑. Cholestatic pattern — biliary obstruction, infiltration, drugs. Confirm hepatic origin with GGT (also rises in bone disease/pregnancy where GGT is normal).
  • Bilirubin ↑. Unconjugated (hemolysis, Gilbert, resorbing hematoma) vs conjugated (obstruction, hepatocellular). Fractionate to interpret.

▼ Why it falls

  • Albumin ↓. Synthetic failure (chronic liver disease), loss (nephrotic, protein-losing enteropathy), or negative acute-phase response (inflammation). Low albumin also masks a calcium and an anion-gap reading.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
ALT normal7–56>1000 massive (ischemic/APAP/viral)U/L
Albumin low<3.53.5–5.0g/dL

Special situations & can't-miss

  • AST/ALT in the thousands + coagulopathy + encephalopathy = acute liver failure — time-critical, consider NAC and transplant referral.
  • Synthetic function is INR + albumin + bilirubin, NOT the transaminases (which measure injury, not function). A normalizing AST/ALT in fulminant failure can mean recovery OR exhaustion of hepatocytes.
  • Isolated indirect hyperbilirubinemia in a well patient → Gilbert, reassure.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Pediatrics. ALP is physiologically high during bone growth — a high ALP in a growing child is usually bone, not biliary.
  • Pregnancy. ALP rises (placental). New RUQ pain + labs → consider HELLP, acute fatty liver of pregnancy.
  • Older adults. Muscle-source AST from injury/rhabdo can confound; correlate with ALT and CK.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • R-factor. (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN). >5 hepatocellular, <2 cholestatic, 2–5 mixed — structures the drug-induced-liver-injury workup.

ED lens

Pattern first (hepatocellular vs cholestatic), then function (INR/albumin/bili), then the time-critical few (shock liver, APAP, obstruction with cholangitis).

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Jaundice and rising bilirubin are common in end-stage disease; management targets pruritus and comfort, not the numbers.

CMP

Total Protein & Albumin/Globulin

Nutrition, synthetic reserve, and a hint of paraproteins — read albumin and the gap separately.

Reference Total protein 6.0–8.3 g/dL · Albumin 3.5–5.0 g/dL · Globulin ~2.0–3.5 g/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated total protein / high globulin gap. Increased globulins: chronic inflammation/infection, autoimmune disease, and — the can't-miss — a monoclonal gammopathy (multiple myeloma, Waldenström). Also hemoconcentration/dehydration (relative).

▼ Why it falls

  • Low albumin. Synthetic failure (chronic liver disease), loss (nephrotic syndrome, protein-losing enteropathy, burns), dilution, and — very commonly — the negative acute-phase response of acute/chronic inflammation and malnutrition. Low albumin is a robust marker of illness severity and poor prognosis.

  • Low albumin distorts other labs: it masks a low total calcium (correct it) and hides an anion-gap acidosis (add ~2.5 to the gap per 1 g/dL albumin below 4).
  • Albumin is a strong prognostic marker across surgery, sepsis, heart failure, and cirrhosis — a low value flags physiologic reserve.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Elderly / chronically ill. Low albumin is common and multifactorial (intake, inflammation, liver) — an important, often-overlooked prognostic signal rather than a simple nutrition readout.

  • Nephrotic syndrome / burns. Large external protein losses drop albumin substantially; expect edema and hypercoagulability (nephrotic).

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Albumin-corrected calcium & anion gap. See Calcium and Bicarbonate — both require albumin correction to avoid misreads.

ED lens

Albumin is the actionable piece: it re-interprets calcium and the anion gap and flags physiologic reserve. A wide globulin gap prompts a myeloma workup.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Low albumin is expected and prognostically consistent with advanced disease; not corrected. Useful only insofar as it informs prognosis discussions.

Electrolytes

Magnesium (Mg²⁺)

The forgotten cation — and the reason your potassium won't correct.

Reference 1.7–2.2 mg/dL (≈0.7–0.9 mmol/L)

▲ Why it rises

  • Hypermagnesemia. Almost always iatrogenic + renal impairment: Mg-containing laxatives/antacids in CKD, or IV Mg (obstetric pre-eclampsia treatment). Progressive loss of DTRs → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest at extreme levels.

▼ Why it falls

  • Hypomagnesemia. GI loss (diarrhea, PPIs — chronic use, malabsorption, alcoholism), renal wasting (loop/thiazide diuretics, aminoglycosides, cisplatin), poor intake, refeeding. Very common and under-recognized in the alcoholic and critically ill.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Low<1.71.7–2.2>2.2 elevatedmg/dL
Severe low<1.0 (symptomatic)>4 loss of DTRs / cardiacmg/dL

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Hypomagnesemia is the reason refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia won't correct — low Mg promotes renal K wasting and impairs PTH. Replace magnesium FIRST or the K and Ca repletion fails.
  • Torsades de pointes: IV magnesium is the treatment regardless of the serum Mg level.
  • Serum Mg reflects only ~1% of total body magnesium (mostly intracellular/bone), so a 'normal' level can coexist with real depletion — replete empirically in the symptomatic or refractory-hypokalemic patient.
  • In pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, Mg is therapeutic; monitor DTRs, respiratory rate, and urine output for iatrogenic toxicity, with calcium gluconate as the antidote.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Alcohol use disorder. Chronically depleted (poor intake, renal wasting, GI loss) — a routine repletion target, especially in withdrawal.
  • CKD/renal failure. The population at risk for HYPERmagnesemia; avoid Mg-containing OTC products.
  • Chronic PPI use. Recognized cause of clinically significant hypomagnesemia.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Check with K and Ca. Interpret and replete as a trio — refractory hypokalemia/hypocalcemia mandates a magnesium check.

ED lens

The cation to check when potassium or calcium won't correct, and the drug for torsades. Serum level underestimates true depletion — treat empirically when the picture fits.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Repleted only for symptomatic relief (e.g., cramps, arrhythmia causing distress). Deprescribe Mg-containing products in renal impairment.

Phosphate (PO₄)

Watch it plummet in refeeding and DKA treatment — and skyrocket in tumor lysis.

Reference 2.5–4.5 mg/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • Hyperphosphatemia. Reduced renal excretion (CKD/AKI — the most common), massive cellular release (tumor lysis syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, hemolysis), acidosis (transcellular shift out), excess intake (phosphate enemas/binders), hypoparathyroidism. Drives hypocalcemia by precipitation.

▼ Why it falls

  • Hypophosphatemia. Transcellular shift INTO cells (refeeding syndrome, insulin therapy in DKA, respiratory alkalosis), renal wasting (hyperparathyroidism, Fanconi, diuretics), decreased intake/absorption (alcoholism, malnutrition, antacid binders). Severe (<1.0) → muscle weakness, respiratory failure, hemolysis, rhabdo, arrhythmia.

  • Refeeding syndrome (your can't-miss): reintroducing carbohydrate in a malnourished/alcoholic/prolonged-NPO patient triggers an insulin surge that drives phosphate (and K, Mg) intracellularly — profound hypophosphatemia can cause respiratory/cardiac failure. Anticipate, monitor, replete, and advance nutrition slowly.
  • DKA treatment: insulin drives phosphate into cells; the initial 'normal/high' phosphate falls during therapy. Routine repletion isn't required unless severe or symptomatic — monitor.
  • Tumor lysis syndrome: hyperphosphatemia + hyperkalemia + hyperuricemia + hypocalcemia (from Ca-phosphate precipitation) + AKI — an oncologic emergency requiring aggressive hydration ± rasburicase.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Malnourished / alcoholic / prolonged NPO. Highest refeeding-hypophosphatemia risk; check and replete phosphate as nutrition is reintroduced.
  • CKD. Chronic hyperphosphatemia drives renal osteodystrophy and vascular calcification; managed with binders and diet.
  • Pediatrics. Higher normal phosphate ranges during growth (bone turnover) — don't misread against adult cutoffs.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Interpret with calcium, PTH, vitamin D. The mineral-bone axis is a system; phosphate rarely interpreted in isolation in CKD.

ED lens

The electrolyte that crashes in refeeding and DKA treatment and spikes in tumor lysis/rhabdo. Severe hypophosphatemia is a respiratory-failure risk; read PO₄ with calcium.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not routinely checked or corrected unless symptomatic. Relevant if refeeding is attempted for comfort.

  • Pediatrics. Consider trauma (including non-accidental), and structural anomalies as causes of pediatric pancreatitis.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • None. Interpret against the 3× ULN diagnostic threshold and the clinical picture; the absolute value is not corrected or graded.

ED lens

≥3× ULN + compatible pain = pancreatitis; the height doesn't grade severity, and modest bumps are nonspecific (renal, ischemia, DKA). Score severity clinically.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not obtained. Pancreatitis pain at end of life is managed symptomatically per goals.

Endocrine

TSH + Free T4 (± T3)

The most sensitive first-line thyroid test — but interpret the pair, and beware the sick patient.

Reference TSH ~0.4–4.0 mIU/L · Free T4 ~0.8–1.8 ng/dL

▲ Why it rises

  • TSH high. Primary HYPOthyroidism (failing gland, high TSH + low free T4). Subclinical hypothyroidism = high TSH with normal free T4. Also: recovery phase of nonthyroidal illness, poor levothyroxine adherence, and (rarely) TSH-secreting adenoma with high free T4.
  • Free T4 high. HYPERthyroidism (with suppressed TSH): Graves, toxic nodule/multinodular goiter, thyroiditis (release phase), excess thyroid hormone. Rare central causes give high T4 with inappropriately normal/high TSH.

▼ Why it falls

  • TSH low. Primary HYPERthyroidism (suppressed TSH + high free T4/T3). Subclinical hyperthyroidism = low TSH with normal free T4. Also nonthyroidal illness, steroids/dopamine, and central hypothyroidism (low TSH + low free T4 — the pituitary problem).
  • Free T4 low. HYPOthyroidism (primary: high TSH; central: low/normal TSH). Also severe nonthyroidal illness.

Graded thresholds

TSH low (hyper)<0.40.4–4.0>4.0 (hypo)mIU/L
Free T4<0.8 (hypo)0.8–1.8>1.8 (hyper)ng/dL

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Interpret TSH and free T4 as a PAIR: high TSH + low T4 = primary hypothyroid; low TSH + high T4 = primary hyperthyroid; low TSH + low T4 = central hypothyroid (pituitary/hypothalamic — don't miss it); high TSH + high T4 = TSH-secreting adenoma or resistance (rare).
  • Nonthyroidal illness ('sick euthyroid') — the inpatient trap: acutely ill patients show low T3, then low T4 and variable TSH WITHOUT true thyroid disease. Avoid checking thyroid function in acute illness unless thyroid disease is genuinely suspected, and don't treat based on these transient changes — recheck when recovered.
  • Myxedema coma (hypothyroid) and thyroid storm (hyperthyroid) are CLINICAL emergencies treated on the clinical picture — don't wait for confirmatory labs to act.
  • Amiodarone, lithium, checkpoint inhibitors, and steroids all perturb thyroid tests — check a med list before over-interpreting.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Pregnancy. Trimester-specific TSH reference ranges (lower, especially first trimester as hCG stimulates the thyroid); using non-pregnant cutoffs misclassifies. Overt hypothyroidism must be treated for fetal neurodevelopment.
  • Older adults. TSH reference range drifts upward with age; a mildly high TSH in an elder may be normal for age — avoid over-treating subclinical hypothyroidism.
  • Acutely ill inpatients. Nonthyroidal illness syndrome makes thyroid panels hard to interpret — defer unless truly indicated.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Free T4 (and free T3) reflex. When TSH is abnormal, the free hormones localize and grade the disorder; TSH alone can mislead in central disease and sick-euthyroid.

ED lens

TSH + free T4 read as a pair. Beware sick-euthyroid in the acutely ill (defer testing), treat myxedema/storm on clinical grounds, and use pregnancy- and age-appropriate ranges.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not routinely checked. Continue or simplify levothyroxine per goals; new workups are rarely indicated in comfort-focused care.

ABG

Arterial Blood Gas (ABG / VBG)

The acid-base and oxygenation truth-teller — read in a fixed sequence every time.

Reference pH 7.35–7.45 · PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg · HCO₃ 22–26 · PaO₂ 80–100 mmHg · SaO₂ >95%

▲ Why it rises

  • pH ↑ alkalemia. Respiratory alkalosis (hyperventilation — pain, anxiety, sepsis, PE, salicylate, high altitude) or metabolic alkalosis (vomiting, diuretics, hyperaldosteronism).
  • PaCO₂ ↑. Hypoventilation/respiratory acidosis — COPD, sedation/opioids, neuromuscular weakness, fatigue. A rising CO₂ in a tiring asthmatic is an ominous pre-arrest sign.

▼ Why it falls

  • pH ↓ acidemia. Metabolic acidosis (see bicarbonate) or respiratory acidosis (hypoventilation).
  • PaO₂ / SaO₂ ↓. Hypoxemia — V/Q mismatch, shunt, hypoventilation, diffusion defect, low inspired O₂. Compute the A–a gradient to localize.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
pH acidemia<7.357.35–7.45>7.45 alkalemia
PaCO₂<3535–45>45mmHg
PaO₂ hypoxemia<8080–100mmHg

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Fixed reading sequence: (1) pH — acidemic or alkalemic? (2) primary driver — CO₂ (resp) or HCO₃ (metabolic)? (3) compensation appropriate? (4) if metabolic acidosis, anion gap; (5) delta-delta for mixed disorders.
  • Winter's formula for a metabolic acidosis: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5×HCO₃ + 8 (±2). Actual > expected = concurrent respiratory acidosis; < expected = concurrent respiratory alkalosis.
  • A 'normal' CO₂ in a severe asthmatic who should be blowing it off signals impending respiratory failure.
  • VBG substitutes well for pH and CO₂ trend in many scenarios (add ~0.03–0.05 pH, subtract ~4–5 mmHg CO₂) but does NOT assess oxygenation — use pulse ox/ABG for that.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • COPD (chronic CO₂ retainer). Chronically elevated CO₂ with compensatory high bicarb and near-normal pH is the baseline; treat the delta from THEIR baseline, and target their usual SpO₂ (often 88–92%) to avoid oxygen-induced hypercapnia.
  • Pregnancy. Chronic respiratory alkalosis is normal (progesterone-driven); a 'normal' PaCO₂ of 40 in a pregnant patient may actually be relative hypercapnia.
  • High altitude. Lower baseline PaO₂ and a compensated respiratory alkalosis are expected.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • A–a gradient. Calculated (see Calculators); localizes hypoxemia to shunt/V-Q mismatch vs hypoventilation.
  • Anion gap + delta-delta + Winter's. The metabolic-acidosis follow-through — see Calculators.

ED lens

The disciplined stepwise read prevents missed mixed disorders. Trend CO₂ in the tiring respiratory patient — the number that turns 'normal' is the one that intubates.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Rarely obtained; acid-base derangements of dying are managed with comfort measures, not correction. Avoid painful arterial sticks unless a result would change comfort-directed care.

Perfusion

Lactic Acid (Lactate)

A tissue-perfusion and metabolic-stress marker — clearance predicts survival.

Reference 0.5–2.0 mmol/L (venous, resting)

▲ Why it rises

  • Type A (hypoperfusion). Anaerobic metabolism from inadequate oxygen delivery: septic shock, hypovolemia, cardiogenic/obstructive shock, severe hypoxemia, mesenteric/limb ischemia, severe anemia. The classic sepsis flag.
  • Type B (non-hypoperfusion). Impaired clearance or altered metabolism WITHOUT tissue hypoxia: liver failure, malignancy, thiamine deficiency, metformin, alcohol, beta-agonists (albuterol), seizures, catecholamine surge, mitochondrial toxins (cyanide, CO).

▼ Why it falls

  • Normal/low. Adequate perfusion and clearance.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Normal0.5–2.0>2.0 elevatedmmol/L
High-risk>4.0 shock territorymmol/L

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Sepsis: lactate ≥2 supports occult hypoperfusion; ≥4 mmol/L defines high-risk/septic-shock territory and drives resuscitation bundles. Lactate clearance (or normalization) is a key resuscitation target and outcome predictor.
  • Don't anchor on 'it's just sepsis' — a very high lactate with a benign abdomen can be mesenteric ischemia ('pain out of proportion'). Consider the type-B mimics (albuterol, seizure, metformin) before over-resuscitating a non-hypoperfusion lactate.
  • Tourniquet/prolonged draw and struggling can artifactually raise a venous lactate — recheck a surprising isolated value.
  • A rising lactate despite resuscitation is a bad-trajectory signal that should prompt source re-evaluation.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Liver failure. Impaired lactate clearance elevates baseline and blunts clearance — interpret trends cautiously.
  • Metformin (esp. with AKI). Metformin-associated lactic acidosis — consider in the acidotic diabetic on metformin with renal impairment.
  • Post-seizure / heavy albuterol. Transient benign lactate elevation; expect rapid clearance and don't mistake for shock.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Contributes to the anion gap. Lactate is a major unmeasured anion in high-gap metabolic acidosis.

ED lens

A frontline sepsis and shock biomarker. Serial lactate/clearance guides resuscitation; think beyond sepsis when perfusion looks adequate.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not obtained in comfort-focused care; an elevated lactate is expected in the actively dying and does not prompt resuscitation.

Inflammation

C-Reactive Protein (CRP)

A sensitive but non-specific acute-phase reactant — and a genuine decision-driver in pediatrics.

Reference <3 mg/L (conventional 'normal' <10 mg/L; hs-CRP used for cardiovascular risk)

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated CRP. Hepatic acute-phase response to IL-6: infection (bacterial > viral, generally), tissue injury/surgery, autoimmune/inflammatory disease, malignancy. Rises within 6–8h, peaks ~48h — faster kinetics than ESR.

▼ Why it falls

  • Low/normal. Absence of significant acute-phase activation; also drops quickly once the stimulus resolves (short half-life ~19h), making it a good response-to-treatment tracker.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Normal<3>10 clinically significantmg/L
Moderate10–100>100 marked (often bacterial/serious)mg/L

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • CRP as a driving factor in pediatrics (your teaching point): in the young/febrile child — especially the well-appearing infant or neonatal sepsis evaluation, and in serious bacterial infection risk-stratification — CRP (often alongside procalcitonin) is weighted heavily because history/exam are limited, classic localizing signs are absent, and the consequences of a missed occult bacterial infection are severe. CRP's rapid kinetics let you trend the trajectory, and a rising CRP with a compatible picture raises the pre-test probability of serious bacterial infection enough to change disposition (admit, cultures, empiric antibiotics). It is used as part of a rule (with PCT, WBC/ANC, and validated pathways), never as a stand-alone.
  • hs-CRP is a distinct assay for cardiovascular risk stratification — not interchangeable with the inflammatory-range CRP.
  • CRP is non-specific: a high value confirms inflammation, not its source. Never let a high CRP substitute for source identification, and don't withhold treatment for a low CRP if the clinical picture is convincing (early/localized infection can have a normal CRP).

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Neonates/infants. Integrated into neonatal-sepsis and febrile-infant pathways; interpreted with age-specific thresholds and serial values because a single early CRP can be normal in true early infection.
  • Pregnancy/postpartum. Baseline mildly elevated; recent delivery/surgery raises it independent of infection.
  • Chronic inflammatory disease. Persistently elevated baseline complicates interpretation of a new acute rise; trend from the patient's own baseline.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Pair with procalcitonin/ESR. PCT is more specific for systemic bacterial infection; ESR is slower and reflects a longer time-window. Using them together improves discrimination.

ED lens

Best as a trend and a pre-test-probability modifier, most decisive in pediatrics and undifferentiated fever. High = inflammation somewhere; it never names the source.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not indicated. Fever and inflammation at end of life are managed symptomatically; a CRP will not change a comfort-focused plan.

Inflammation

Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR)

A slow, nonspecific inflammation gauge — most useful for a few specific diagnoses and for tracking them.

Reference Roughly < (age/2) mm/hr for men; < ((age+10)/2) for women (age-dependent)

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated ESR. Rises with fibrinogen/immunoglobulins (acute-phase and chronic inflammation): infection, autoimmune/rheumatic disease, malignancy, tissue injury. Rises and falls SLOWLY (days-weeks) — reflects a longer time-window than CRP. Also elevated by anemia, older age, female sex, pregnancy, and hypergammaglobulinemia INDEPENDENT of inflammation.

▼ Why it falls

  • Low ESR. Polycythemia, extreme leukocytosis, sickle cell disease, hypofibrinogenemia, heart failure, cachexia — conditions that mechanically slow sedimentation. A low ESR can mask underlying inflammation.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Normal (age-dependent)< age/2 (M)> age/2 elevatedmm/hr
Markedly high>100 (infection/malignancy/autoimmune)mm/hr

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Highest-yield uses: giant cell (temporal) arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica (markedly elevated, often >50; a driver for urgent steroids to prevent blindness), and following disease activity in osteomyelitis, endocarditis, and some rheumatic diseases.
  • Very high ESR (>100 mm/hr) narrows the differential: serious infection (osteomyelitis, endocarditis, abscess), malignancy (especially multiple myeloma — check with the total protein/globulin gap), or major autoimmune disease.
  • ESR is slow and nonspecific — pair with CRP (faster kinetics). A normal ESR does not exclude acute inflammation (CRP may already be up while ESR lags), and a high ESR names no source.
  • Not a screening test for asymptomatic patients — the pretest probability must justify it.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Older adults. ESR rises with age; use an age-adjusted upper limit (Miller: men age/2, women (age+10)/2) to avoid over-calling.
  • Female sex / pregnancy. Higher baseline independent of disease.
  • Anemia. Falsely elevates ESR (fewer RBCs sediment faster); interpret alongside the hematocrit.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Age-adjusted upper limit. Miller formula (above) prevents mislabeling a physiologically higher elderly ESR as pathologic.
  • Pair with CRP. CRP is faster and less confounded; using both improves interpretation.

ED lens

A slow, confounded marker best reserved for GCA/PMR and tracking specific chronic infections. Age-adjust the upper limit, pair with CRP, and remember a high value never names its source.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not indicated; will not change a comfort-focused plan.

Inflammation

Procalcitonin (PCT)

The most bacteria-specific of the common inflammatory markers — an antibiotic-stewardship tool, not a rule-out.

Reference <0.1 ng/mL healthy; interpretation is threshold-banded (below)

▲ Why it rises

  • Elevated PCT. Upregulated by bacterial endotoxin/cytokines (and suppressed by interferon-gamma in viral illness), so it rises preferentially in systemic BACTERIAL infection and sepsis. Also non-infectious: major surgery, burns, trauma, cardiogenic shock, first days post-arrest, medullary thyroid carcinoma.

▼ Why it falls

  • Low PCT. Low likelihood of systemic bacterial infection — supports withholding/stopping antibiotics in the right context (e.g., stable respiratory infection). Localized infection without systemic response can have a low PCT.

Graded thresholds

DecreasedNormalElevated
Unlikely<0.25>0.25 antibiotics encouragedng/mL
Sepsis threshold>0.5 sepsis likelyng/mL
High≥2.0 strong systemic bacterial/sepsisng/mL

Verify before relying: thresholds vary by assay, lab, and clinical context. Correlate with the patient.

Special situations & can't-miss

  • Threshold bands (respiratory-infection / stewardship context — verify against your local algorithm): <0.25 ng/mL bacterial infection unlikely, antibiotics discouraged; >0.25 increased likelihood, antibiotics encouraged. For sepsis: <0.5 lower likelihood; >0.5 increased likelihood; ≥2.0 ng/mL strongly suggests systemic bacterial infection/sepsis with higher severity.
  • Primary evidence-based use is antibiotic STEWARDSHIP — guiding when to START and especially when to STOP antibiotics — and serial trends for de-escalation, not as a stand-alone sepsis rule-out.
  • In suspected sepsis, treat unstable patients empirically regardless of PCT — its imperfect sensitivity means a low value never justifies withholding antibiotics from a shocked patient.
  • PCT does not respond to viral infections, Mycoplasma/atypicals, or purely localized infection — a normal PCT in viral illness is expected and helps de-escalate.

Age / sex / population modifiers

  • Renal failure / dialysis. Reduced clearance elevates baseline PCT; use higher thresholds and trends rather than a single cutoff.
  • Neutropenia / immunosuppression. PCT is leukocyte-mediated; neutropenia can blunt the response, and data are limited in the immunosuppressed.
  • Cancer patients. Specificity falls; a higher cutoff (~1.0 ng/mL) has been proposed for sepsis in active cancer — interpret cautiously.
  • Neonates. Physiologic PCT surge in the first ~48h of life requires age-adjusted reference values, distinct from the adult bands.

Corrections & companion calculations

  • Use as a trend + with clinical picture. Serial PCT falling by ~80% supports antibiotic discontinuation in stewardship protocols; interpret with the whole clinical picture.

ED lens

More specific for bacterial infection than CRP/WBC and best used to guide starting and stopping antibiotics — but never overrides clinical instability in suspected sepsis.

Hospitalist / hospice & palliative lens

Not indicated; will not alter a comfort-focused plan.

Bedside math

Calculators

The companion calculations referenced throughout the atlas — gap chemistry, oxygenation, renal estimation, and osmolar screening. Formulas are shown so they stay teachable.

Anion Gap (+ albumin correction)

AG = Na − (Cl + HCO₃). Normal ~8–12. Corrects for hypoalbuminemia, which otherwise hides a gap acidosis.

—

Glucose-Corrected Sodium

Hyperglycemia pulls water intravascularly and dilutes sodium. Adds 1.6 (or 2.4) per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100.

—

Albumin-Corrected Calcium

Low albumin lowers total calcium without lowering the active ionized fraction. Corrected Ca = Ca + 0.8×(4.0 − albumin).

—

A–a Gradient

Localizes hypoxemia. A–a = PAO₂ − PaO₂, where PAO₂ = FiO₂×(Patm−47) − PaCO₂/0.8. Normal ≈ (age/4)+4 on room air.

—

Winter's Formula

Expected respiratory compensation for a metabolic acidosis. Expected PaCO₂ = 1.5×HCO₃ + 8 (±2).

—

Delta–Delta Ratio

Detects a mixed disorder hiding behind a gap acidosis. Δgap/Δbicarb = (AG−12)/(24−HCO₃).

—

Osmolar Gap

Screens for toxic alcohols. Calc osm = 2×Na + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8. Gap = measured − calculated (normal < 10).

—

eGFR (CKD-EPI 2021, race-free)

Estimates GFR from creatinine. Especially important when muscle mass is atypical — a 'normal' Cr can hide low GFR.

—

Creatinine Clearance (Cockcroft–Gault)

Weight-based CrCl still used in many drug-dosing references. CrCl = (140−age)×wt×(0.85 if female)/(72×Cr).

—

Effective Osmolality (HHS)

Effective osm = 2×Na + glucose/18 (BUN omitted, as urea crosses membranes freely). Drives HHS assessment.

—

Absolute Neutrophil Count

ANC = WBC × (%neutrophils + %bands)/100. Drives febrile-neutropenia decisions (<500 severe).

—

BUN:Creatinine Ratio

>20:1 suggests prerenal azotemia or GI bleed; 10–15:1 suggests intrinsic renal.

—

VBG ↔ ABG Estimate

Estimates arterial values from a venous gas (or vice-versa). Venous pH runs ~0.03–0.04 lower and venous PCO₂ ~4–5 mmHg higher than arterial. Does NOT estimate oxygenation — use pulse ox/ABG for PaO₂.

—

Age-Adjusted D-Dimer

For patients >50y with LOW/intermediate pretest probability. Cutoff = age × 10 ng/mL FEU (not for DDU assays). Confirm your lab reports FEU before using.

—
Verify before relying. Every threshold, dialysis/treatment cutoff, drug-dosing estimate, and decision rule here is a teaching reference, not a substitute for the current guideline, your local assay's reference interval, or clinical judgment. Correlate all values with the patient in front of you.

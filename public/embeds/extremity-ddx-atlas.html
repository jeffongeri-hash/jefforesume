Extremity Pain Differential Diagnosis Atlas

193 named conditions across shoulder, elbow, wrist/hand, hip, knee, and ankle/foot — grouped by anatomic location, each with mechanism of injury, exam findings, typical presentation, distinguishing mimickers, and imaging/workup.

Shoulder pain differentials split naturally by WHERE the pain is (anterior/lateral/posterior) and whether it moves with the joint or is referred from the neck, chest, or abdomen. Location plus the age/activity profile narrows this list fast.

Anterior Shoulder 12 conditions

Biceps Tendinopathy / Tenosynovitis (long head)

MechanismRepetitive overhead loading or a degenerative tendon irritated as it tracks through the bicipital groove; often coexists with impingement/cuff disease rather than occurring in isolation.
Exam FindingsTenderness directly over the bicipital groove (~3 cm below the anterior acromion, arm in slight IR) that moves with the tendon on rotation. Positive Speed's test (resisted forward flexion, elbow extended, forearm supinated) and Yergason's test (resisted supination with elbow at 90°).
Typical PresentationInsidious anterior shoulder ache in an overhead athlete or middle-aged patient, worse with lifting/reaching, often described as a 'catching' ache rather than sharp pain.
Distinguishing MimickersSLAP tear (biceps anchor pathology — consider if mechanical catching/popping or a distinct traumatic onset); subacromial impingement (concomitant, very common); AC joint arthritis (more superior, worse with cross-body adduction).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound can show tendon thickening/fluid in the sheath. MRI/MR arthrogram if SLAP tear is suspected.

SLAP Tear (Superior Labrum Anterior-to-Posterior)

MechanismTraction injury (fall on outstretched arm, sudden pull) or repetitive overhead throwing generating peel-back forces on the biceps anchor.
Exam FindingsMechanical popping/catching with overhead motion; O'Brien's active compression test (pain deep in the joint with resisted forward flexion in pronation, relieved in supination) is the most commonly used, though poorly specific in isolation.
Typical PresentationOverhead athlete (thrower, swimmer) with deep, poorly localized anterior shoulder pain and a sense of internal derangement/catching, often after a specific traumatic event or with progressive throwing-related decline in velocity/control.
Distinguishing MimickersBiceps tendinopathy (may coexist); internal impingement in throwers; AC joint pathology.
Imaging / WorkupMR arthrogram is the imaging test of choice — far more sensitive than standard MRI for labral tears; plain films to exclude bony pathology first.

AC Joint Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative, cumulative loading over years; accelerated by prior AC separation or heavy lifting history.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the AC joint (a fingertip-width area). Positive cross-body (horizontal) adduction test. Often a visible/palpable prominence if there's a prior separation.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (>40s) or former weightlifter with focal 'top of shoulder' pain, worse reaching across the body (putting on a seatbelt, reaching for the opposite pocket) rather than overhead.
Distinguishing MimickersDistal clavicle osteolysis (younger weightlifters, more acute); subacromial impingement (more diffuse, positive Neer/Hawkins rather than isolated AC tenderness); AC separation (history of trauma, step-off deformity).
Imaging / WorkupZanca view (AP with 10–15° cephalic tilt) is the best plain film; diagnostic AC lidocaine injection can confirm the pain generator.

AC Joint Separation (Sprain)

MechanismDirect fall onto the point of the shoulder (adducted arm) — classic mechanism in cycling falls and contact sports.
Exam FindingsTenderness and swelling over the AC joint; a visible step-off/prominence of the distal clavicle in higher grades (III+); painful cross-body adduction.
Typical PresentationAcute injury after a fall directly onto the shoulder, with focal superior pain and a visible bump if higher grade.
Distinguishing MimickersClavicle fracture (tenderness along the shaft, not isolated to the joint); distal clavicle fracture (can mimic a high-grade separation on exam).
Imaging / WorkupBilateral AP shoulder views ± weighted stress views to grade (Rockwood classification I–VI); CT if surgical planning needed.

Distal Clavicle Osteolysis

MechanismRepetitive microtrauma to the distal clavicle from chronic heavy loading — classically weightlifters ('weightlifter's shoulder'), especially bench press and overhead pressing.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness at the AC joint, positive cross-body adduction, pain reproduced with bench press-type loading.
Typical PresentationYoung to middle-aged weightlifter with insidious, activity-related AC pain worsening over months, no discrete trauma.
Distinguishing MimickersAC osteoarthritis (older, less activity-specific); AC separation (acute trauma history).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may show distal clavicle resorption/cystic change; MRI more sensitive early.

Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder) — Freezing Phase

MechanismIdiopathic capsular fibrosis and contracture; strongly associated with diabetes, thyroid disease, and prior shoulder immobilization (post-surgery, post-fracture, post-stroke).
Exam FindingsGLOBAL restriction of BOTH active AND passive ROM in all planes, especially external rotation — the hallmark that separates this from a rotator cuff problem (where passive ROM is preserved).
Typical PresentationDiabetic or middle-aged (40–60s) patient with progressive, diffuse aching shoulder pain evolving over weeks to months into significant stiffness, often worse at night, no clear inciting trauma.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff tear (AROM lost, PROM preserved — the key distinguisher); glenohumeral OA (crepitus, bony endpoint, older patient, x-ray changes); calcific tendinitis (more acute onset).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; plain films to exclude OA/calcific tendinitis; MRI not routinely needed unless atypical.

Glenohumeral Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative cartilage loss, often idiopathic or post-traumatic (prior dislocation, fracture) or secondary to cuff arthropathy (chronic massive cuff tear allowing humeral head migration).
Exam FindingsCrepitus with ROM, restricted AROM AND PROM (bony end-feel), pain at end-range, may have a positive grind.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (60s+) with gradual, progressive deep shoulder ache and stiffness, often worse with activity and at night.
Distinguishing MimickersAdhesive capsulitis (softer end-feel, younger/diabetic profile); cuff arthropathy (long-standing cuff tear history).
Imaging / WorkupPlain AP/axillary/scapular-Y views show joint space narrowing, osteophytes, humeral head flattening.

Anterior Glenohumeral Instability / Labral (Bankart) Tear

MechanismTraumatic anterior dislocation (arm forced into abduction/external rotation) or recurrent subluxation in a young athlete; a Bankart lesion (anteroinferior labral tear) is the classic associated injury.
Exam FindingsPositive apprehension test with relief on relocation (posterior force); may have a sulcus sign if multidirectional laxity coexists.
Typical PresentationYoung athlete (contact sports, overhead throwing) with a history of dislocation/subluxation, now with recurrent apprehension or a sense the shoulder will 'come out' with overhead/abduction-ER positions.
Distinguishing MimickersSLAP tear (deeper, less positional apprehension); multidirectional instability (more generalized laxity, often atraumatic).
Imaging / WorkupMR arthrogram to characterize labral injury and any associated Hill-Sachs/bony Bankart lesion; plain films first to exclude fracture.

Subscapularis Tear

MechanismTraumatic hyperextension/external rotation force, or degenerative tearing often associated with biceps subluxation (subscapularis stabilizes the biceps in the groove).
Exam FindingsPositive lift-off test and/or belly-press test; may have increased passive external rotation compared to the contralateral side (loss of the anterior restraint).
Typical PresentationAnterior shoulder pain with weakness in internal rotation activities (tucking in a shirt, pushing off), sometimes after a traumatic hyperextension event, sometimes insidious in older patients.
Distinguishing MimickersSupraspinatus/infraspinatus tear (different weakness pattern — abduction/ER rather than IR); biceps instability alone.
Imaging / WorkupMRI; ultrasound can also assess dynamically.

Calcific Tendinitis (Supraspinatus)

MechanismCalcium hydroxyapatite deposition within the tendon, with acute presentations occurring during the resorptive phase when the crystal deposit becomes acutely inflammatory.
Exam FindingsMarked tenderness and often significant restriction of ALL motion during the acute resorptive attack — can look like a septic joint or severe impingement.
Typical PresentationSudden-onset, SEVERE shoulder pain (often waking the patient from sleep) with marked restriction of motion, frequently in a patient 30–60, no trauma history — the acuity and severity often prompts an ED visit.
Distinguishing MimickersSeptic arthritis (fever, systemic signs — always consider); acute rotator cuff tear; gout/pseudogout of the shoulder (rare but occurs).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films show the calcific deposit (often diagnostic); if any concern for infection, aspirate.

Pectoralis Major Rupture

MechanismEccentric overload during bench press (classically lowering a heavy weight) — the tendon fails at the humeral insertion.
Exam FindingsEcchymosis and a palpable/visible defect in the anterior axillary fold; asymmetric pectoral contour with resisted adduction; weakness with adduction/internal rotation.
Typical PresentationYoung to middle-aged male weightlifter with a sudden 'pop' and sharp pain during a heavy bench press, immediate bruising over the chest/axilla.
Distinguishing MimickersPectoralis strain (partial, no palpable defect); biceps rupture (defect is at the elbow/arm, different mechanism).
Imaging / WorkupMRI to confirm complete vs. partial tear and plan surgical timing (early repair has better outcomes).

Sternoclavicular Joint Injury / Dislocation

MechanismDirect blow to the medial clavicle or indirect force through the shoulder (classic: pile-up in football/rugby).
Exam FindingsTenderness and possible deformity at the SC joint; ANTERIOR dislocation shows a visible prominence; POSTERIOR dislocation may show a relative depression with the medial clavicle less prominent — the dangerous one.
Typical PresentationTrauma with medial chest/shoulder pain; posterior dislocations may present with dysphagia, dyspnea, or a sense of chest fullness from mediastinal structure compression — a TRUE ORTHOPEDIC EMERGENCY.
Distinguishing MimickersClavicle fracture (more lateral, palpable crepitus along shaft); costochondral injury.
Imaging / WorkupCT chest/SC joints is the study of choice — plain films are notoriously difficult to interpret at this joint. Posterior dislocation warrants immediate surgical/vascular consultation given proximity to great vessels and trachea.

Lateral / Subacromial Shoulder 4 conditions

Subacromial Impingement Syndrome

MechanismMechanical compression of the rotator cuff/subacromial bursa between the humeral head and the coracoacromial arch during overhead elevation; contributed to by acromial morphology, poor scapular mechanics, or cuff weakness.
Exam FindingsPainful arc 60–120° of abduction; positive Neer and Hawkins-Kennedy signs (sensitive, not specific); weakness may be present if cuff pathology has developed.
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged patient with insidious lateral/anterior shoulder pain, worse with overhead reaching, often occupational (painters, manual laborers) or from repetitive overhead sport.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff tear (weakness is more prominent, positive lag signs); AC arthritis (more isolated superior tenderness); calcific tendinitis (more acute/severe).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; plain films to assess acromial morphology and exclude calcific tendinitis/OA; MRI if failing conservative management or a tear is suspected.

Rotator Cuff Tear (Partial or Full-Thickness)

MechanismDegenerative tearing from chronic impingement/tendinopathy (most common, older patients) or acute traumatic tear (fall, forced eccentric load, or during a dislocation in older patients).
Exam FindingsWeakness on empty/full can test (supraspinatus), external rotation lag sign or resisted ER weakness (infraspinatus), drop arm test if large/massive tear; atrophy of the supraspinatus/infraspinatus fossa may be visible in chronic tears.
Typical PresentationPatient over 40 with lateral shoulder pain and weakness, often night pain, may follow a fall or sudden pull, or be purely degenerative/insidious; younger patients typically need a clear traumatic mechanism.
Distinguishing MimickersImpingement alone (no true weakness); adhesive capsulitis (PROM also restricted, unlike a cuff tear); cervical radiculopathy (neck pain, dermatomal distribution).
Imaging / WorkupMRI is the gold standard for tear size/retraction; ultrasound is a good dynamic, lower-cost alternative in experienced hands.

Subacromial-Subdeltoid Bursitis

MechanismOveruse/repetitive overhead activity or secondary to underlying cuff/impingement pathology causing bursal inflammation.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the lateral subacromial region, painful arc, often overlapping impingement signs.
Typical PresentationSimilar to impingement — activity-related lateral shoulder pain; can have an acute flare with more focal swelling/warmth.
Distinguishing MimickersImpingement/rotator cuff disease (frequently coexists); septic bursitis (warmth, fever — consider if acute and systemic).

Internal (Posterosuperior) Impingement

MechanismSeen specifically in overhead throwers — repetitive abduction/external rotation causes the undersurface of the posterior cuff to contact the posterosuperior glenoid/labrum.
Exam FindingsPain reproduced in the late cocking (abduction/max ER) position; often associated with a GIRD (glenohumeral internal rotation deficit) on exam.
Typical PresentationCompetitive overhead thrower (baseball pitcher) with posterior shoulder pain specifically during the late cocking phase, progressive decline in throwing performance.
Distinguishing MimickersSLAP tear (frequently coexists); standard subacromial impingement (different provocative position).
Imaging / WorkupMR arthrogram; clinical exam and throwing history are central to diagnosis.

Posterior Shoulder 5 conditions

Posterior Labral Tear / Posterior Instability

MechanismPosteriorly directed force on a flexed, adducted arm (classic: falling forward onto an outstretched hand, or an axial load through a flexed elbow — also seen after seizures and electrocutions with bilateral involvement).
Exam FindingsPosterior apprehension/jerk test; pain with posterior-directed load on a forward-flexed, internally rotated arm.
Typical PresentationLess common than anterior instability; may follow a seizure (classically BILATERAL posterior shoulder pain/dislocation) or a fall onto an outstretched flexed arm; often subtler and more frequently missed than anterior dislocation.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff pathology; anterior instability (opposite direction, more common).
Imaging / WorkupAxillary view is essential — posterior dislocation is notoriously missed on a standard AP alone ("light bulb sign"). MR arthrogram for labral characterization.

Infraspinatus/Teres Minor Tendinopathy

MechanismOveruse in overhead athletes or degenerative change as part of broader cuff disease.
Exam FindingsWeakness/pain with resisted external rotation, tenderness posteriorly over the infraspinatus fossa.
Typical PresentationPosterior shoulder ache in an overhead athlete, or an older patient with broader cuff degeneration.
Distinguishing MimickersSuprascapular neuropathy (weakness without pain, atrophy); posterior labral pathology.

Suprascapular Nerve Entrapment

MechanismCompression at the suprascapular notch (affects BOTH supraspinatus and infraspinatus) or more distally at the spinoglenoid notch (affects infraspinatus ONLY) — most often from a paralabral cyst arising from a labral tear, or from repetitive traction in overhead athletes (volleyball is classic for isolated infraspinatus involvement).
Exam FindingsWeakness of external rotation (and abduction if proximal/notch-level) often with LESS pain than expected for the degree of weakness; visible infraspinatus fossa atrophy in chronic cases.
Typical PresentationInsidious, often painless or mildly painful weakness noticed over weeks-months, sometimes an overhead athlete (volleyball spiker) or found incidentally when a 'cuff tear' doesn't add up on exam.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff tear (this is the big one to distinguish — nerve entrapment causes disproportionate weakness with relatively little pain and pinpoint atrophy patterns on exam); cervical radiculopathy (C5-C6, but that gives a broader distribution).
Imaging / WorkupMRI to look for a spinoglenoid/paralabral cyst and characteristic denervation edema/atrophy of the involved muscle; EMG confirms the neuropathy and localizes it.

Quadrilateral Space Syndrome

MechanismCompression of the axillary nerve (and posterior circumflex humeral artery) within the quadrilateral space (bordered by teres minor, teres major, long head of triceps, humeral shaft) — classically from fibrous bands, or dynamically in throwing/overhead athletes with the arm in abduction-external rotation.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the quadrilateral space (posterior shoulder); may have poorly localized, non-dermatomal paresthesias into the arm; teres minor (and sometimes deltoid) atrophy in chronic cases.
Typical PresentationYoung athlete (overhead throwing/volleyball) with vague posterior shoulder pain and paresthesias worsened by overhead positioning, sometimes described as claudication-like with repetitive use.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff disease, cervical radiculopathy, thoracic outlet syndrome, suprascapular nerve entrapment — this is a genuinely difficult diagnosis of exclusion.
Imaging / WorkupMRI showing selective teres minor ± deltoid denervation/atrophy is the key finding; MR/CT angiography can show posterior circumflex humeral artery compromise with the arm positioned in abduction-ER.

Scapular Dyskinesis / Snapping Scapula Syndrome

MechanismAbnormal scapulothoracic mechanics from muscular weakness/imbalance (serratus anterior, trapezius) or, in snapping scapula, mechanical friction between the scapula and thoracic wall (bursitis, bony variants, or muscle atrophy).
Exam FindingsVisible scapular winging or dyskinesis with arm elevation; palpable/audible crepitus or 'snap' with scapular motion in snapping scapula variant.
Typical PresentationOverhead athlete or someone with chronic postural strain, presenting with vague periscapular ache and a visible abnormal scapular movement pattern, or an audible/palpable grinding with shoulder shrugs.
Distinguishing MimickersLong thoracic nerve palsy (serratus winging — more pronounced, weakness-driven); rotator cuff disease.
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical/observational; dynamic ultrasound or CT for snapping scapula bony anatomy if surgical planning needed.

Fracture, Infection & Referred Pain 9 conditions

Proximal Humerus Fracture

MechanismFall onto an outstretched hand or direct lateral fall onto the shoulder, especially in osteoporotic older adults; higher energy in younger patients.
Exam FindingsMarked tenderness, swelling, ecchymosis (may track down the arm days later), painful/limited ROM; always check axillary nerve sensation (lateral shoulder 'regimental badge') given proximity.
Typical PresentationOlder patient after a ground-level fall with immediate shoulder pain and inability to raise the arm; ecchymosis often more visible 24–48h later.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff tear (can coexist, especially in greater tuberosity fractures); dislocation (may occur together — fracture-dislocation).
Imaging / WorkupAP, scapular-Y, and axillary views; CT if surgical planning or comminution needs characterization.

Clavicle Fracture

MechanismDirect fall onto the shoulder or outstretched hand; common in cyclists and contact sports; mid-shaft is the most frequent location.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness, palpable deformity/crepitus along the shaft, skin tenting risk with significant displacement (assess skin integrity).
Typical PresentationAcute injury with focal clavicular pain, arm held adducted against the body, visible deformity in displaced fractures.
Distinguishing MimickersAC separation (more distal, joint-focused); SC injury (more medial).
Imaging / WorkupAP clavicle view; CT if significantly comminuted/displaced or for surgical planning; always assess for skin tenting/neurovascular compromise (brachial plexus is nearby).

Glenohumeral Dislocation (Anterior/Posterior/Inferior)

MechanismANTERIOR (~95% of cases): forced abduction-external rotation. POSTERIOR: seizure, electrocution, or a direct posterior blow to a flexed adducted arm. INFERIOR (luxatio erecta, rare): forced hyperabduction.
Exam FindingsAnterior: arm held in slight abduction/external rotation, loss of normal deltoid contour ('squared-off' shoulder), palpable anterior humeral head. Posterior: arm held adducted/internally rotated, often subtle. ALWAYS document axillary nerve sensation and distal pulses before and after reduction.
Typical PresentationTraumatic event or seizure with acute shoulder pain and visible deformity (anterior) or a more subtle presentation with the arm locked in internal rotation (posterior, frequently missed initially).
Distinguishing MimickersProximal humerus fracture (may coexist); posterior dislocation is specifically mimicked by 'just a sprain' if the axillary view isn't obtained.
Imaging / WorkupAP + axillary (or scapular-Y) views are mandatory — posterior dislocations are missed on AP alone. Post-reduction films and repeat neurovascular exam are essential.

Septic Arthritis of the Glenohumeral Joint

MechanismHematogenous seeding, direct inoculation (injection, surgery), or contiguous spread; risk factors include IVDU, immunosuppression, diabetes, and recent joint injection.
Exam FindingsWarmth, effusion (harder to appreciate than in the knee given the joint's depth), severely painful and globally restricted ROM (both active and passive), often systemically ill.
Typical PresentationFever, severe shoulder pain, and near-complete inability to move the joint, often in an at-risk host (diabetic, IVDU, immunosuppressed, dialysis).
Distinguishing MimickersAcute calcific tendinitis (can look strikingly similar — severe pain, restricted motion, but typically afebrile); adhesive capsulitis (much more gradual onset, not acutely toxic-appearing).
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis is essential (image-guided given joint depth); blood cultures, inflammatory markers; do not delay for imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

Avascular Necrosis of the Humeral Head

MechanismDisruption of the humeral head blood supply — corticosteroid use, alcohol use disorder, sickle cell disease, prior fracture/dislocation, or idiopathic.
Exam FindingsProgressive restriction of motion and pain, may have crepitus in later stages; nonspecific early in the disease course.
Typical PresentationPatient with a known risk factor (chronic steroid use, sickle cell, heavy alcohol use) presenting with gradually worsening deep shoulder pain, sometimes bilateral.
Distinguishing MimickersGlenohumeral OA (older, more insidious, no risk-factor profile needed); adhesive capsulitis.
Imaging / WorkupMRI is far more sensitive than plain films for early-stage AVN, which can appear normal on x-ray.

Parsonage-Turner Syndrome (Neuralgic Amyotrophy / Brachial Plexitis)

MechanismIdiopathic or post-infectious/post-vaccination inflammatory brachial plexopathy; presumed autoimmune.
Exam FindingsPatchy, often multi-nerve-distribution weakness and atrophy (not confined to a single muscle the way isolated nerve entrapment is) that develops AFTER the acute pain phase resolves.
Typical PresentationClassic biphasic course: sudden, severe, often nocturnal shoulder/arm pain (can mimic a cuff tear or even cardiac pain in its intensity) that resolves over days to weeks, FOLLOWED by profound, sometimes multi-muscle weakness and atrophy that becomes apparent as the pain subsides.
Distinguishing MimickersCervical radiculopathy (more dermatomal, associated neck pain); rotator cuff tear (weakness pattern doesn't fit a single tendon); suprascapular neuropathy (Parsonage-Turner is broader/patchier).
Imaging / WorkupMRI shows denervation edema often in multiple, non-contiguous muscles (a key distinguishing feature from single-nerve entrapment); EMG for confirmation and localization.

Cervical Radiculopathy (C5–C6 referred shoulder pain)

MechanismNerve root compression from disc herniation or foraminal stenosis, most often C5-C6.
Exam FindingsPositive Spurling test; may have dermatomal sensory change and myotomal weakness (C5: deltoid/biceps; C6: wrist extensors); neck pain often present but not always prominent.
Typical PresentationPatient with neck and shoulder pain, sometimes radiating down the arm, that does NOT reproduce with isolated shoulder maneuvers (normal impingement signs) but IS reproduced/worsened with neck extension and rotation.
Distinguishing MimickersRotator cuff disease (very commonly confused — the key is a normal shoulder exam with a positive neck exam); suprascapular neuropathy.
Imaging / WorkupCervical spine MRI; EMG/NCS if the picture is ambiguous or surgery is being considered.

Referred Pain — Diaphragmatic Irritation (Kehr's Sign) & Visceral Sources

MechanismDiaphragmatic irritation refers pain to the shoulder via the phrenic nerve (C3-C5) shared dermatomal pathway. Causes include splenic injury/rupture (classic left shoulder pain — Kehr's sign), subphrenic abscess, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, and cardiac ischemia (can refer to the left shoulder/arm).
Exam FindingsThe shoulder exam itself is entirely NORMAL — full pain-free ROM, no tenderness, no positive provocative tests. This normal shoulder exam in the face of significant reported pain is the critical clue.
Typical PresentationShoulder pain, often left-sided, in a patient with recent trauma (splenic injury), abdominal symptoms, syncope/hypotension (ruptured ectopic, intra-abdominal bleed), or cardiac risk factors and chest symptoms.
Distinguishing MimickersAny true shoulder pathology — the distinguishing feature is a NORMAL shoulder exam plus a systemic/visceral symptom or history that doesn't fit isolated MSK pain.
Imaging / WorkupThis is a 'don't anchor on the shoulder' diagnosis — targeted workup based on the suspected visceral source (FAST/CT for trauma, hCG and pelvic ultrasound for possible ectopic, ECG/troponin for cardiac concern).

Pancoast Tumor (Superior Sulcus Lung Tumor)

MechanismApical lung tumor invading the brachial plexus (typically lower trunk, C8-T1), sympathetic chain, and/or adjacent ribs/vertebrae.
Exam FindingsShoulder/arm pain with an ULNAR distribution (C8-T1) weakness/sensory change; may have Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) from sympathetic chain involvement; normal glenohumeral provocative tests.
Typical PresentationSmoker, typically 50s-60s, with progressive shoulder and medial arm/hand pain, weakness, and possibly ipsilateral Horner's syndrome — often initially misattributed to a musculoskeletal cause for months before diagnosis.
Distinguishing MimickersCervical radiculopathy (C8-T1 distribution overlaps); thoracic outlet syndrome; peripheral neuropathy.
Imaging / WorkupChest x-ray may show an apical mass/cap; CT chest and MRI to assess brachial plexus and chest wall invasion; this diagnosis is frequently DELAYED because it masquerades as a musculoskeletal shoulder problem — red flags are a smoking history, Horner's signs, and an ulnar-distribution deficit.

Elbow pain sorts cleanly by quadrant — lateral, medial, posterior, anterior — and the medial/lateral split is almost always driven by whether the mechanism was a valgus (medial-sided) or varus/extension (lateral-sided) overload.

Lateral Elbow 7 conditions

Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow)

MechanismRepetitive wrist extension/grip loading causing angiofibroblastic degeneration (a tendinosis, not true inflammation) of the extensor carpi radialis brevis origin at the lateral epicondyle.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness just distal/anterior to the lateral epicondyle; pain reproduced with resisted wrist extension (with the elbow extended) and resisted middle-finger extension.
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged patient (despite the name, more common in non-tennis players — manual laborers, computer users) with insidious lateral elbow ache worse with gripping (shaking hands, lifting a coffee cup).
Distinguishing MimickersRadial tunnel syndrome (tenderness more distal, over the supinator, worsened by resisted supination rather than pure wrist extension, no true motor weakness); PIN syndrome (true motor weakness, no sensory change since PIN is purely motor); radiocapitellar OA/plica (mechanical clicking, effusion).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound can show tendinosis changes/tearing; plain films if suspecting OA or if failing conservative treatment.

Radial Tunnel Syndrome

MechanismCompression of the radial nerve (posterior interosseous branch) within the radial tunnel, most often at the arcade of Fröhse (proximal edge of supinator).
Exam FindingsTenderness ~4 cm distal to the lateral epicondyle (more distal than lateral epicondylitis), pain with resisted supination and resisted long-finger extension; NO true motor weakness (distinguishing it from PIN syndrome) since compression here is often more irritative than compressive.
Typical PresentationDiffuse, deep lateral forearm ache that can coexist with or mimic lateral epicondylitis, often described as more diffuse and less sharply localized, and classically FAILS treatment for lateral epicondylitis.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral epicondylitis (more proximal tenderness, resisted wrist extension is the trigger rather than supination); PIN syndrome (has true weakness).
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical; EMG/NCS can help but is often normal since this is more a pain syndrome than a true compressive neuropathy; MRI to exclude a mass lesion.

Posterior Interosseous Nerve (PIN) Syndrome

MechanismTrue compressive neuropathy of the PIN (motor branch of radial nerve) at the arcade of Fröhse, from a mass (lipoma, ganglion), direct trauma, or radial head fracture/dislocation.
Exam FindingsTRUE motor weakness — finger and thumb extension weakness with WRIST RADIAL DEVIATION on attempted extension (ECRL spared since it's innervated proximal to the PIN split), no sensory loss (PIN is a pure motor branch).
Typical PresentationProgressive finger drop / weakness in extending the fingers, sometimes after a radial head fracture or with a slowly enlarging lateral elbow mass, generally LESS painful than lateral epicondylitis.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral epicondylitis (pain-predominant, no true weakness); radial tunnel syndrome (pain-predominant, no weakness); cervical radiculopathy (C7 — but that would have sensory change too).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS confirms and localizes; MRI to look for a compressive mass.

Radial Head Fracture

MechanismFall onto an outstretched hand with the elbow extended and forearm pronated, transmitting axial load through the radiocapitellar joint.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the radial head, painful/limited forearm rotation (pronation/supination) more than flexion/extension, joint effusion (positive 'fat pad sign' on x-ray correlates with a palpable effusion).
Typical PresentationFOOSH injury with lateral elbow pain and specifically painful rotation of the forearm; may have a subtle presentation if non-displaced.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral epicondylitis (no trauma, no effusion, no restricted rotation); elbow dislocation (more global deformity).
Imaging / WorkupAP/lateral/oblique elbow films — a posterior fat pad sign (always abnormal) or a prominent anterior 'sail sign' suggests occult fracture even without a visible fracture line; CT if operative planning needed.

Radiocapitellar Chondromalacia / Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative cartilage wear at the radiocapitellar joint, from cumulative loading, prior trauma, or a valgus overload history (throwers).
Exam FindingsCrepitus and pain with forearm rotation and elbow extension/flexion, may have a mechanical catch; loss of terminal extension is common.
Typical PresentationOlder patient or former throwing athlete with chronic lateral elbow ache, stiffness, and crepitus, worse with rotational activities.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral epicondylitis (no crepitus, no loss of ROM); synovial plica (mechanical snap more prominent).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films show joint space narrowing/osteophytes; MRI if internal derangement (loose body, plica) is suspected.

Synovial Plica Syndrome (Elbow)

MechanismA hypertrophic/fibrotic synovial fold (usually posterolateral) becomes symptomatic, mechanically snapping or impinging within the radiocapitellar joint, often from repetitive pronosupination.
Exam FindingsPalpable/audible snap over the posterolateral radiocapitellar joint with pronation/supination or flexion/extension; localized tenderness just posterior to the lateral epicondyle.
Typical PresentationYounger, active patient (racquet sports, weightlifters) with a mechanical clicking/catching sensation and lateral elbow discomfort, often mimicking lateral epicondylitis but with a mechanical snap component.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral epicondylitis (no true mechanical snap); loose body (more locking than snapping).
Imaging / WorkupMRI can show the thickened plical band; often a diagnosis of exclusion confirmed at arthroscopy.

Lateral Collateral Ligament Injury / Posterolateral Rotatory Instability

MechanismVarus stress or a prior elbow dislocation/surgery destabilizing the lateral ligament complex, leading to recurrent subluxation of the radial head relative to the capitellum.
Exam FindingsPositive pivot-shift/lateral pivot apprehension test; posterolateral rotatory instability test; may report the elbow 'giving way' or clunking, especially pushing up from a chair (supination + valgus + extension load).
Typical PresentationHistory of prior elbow dislocation or surgery, now with recurrent instability episodes, apprehension, or a painful clunk with specific activities like pushing up from a seated position.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple lateral epicondylitis (no instability component); radiocapitellar OA (more chronic pain/stiffness, less true instability).
Imaging / WorkupMRI for the LCL complex; stress fluoroscopy/exam under anesthesia for dynamic instability confirmation.

Medial Elbow 5 conditions

Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's Elbow)

MechanismRepetitive wrist flexion/pronation loading causing tendinosis of the common flexor-pronator origin at the medial epicondyle — much less common than lateral epicondylitis (roughly 1:5 to 1:7 ratio).
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the medial epicondyle, pain with resisted wrist flexion and forearm pronation.
Typical PresentationGolfer, thrower, or manual laborer with insidious medial elbow ache worse with gripping/swinging activities.
Distinguishing MimickersUlnar collateral ligament injury (more distal/deeper tenderness along the ligament, positive valgus stress test, seen in throwers); cubital tunnel syndrome (numbness/tingling in the ulnar distribution, positive Tinel's over the cubital tunnel).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound/MRI if refractory or to exclude UCL injury.

Ulnar Collateral Ligament (UCL) Injury

MechanismRepetitive valgus overload during the late cocking/acceleration phase of throwing (baseball pitchers classically) causing attritional tearing, or an acute valgus traumatic tear.
Exam FindingsTenderness along the UCL course (distal to the medial epicondyle), positive moving valgus stress test (pain reproduced through an arc as the elbow moves from flexion to extension under valgus load) — more sensitive than static valgus stress in throwers.
Typical PresentationCompetitive throwing athlete with medial elbow pain during throwing, classically with a decline in throwing velocity/control and pain specifically during the late cocking/acceleration phase; may report a discrete 'pop' in an acute complete tear.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial epicondylitis (more proximal, less positional/throwing-phase-specific); flexor-pronator strain (more muscular, less ligamentous tenderness); ulnar neuropathy (coexists often given anatomic proximity).
Imaging / WorkupMRI (arthrogram increases sensitivity for partial tears); dynamic ultrasound with valgus stress is increasingly used.

Cubital Tunnel Syndrome (Ulnar Neuropathy at the Elbow)

MechanismUlnar nerve compression/traction at the cubital tunnel (behind the medial epicondyle), from chronic leaning on the elbow, prolonged flexion (sleeping with elbows bent), valgus overload in throwers, or a shallow/subluxating nerve.
Exam FindingsPositive Tinel's sign over the cubital tunnel, positive elbow flexion test (paresthesias with sustained flexion), sensory change in the ulnar 1.5 fingers (small finger and ulnar half of ring finger), late findings include intrinsic hand muscle weakness/atrophy (interossei, hypothenar) and a positive Froment's sign.
Typical PresentationNumbness/tingling in the ring and small fingers, often worse at night or with prolonged elbow flexion (talking on the phone, sleeping), progressing to grip weakness/clumsiness in advanced cases.
Distinguishing MimickersC8-T1 cervical radiculopathy (broader distribution, neck symptoms); Guyon's canal (wrist-level) ulnar entrapment (sensory sparing of the DORSAL ulnar hand, since that branch exits proximal to Guyon's canal — this is the key localizing distinction); UCL injury (throwers, ligamentous rather than neural symptoms).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS confirms and localizes (distinguishes elbow from wrist-level entrapment); MRI/ultrasound if a structural cause (subluxation, ganglion, cubitus valgus deformity) is suspected.

Medial Epicondyle Avulsion Fracture (Little League Elbow — pediatric)

MechanismAcute valgus overload avulsing the medial epicondylar apophysis in a skeletally immature thrower, or chronic repetitive valgus stress causing apophysitis/microavulsion.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness and swelling over the medial epicondyle, pain with resisted wrist flexion and valgus stress; may have a mechanical block to motion if the fragment is displaced/incarcerated in the joint.
Typical PresentationAdolescent throwing athlete (Little League pitcher classically) with acute medial elbow pain during a throw, or chronic progressive medial elbow pain with a documented high pitch count.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple flexor-pronator strain (no bony tenderness, no radiographic finding); UCL sprain (in this age group, the apophysis usually fails before the ligament does).
Imaging / WorkupAP/lateral elbow films with comparison views of the contralateral (uninjured) elbow are often needed given open growth plates — look for widening or displacement of the medial epicondylar apophysis.

Flexor-Pronator Mass Strain

MechanismAcute or repetitive overload of the common flexor-pronator muscle group, often accompanying or preceding UCL injury in throwers.
Exam FindingsDiffuse tenderness over the flexor-pronator mass (more muscular/diffuse than the discrete point tenderness of epicondylitis), pain with resisted wrist flexion/pronation, no ligamentous laxity on valgus stress.
Typical PresentationThrower or overhead athlete with medial forearm/elbow soreness, often early in a UCL injury continuum or as an isolated overuse strain.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial epicondylitis (more focal at the origin); UCL sprain (deeper, ligamentous, positive valgus stress).

Posterior Elbow 5 conditions

Olecranon Bursitis

MechanismDirect trauma/repetitive pressure over the olecranon ('student's elbow' from leaning on a desk) or, less commonly, gout/infection seeding the bursa.
Exam FindingsA distinct, fluctuant, often boggy swelling directly over the olecranon tip, typically with FULL, relatively pain-free elbow ROM (a key distinguisher from intra-articular pathology) unless septic.
Typical PresentationVisible swelling over the point of the elbow, often after leaning/pressure or minor trauma; if warm, erythematous, and associated with fever, concerning for septic bursitis.
Distinguishing MimickersSeptic bursitis (warmth, fever, more pain) vs. simple traumatic/inflammatory bursitis (less pain, no systemic signs) — the distinction changes management entirely; gout/pseudogout (may have tophi or a crystal history); triceps tendon rupture (weakness with extension, different location).
Imaging / WorkupAspirate if any concern for infection or crystal arthropathy — send cell count, gram stain, culture, and crystal analysis; the fluid:serum glucose ratio and cell count help but clinical judgment/culture are definitive.

Triceps Tendinopathy / Rupture

MechanismTendinopathy from repetitive extension loading (throwing, weightlifting); rupture from a sudden eccentric load (a fall onto an outstretched, flexed elbow, or forced flexion during resisted extension — e.g., a bench press failure).
Exam FindingsTendinopathy: tenderness at the triceps insertion, pain with resisted extension. Rupture: palpable defect proximal to the olecranon, significant weakness or inability to extend the elbow against gravity/resistance, positive 'modified Thompson test' (squeeze the triceps — no extension response in complete rupture).
Typical PresentationTendinopathy: gradual posterior elbow ache in an athlete. Rupture: acute posterior elbow pain with a 'pop,' swelling, and marked extension weakness after a fall or heavy lift.
Distinguishing MimickersOlecranon fracture (bony tenderness, positive imaging, may also have extension weakness); olecranon bursitis (preserved strength).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films to exclude an avulsion fracture (a small bony flake at the olecranon suggests a bony avulsion); MRI to confirm and grade a suspected tendon tear.

Olecranon Fracture

MechanismDirect fall onto a flexed elbow (direct blow to the olecranon) or an avulsion-type injury from a sudden triceps contraction against resistance.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness and swelling over the olecranon, palpable gap/defect if displaced, inability to extend the elbow against gravity if the extensor mechanism is disrupted by fracture displacement.
Typical PresentationFall directly onto the point of the elbow with immediate posterior pain, swelling, and difficulty extending the arm.
Distinguishing MimickersTriceps rupture (similar extension weakness, but no bony point tenderness/fracture line); olecranon bursitis (preserved extension strength).
Imaging / WorkupAP/lateral elbow films; CT if comminuted or intra-articular extension needs characterization for surgical planning.

Posterior (Extension) Impingement / Valgus Extension Overload Syndrome

MechanismRepetitive forceful extension in a valgus-loaded elbow (the deceleration/follow-through phase of throwing) drives the olecranon into the olecranon fossa, producing posteromedial osteophytes and loose bodies — frequently coexists with UCL insufficiency, which increases the valgus load transmitted posteriorly.
Exam FindingsPain at terminal, forced extension; tenderness posteromedially at the olecranon tip; may have a mechanical block or locking from loose bodies; often reduced terminal extension (flexion contracture) compared to the contralateral side.
Typical PresentationThrowing athlete (especially pitchers) with posterior elbow pain specifically during ball release/follow-through, sometimes with mechanical locking/catching from loose bodies.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple olecranon bursitis (no mechanical block, no throwing-phase specificity); triceps tendinopathy (more diffuse, not a discrete terminal-extension pain).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may show posteromedial olecranon osteophytes/loose bodies; MRI/CT for full characterization, especially if concurrent UCL injury is suspected.

Elbow Osteoarthritis (Posterior Osteophytes)

MechanismDegenerative change, often accelerated by prior trauma or repetitive high-load throwing/lifting history, with characteristic posterior/posteromedial osteophyte formation limiting extension.
Exam FindingsLoss of terminal extension (flexion contracture), crepitus, possible loose-body locking episodes.
Typical PresentationOlder patient or former heavy-labor/throwing athlete with chronic stiffness and a flexion contracture, sometimes intermittent locking.
Distinguishing MimickersValgus extension overload (younger throwing athlete, more acute); simple bursitis (no ROM loss).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films show osteophytes and joint space changes; CT for loose body localization if surgery is considered.

Anterior Elbow & Other 5 conditions

Distal Biceps Tendon Rupture

MechanismSudden eccentric load on a flexed, supinated elbow (classic: trying to catch a heavy falling object, or a forceful eccentric contraction while lifting) — predominantly affects men in their 40s-50s.
Exam FindingsPalpable defect in the antecubital fossa, proximal retraction of the biceps muscle belly ('Popeye' deformity, though less dramatic than a proximal long head rupture), positive hook test (unable to hook a finger under the intact distal tendon), weakness in supination MORE than flexion (supination is the more specific loss).
Typical PresentationSudden 'pop' and sharp anterior elbow pain during a heavy lift, with immediate bruising and a palpable/visible defect, and specific weakness turning a doorknob or using a screwdriver (supination).
Distinguishing MimickersBiceps tendinopathy/partial tear (no palpable complete defect, hook test negative); brachialis strain.
Imaging / WorkupMRI (or ultrasound in experienced hands) to confirm complete vs. partial tear — early surgical repair (within 2–3 weeks) has substantially better outcomes than delayed repair, so timely referral matters.

Pronator Teres Syndrome

MechanismMedian nerve compression as it passes between the two heads of pronator teres, from repetitive pronation/gripping activities (rowers, weightlifters, manual laborers).
Exam FindingsTenderness over the proximal volar forearm (pronator teres), positive provocative tests with resisted pronation/resisted flexor digitorum superficialis, sensory change in the median distribution INCLUDING the palmar cutaneous branch (thenar palm sensation is affected, unlike in carpal tunnel where this branch is spared since it travels superficial to the flexor retinaculum).
Typical PresentationForearm-dominant aching and paresthesias in the median distribution, often worse with repetitive pronation activity, sometimes confused with carpal tunnel syndrome but with more proximal forearm pain and palm involvement.
Distinguishing MimickersCarpal tunnel syndrome (more distal, nocturnal, spares the palm since the palmar cutaneous branch is unaffected); cervical radiculopathy (C6-C7, broader distribution).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS (though can be falsely normal since this is often a dynamic/positional compression); clinical exam and response to activity modification are often more informative.

Elbow Dislocation

MechanismFall onto an outstretched, extended arm, most often causing a posterior dislocation; higher-energy mechanisms may produce an associated fracture ('terrible triad': dislocation + radial head fracture + coronoid fracture).
Exam FindingsVisible deformity, marked swelling, painful/absent motion; CAREFULLY assess and document brachial artery pulse and median/ulnar/radial nerve function before and after reduction — the brachial artery and nerves are at risk.
Typical PresentationAcute high-energy fall or sports injury with obvious elbow deformity and inability to move the joint.
Distinguishing MimickersSupracondylar fracture (especially in children — similar deformity, but the mechanism/age and radiographic findings differ); radial head fracture/subluxation alone.
Imaging / WorkupPre- and post-reduction plain films are mandatory; assess for associated fractures (terrible triad); vascular imaging (CTA) if any pulse asymmetry or hard signs of vascular injury after reduction.

Radial Head Subluxation (Nursemaid's Elbow — pediatric)

MechanismAxial traction on a pronated, extended arm (classic: an adult pulling/swinging a toddler by the hand/wrist) causing the annular ligament to slip over the radial head.
Exam FindingsThe child holds the arm slightly flexed and pronated, refusing to use it, but with NO swelling, NO bony tenderness, and NO deformity — a notably comfortable-appearing child with pseudo-paralysis of that arm alone.
Typical PresentationToddler (typically 1–4 years) who was pulled/lifted by the hand and now refuses to use the arm, holding it limply at the side.
Distinguishing MimickersOccult fracture (would have point tenderness/swelling, which nursemaid's elbow lacks); a more serious injury should be suspected if there is ANY swelling, deformity, or bony tenderness.
Imaging / WorkupPurely clinical if the history and exam are classic — reduction (hyperpronation or supination-flexion technique) can be attempted without imaging; obtain films first if the history is inconsistent, there's point tenderness/swelling, or reduction attempts fail.

Elbow Septic Arthritis

MechanismHematogenous spread, direct inoculation, or contiguous spread from adjacent infection (e.g., septic olecranon bursitis extending into the joint).
Exam FindingsEffusion, warmth, severe pain with ANY motion (both active and passive), often held in a position of maximal capsular volume (~70–80° flexion); may be febrile/systemically ill.
Typical PresentationAcute, severely painful, swollen elbow with global restriction of motion, often in an at-risk host (diabetic, immunosuppressed, IVDU, recent joint injection).
Distinguishing MimickersSeptic olecranon bursitis (superficial, ROM often preserved — the key distinguishing feature); gout/pseudogout of the elbow (can look identical clinically, requiring aspiration to distinguish).
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis is mandatory; blood cultures and inflammatory markers; do not delay for imaging.

Radial Wrist 6 conditions

De Quervain Tenosynovitis

MechanismRepetitive thumb/wrist use (texting, lifting a baby, repetitive grasping) causing stenosing tenosynovitis of the APL and EPB tendons in the first dorsal compartment.
Exam FindingsTenderness directly over the radial styloid/first dorsal compartment; positive Finkelstein/Eichhoff test (thumb-in-fist ulnar deviation reproduces sharp pain).
Typical PresentationNew parent or repetitive-grip worker with radial-sided wrist pain, worse gripping or lifting with the thumb extended (classically lifting an infant under the arms).
Distinguishing MimickersFirst CMC (thumb basal joint) arthritis — the most common confounder, especially in older patients; test the CMC grind separately since Finkelstein's can be positive in both.
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound can show tendon sheath thickening/fluid if uncertain.

Scaphoid Fracture

MechanismFall onto an outstretched, extended, radially deviated wrist (FOOSH) — the most commonly fractured carpal bone.
Exam FindingsTenderness in the anatomic snuffbox AND at the volar scaphoid tubercle; pain with axial thumb compression ("scaphoid compression test").
Typical PresentationFOOSH injury with radial wrist pain; INITIAL X-RAYS ARE OFTEN NORMAL — this is a classically occult fracture.
Distinguishing MimickersWrist sprain (the default 'safe' diagnosis that leads to missed scaphoid fractures — snuffbox tenderness after a FOOSH should be treated as a fracture until proven otherwise); scapholunate ligament injury.
Imaging / WorkupDedicated scaphoid views; if negative but clinical suspicion remains, THUMB SPICA SPLINT and repeat films or MRI/CT in 10–14 days — missed fractures risk nonunion and avascular necrosis given the retrograde blood supply.

Scapholunate Ligament Injury (SLAC wrist progression)

MechanismFOOSH with the wrist in extension and ulnar deviation, disrupting the scapholunate ligament; if untreated, progresses over years to scapholunate advanced collapse (SLAC) arthritis.
Exam FindingsTenderness just distal to Lister's tubercle over the scapholunate interval; positive Watson (scaphoid shift) test (pain/clunk with dorsally-directed pressure on the scaphoid tubercle during radial-to-ulnar deviation).
Typical PresentationFOOSH with dorsal radial wrist pain and, in chronic/missed cases, a painful clunk with wrist motion and progressive weakness over months to years.
Distinguishing MimickersScaphoid fracture (more volar/snuffbox tenderness, different films needed); simple wrist sprain.
Imaging / WorkupPA clenched-fist view can show scapholunate widening ('Terry Thomas sign'); MRI/arthrogram for ligament integrity if plain films are equivocal.

First CMC (Thumb Basal Joint) Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative wear at the trapeziometacarpal joint, by far most common in postmenopausal women, related to the joint's inherent laxity/shape.
Exam FindingsTenderness at the base of the thumb, positive CMC grind test (axial compression + rotation reproduces pain/crepitus), may have a squared-off appearance to the joint in advanced disease.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (usually a woman >50) with chronic radial-sided thumb/wrist pain worse with pinching/gripping activities (opening jars, turning keys).
Distinguishing MimickersDe Quervain tenosynovitis (more dorsal/first-compartment, positive Finkelstein — both can coexist and both can give a false-positive on the other's test).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films (Robert's view) show joint space narrowing, subluxation, osteophytes.

Intersection Syndrome

MechanismFriction tenosynovitis where the first dorsal compartment tendons (APL/EPB) cross over the second dorsal compartment (ECRL/ECRB), from repetitive wrist extension (rowing, weightlifting, racquet sports).
Exam FindingsTenderness and often palpable/audible crepitus ("squeaking") ~4 cm proximal to the wrist on the dorsoradial forearm — more proximal than De Quervain's.
Typical PresentationAthlete or repetitive-motion worker with dorsal forearm pain/swelling proximal to the wrist, distinctly more proximal than typical De Quervain pain.
Distinguishing MimickersDe Quervain tenosynovitis (more distal, at the radial styloid itself).

Radial Styloid Fracture (Chauffeur's Fracture)

MechanismDirect axial/shear load to the radial styloid, classically from a backfiring hand crank (historical) or a fall with a twisting/shear component.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the radial styloid, swelling, painful wrist motion especially radial deviation.
Typical PresentationFOOSH-type injury with radial-sided wrist pain and swelling.
Distinguishing MimickersScaphoid fracture (more snuffbox-centered); simple sprain.
Imaging / WorkupStandard wrist series; CT if intra-articular extension needs characterization.

Ulnar Wrist 6 conditions

Triangular Fibrocartilage Complex (TFCC) Tear

MechanismAxial load through a pronated, ulnarly-deviated wrist (FOOSH) or chronic ulnar-sided loading/rotation (gymnasts, racquet sports); degenerative tears occur with age and ulnar-positive variance.
Exam FindingsTenderness in the 'ulnar fovea' (between the ulnar styloid and FCU tendon — the fovea sign), pain with the ulnocarpal stress test (axial load + ulnar deviation + rotation), may have a painful/audible click.
Typical PresentationUlnar-sided wrist pain worse with gripping, rotating (turning a doorknob), or weight-bearing through the wrist (push-ups, gymnastics).
Distinguishing MimickersECU tendinopathy/subluxation (more dorsal-ulnar, snapping with supination); lunotriquetral ligament injury; DRUJ instability (may coexist).
Imaging / WorkupMRI or MR arthrogram (arthrogram increases sensitivity for central vs. peripheral tears, which matters for healing potential and treatment).

Extensor Carpi Ulnaris (ECU) Tendinopathy / Subluxation

MechanismRepetitive rotational loading (racquet sports, golf) causing tendinopathy, or an acute supination/flexion injury tearing the ECU subsheath and allowing the tendon to subluxate over the ulnar head.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the dorsal-ulnar wrist at the ECU groove; a palpable/visible SNAP or subluxation of the tendon with active supination — the hallmark of subluxation as opposed to simple tendinopathy.
Typical PresentationRacquet-sport athlete or golfer with ulnar-sided wrist pain, sometimes with an audible/palpable snapping sensation during wrist rotation.
Distinguishing MimickersTFCC tear (fovea tenderness rather than the ECU groove, no snapping); DRUJ instability.

Ulnar Impaction (Abutment) Syndrome

MechanismChronic excessive load transmission through the ulna onto the lunate/TFCC, typically related to ulnar-positive variance (a relatively longer ulna), from congenital variance or a prior distal radius fracture that shortened the radius.
Exam FindingsTenderness centered over the ulnolunate/ulnotriquetral region, pain worsened by gripping in ulnar deviation.
Typical PresentationChronic, activity-related ulnar wrist pain, often in a patient with a history of a healed distal radius fracture (which can create relative ulnar overload) or found to have ulnar-positive variance.
Distinguishing MimickersTFCC tear (frequently coexists/overlaps since impaction damages the TFCC centrally).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films assess ulnar variance; MRI shows lunate/TFCC changes.

Hook of Hamate Fracture

MechanismDirect impact of the end of a racquet, bat, or golf club handle against the base of the palm — classic in golf, baseball, and racquet sports.
Exam FindingsDeep, poorly localized tenderness over the hypothenar/hook of hamate region (palpated with the wrist in slight extension), pain gripping; may have ulnar nerve symptoms if Guyon's canal is affected.
Typical PresentationRacquet/bat/club-sport athlete with deep ulnar-palm pain after a specific swing, often with WEAK GRIP and pain holding the equipment — frequently missed on standard wrist x-rays.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple wrist sprain (this fracture is notoriously missed since it's not visible on standard AP/lateral wrist views).
Imaging / WorkupRequires a CARPAL TUNNEL VIEW or CT to visualize — standard wrist series frequently misses this fracture; have a low threshold for CT in an athlete with this specific mechanism and persistent symptoms.

Guyon's Canal Syndrome (Ulnar Nerve Entrapment at the Wrist)

MechanismCompression of the ulnar nerve within Guyon's canal from a ganglion cyst, hook of hamate fracture, repetitive pressure (cyclists — 'handlebar palsy'), or anomalous anatomy.
Exam FindingsSensory change in the small finger and ulnar half of the ring finger WITH SPARING of the dorsal ulnar hand (the dorsal cutaneous branch exits proximal to Guyon's canal, so it is spared — this is THE key distinguishing feature from cubital tunnel syndrome); may have intrinsic hand weakness in motor-predominant compression.
Typical PresentationCyclist, or patient with a palpable mass/history of hamate fracture, with numbness/tingling in the ulnar 1.5 fingers sparing the back of the hand.
Distinguishing MimickersCubital tunnel syndrome (the CRITICAL distinguisher: cubital tunnel compression DOES cause dorsal ulnar hand numbness, since that branch splits off proximal to the elbow; Guyon's canal compression, being more distal, spares it).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS to localize; MRI/ultrasound to look for a mass (ganglion cyst is the most common cause) or hamate fracture.

Pisotriquetral Arthritis

MechanismDegenerative or post-traumatic arthritis of the pisotriquetral joint, aggravated by repetitive FCU loading/gripping.
Exam FindingsTenderness directly over the pisiform, pain with resisted FCU contraction (wrist flexion with ulnar deviation) and with direct pisiform-triquetral shear/compression.
Typical PresentationChronic ulnar-volar wrist pain, worse gripping, sometimes with a history of direct fall onto the heel of the hand.
Distinguishing MimickersTFCC tear (more dorsal-central ulnar location); FCU tendinopathy alone.

Dorsal Wrist 3 conditions

Dorsal Wrist Ganglion Cyst

MechanismMucinous degeneration/herniation from the scapholunate ligament or joint capsule, often idiopathic; may fluctuate in size and occasionally follows minor trauma.
Exam FindingsA distinct, often transilluminating, fluctuant mass on the dorsal wrist, typically most prominent with wrist flexion; usually mobile and non-tender or mildly tender.
Typical PresentationPatient (often young to middle-aged, more common in women) notices a slowly enlarging or fluctuating dorsal wrist bump, sometimes with mild ache, often cosmetically bothersome.
Distinguishing MimickersExtensor tenosynovitis (more diffuse swelling along the tendon rather than a discrete cystic mass); other soft tissue tumors (less common, warrant imaging if atypical features).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis, often confirmed by transillumination; ultrasound confirms cystic nature if uncertain.

Extensor Tenosynovitis

MechanismOveruse (repetitive wrist/finger extension) or inflammatory arthropathy (RA) causing synovial inflammation within the extensor tendon sheaths.
Exam FindingsDiffuse dorsal swelling and tenderness along the extensor tendons (not a discrete cystic mass), pain with resisted finger/wrist extension, possible crepitus.
Typical PresentationRepetitive-use worker or a patient with known/undiagnosed inflammatory arthritis presenting with dorsal wrist swelling and pain with extension activities.
Distinguishing MimickersGanglion cyst (discrete mass vs. this diffuse swelling); early RA flare (check for other joint involvement, morning stiffness).

Kienböck Disease (Lunate Avascular Necrosis)

MechanismDisrupted blood supply to the lunate, associated with ulnar-negative variance (a relatively shorter ulna concentrating load on the lunate), repetitive loading, or prior trauma; often idiopathic.
Exam FindingsCentral dorsal wrist tenderness over the lunate, decreased grip strength, progressive stiffness; may have swelling.
Typical PresentationYoung to middle-aged adult (often a manual laborer) with insidious, progressive central dorsal wrist pain and stiffness over months, sometimes minimized initially as a simple sprain.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple wrist sprain/strain (this diagnosis is often delayed because early x-rays can appear normal); scapholunate ligament injury.
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may be normal early — MRI is far more sensitive for detecting early lunate avascular changes and is warranted for persistent unexplained central wrist pain.

Volar / Central Wrist 5 conditions

Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

MechanismCompression of the median nerve within the carpal tunnel from repetitive wrist flexion/extension, pregnancy-related fluid retention, hypothyroidism, diabetes, or idiopathic tenosynovial thickening.
Exam FindingsSensory change in the median distribution (thumb, index, middle, radial half of ring finger) SPARING the thenar palm (palmar cutaneous branch travels superficial to the retinaculum); thenar atrophy and weak thumb abduction/opposition in advanced/chronic cases; Phalen's and Tinel's are only moderately sensitive/specific.
Typical PresentationNocturnal hand numbness/tingling that wakes the patient (classic), often shaken out for relief ('flick sign'), progressing to daytime symptoms with repetitive gripping and eventually to weakness/clumsiness (dropping objects) in advanced disease.
Distinguishing MimickersPronator teres syndrome (more proximal forearm pain, DOES affect the palm since the palmar cutaneous branch is compressed there); cervical radiculopathy (C6, broader distribution, neck symptoms).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis supported by EMG/NCS, especially before surgical referral or if the picture is atypical.

Flexor Tenosynovitis (Kanavel's Signs)

MechanismBacterial infection (often after a penetrating injury) tracking along the flexor tendon sheath — a surgical emergency.
Exam FindingsKanavel's four signs: (1) fusiform ('sausage') swelling of the digit, (2) held in slight flexion at rest, (3) tenderness along the ENTIRE flexor sheath, (4) pain with PASSIVE extension (the most sensitive/earliest sign).
Typical PresentationRapidly progressive, exquisitely painful, swollen finger after a puncture wound or minor cut, often with fever, and pain grossly out of proportion on passive stretch.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple cellulitis (would not have pain on passive extension along the entire sheath); paronychia (localized to the nail fold, not the whole sheath).
Imaging / WorkupThis is a clinical diagnosis demanding EMERGENT hand surgery consultation and IV antibiotics — do not wait for imaging or labs to delay surgical referral.

Volar Wrist Ganglion Cyst

MechanismSimilar to dorsal ganglion, arising from the volar wrist joints/tendon sheaths (often the radiocarpal joint or the flexor tendon sheath).
Exam FindingsFirm, often small, mobile mass on the volar wrist, may be adjacent to and pulsatile-appearing near the radial artery (must distinguish from a vascular structure/aneurysm).
Typical PresentationSlowly enlarging or static volar wrist mass, sometimes with mild ache or, if compressing the median nerve, mild paresthesias.
Distinguishing MimickersRadial artery aneurysm (pulsatile, requires vascular exam/ultrasound before any attempted aspiration); lipoma.
Imaging / WorkupUltrasound to confirm cystic nature and rule out a vascular lesion before any intervention.

Dupuytren Contracture

MechanismProgressive fibroproliferative disease of the palmar fascia of unclear etiology, strongly associated with Northern European ancestry, family history, diabetes, alcohol use, and smoking.
Exam FindingsPalpable, firm palmar cords/nodules, most often affecting the ring and small fingers, progressing to a fixed flexion contracture at the MCP and/or PIP joints; classically painless.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (usually >50, more common in men) noticing a palmar lump or progressive inability to fully straighten the ring/small finger, generally without pain.
Distinguishing MimickersTrigger finger (catching/locking rather than a fixed contracture, different mechanism); flexor tendon injury (history of trauma, acute).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis based on the characteristic cords and contracture pattern; no imaging typically needed.

Trigger Finger (Stenosing Flexor Tenosynovitis)

MechanismA size mismatch between the flexor tendon (often thickened from a nodule) and the A1 pulley, causing the tendon to catch as it passes through; associated with diabetes, repetitive gripping, and rheumatoid arthritis.
Exam FindingsA palpable nodule at the A1 pulley (distal palmar crease level) that catches/triggers as the finger is flexed and extended, sometimes locking and requiring passive release.
Typical PresentationA finger (often the ring finger or thumb) that catches, clicks, or locks in flexion, sometimes painfully snapping back into extension, worse in the morning.
Distinguishing MimickersDupuytren's (fixed contracture, no triggering/catching); a locked trigger finger can be mistaken for a joint dislocation on a quick exam.

Digits & Fractures 11 conditions

Mallet Finger

MechanismForced flexion of an actively extended DIP joint (classically a ball striking the extended fingertip) avulsing the terminal extensor tendon, with or without a bony avulsion fragment.
Exam FindingsThe DIP joint rests in a flexed posture ("drop") with inability to actively extend it, though passive extension is full.
Typical PresentationSudden inability to straighten the fingertip after a ball/object strikes the extended finger, with a flexed resting posture of the DIP.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple jammed finger/sprain (would retain active extension); a distal phalanx fracture without tendon involvement.
Imaging / WorkupLateral finger x-ray to assess for a bony avulsion fragment and joint subluxation, which affects management (splinting vs. surgical fixation).

Jersey Finger (FDP Avulsion)

MechanismForced hyperextension of an actively flexed DIP joint (classically grabbing an opponent's jersey while flexing the finger, as the jersey pulls away) avulsing the flexor digitorum profundus insertion — the ring finger is most commonly affected.
Exam FindingsInability to actively flex the DIP joint with the PIP held in extension (isolating FDP function); may palpate a tender mass in the palm or along the finger if the tendon has retracted.
Typical PresentationSudden pain and inability to make a fist fully/flex the fingertip after a grabbing injury during a sporting activity (classically football, rugby).
Distinguishing MimickersA simple sprain (would retain DIP flexion); this injury is EASY TO MISS on a cursory exam since the finger can still look and move relatively normally at rest — you must specifically isolate and test FDP function.
Imaging / WorkupThis is a SURGICAL EMERGENCY-ish injury — the tendon retracts and its blood supply is compromised the longer it sits, so timely (within 7–10 days, sooner is better) surgical referral is important; MRI can help localize a retracted tendon stump.

Boutonnière Deformity

MechanismDisruption of the central slip of the extensor tendon at the PIP joint (forced flexion against resistance, a direct blow, or a volar PIP dislocation) allowing the lateral bands to migrate volarly.
Exam FindingsPIP joint held in FLEXION with DIP joint HYPEREXTENSION (the classic deformity); acutely, may just show PIP extensor lag with swelling before the full deformity develops over 1–3 weeks.
Typical PresentationJammed finger injury, sometimes initially dismissed as a simple sprain, that develops the classic flexed-PIP/hyperextended-DIP posture over the following days to weeks as the central slip injury declares itself.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple PIP sprain (the deformity, if it's going to develop, may not be obvious for 1–3 weeks — a high index of suspicion after any 'jammed finger' with volar PIP tenderness is warranted).
Imaging / WorkupElson's test (resisted PIP extension with the PIP flexed 90° over a table edge) can detect an acute central slip injury before the deformity fully develops; x-ray to exclude an avulsion fracture or PIP dislocation.

Swan Neck Deformity

MechanismVolar plate laxity/injury at the PIP allowing hyperextension, often secondary to chronic rheumatoid arthritis, an unrepaired mallet finger (chronic DIP extensor lag causing compensatory PIP hyperextension), or intrinsic muscle tightness.
Exam FindingsPIP joint held in HYPEREXTENSION with DIP joint FLEXION (opposite pattern from boutonnière).
Typical PresentationOften a chronic, progressive deformity in a patient with rheumatoid arthritis or an old, unrepaired mallet finger, rather than an acute presentation.
Distinguishing MimickersBoutonnière deformity (opposite joint posture pattern).

Gamekeeper's / Skier's Thumb (UCL Injury of the Thumb MCP)

MechanismForced radial deviation/hyperabduction of the thumb MCP joint (classic: a ski pole strap forcing the thumb backward during a fall, or any forced abduction injury) tearing the ulnar collateral ligament.
Exam FindingsTenderness and laxity to radial stress (valgus stress) at the thumb MCP joint, tested in BOTH extension AND 30° flexion; a STENER LESION (the adductor aponeurosis interposed between the torn ligament and its insertion, preventing healing) may present as a palpable mass/nodule at the MCP and mandates surgical repair rather than casting.
Typical PresentationFall onto an abducted thumb (skiing, ball sports) with pain and instability at the base of the thumb, weak/painful pinch grip.
Distinguishing MimickersA simple thumb sprain without true ligament laxity; a Stener lesion specifically must be distinguished since it changes management from conservative to surgical.
Imaging / WorkupStress views or MRI/ultrasound to assess for a Stener lesion, which is not reliably palpable and needs imaging to confirm.

Felon (Fingertip Pulp Abscess)

MechanismBacterial infection (often S. aureus) of the closed fingertip pulp space, usually following a minor puncture, splinter, or paronychia extension.
Exam FindingsTense, exquisitely tender, throbbing swelling of the fingertip pulp, often with visible fluctuance; the tight fascial septa of the pulp space create high pressure and risk of vascular compromise/necrosis if untreated.
Typical PresentationRapidly worsening, throbbing fingertip pain, often after a minor injury, with visible swelling and redness confined to the distal pulp.
Distinguishing MimickersParonychia (confined to the nail fold, not the pulp); herpetic whitlow (vesicular, NOT to be incised — an important distinction).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; incision and drainage is definitive treatment, but AVOID incising if herpetic whitlow (vesicular, clear/serous fluid) is suspected instead.

Paronychia

MechanismBacterial (acute, often after minor trauma/nail biting) or fungal/chronic (repeated water exposure) infection of the nail fold.
Exam FindingsErythema, swelling, and tenderness along the lateral or proximal nail fold, may have visible pus.
Typical PresentationPainful, red, swollen area alongside the nail, often after a hangnail, manicure, or nail-biting.
Distinguishing MimickersFelon (involves the pulp, not just the nail fold); herpetic whitlow (vesicular pattern rather than purulent).

Herpetic Whitlow

MechanismHSV inoculation of the fingertip, classically in healthcare/dental workers (exposure to oral secretions) or children (autoinoculation from oral HSV).
Exam FindingsGrouped vesicles on an erythematous base, often with significant pain out of proportion to the visible findings; NOT fluctuant like a bacterial felon.
Typical PresentationPainful vesicular eruption on the fingertip, sometimes with a preceding tingling sensation, in a patient with an oral herpes history or occupational exposure.
Distinguishing MimickersFelon/bacterial infection — CRITICAL to distinguish because incision and drainage of a herpetic whitlow can cause bacterial superinfection and delayed healing and is contraindicated.
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis based on the vesicular morphology; viral PCR/culture if uncertain. Treat with antivirals, NOT incision and drainage.

Boxer's Fracture (5th Metacarpal Neck Fracture)

MechanismAxial load to a clenched fist, classically from punching a hard surface or another person.
Exam FindingsSwelling and tenderness over the 5th metacarpal, loss of the normal knuckle prominence, volar angulation of the fracture apex; ALWAYS inspect for a wound over the knuckle (a 'fight bite' from contact with teeth carries a high infection risk and needs different management).
Typical PresentationPain and swelling over the ulnar hand/knuckle after punching something, sometimes with a small skin wound if the fist contacted teeth.
Distinguishing MimickersA 'fight bite' with an inoculated joint (requires irrigation/antibiotics, sometimes surgical exploration — do NOT simply close the wound).
Imaging / WorkupHand x-rays to assess angulation (up to ~30–40° apex-volar angulation is often accepted for this particular metacarpal given mobility of the 4th/5th CMC joints); explore and irrigate any overlying wound given fight-bite infection risk.

Distal Radius Fracture (Colles' / Smith's)

MechanismColles' (dorsally angulated/displaced): FOOSH with the wrist extended. Smith's (volarly angulated/displaced): fall onto a flexed wrist or a direct blow to the dorsal wrist.
Exam FindingsColles': 'dinner fork' deformity (dorsal displacement). Smith's: 'garden spade' deformity (volar displacement). Both show swelling, deformity, and painful/limited motion; always check median nerve function (acute carpal tunnel can occur with significant displacement).
Typical PresentationFOOSH injury in an older/osteoporotic patient (or high-energy trauma in a younger patient) with an obviously deformed, swollen wrist.
Distinguishing MimickersDistal radius/ulna both-bone fracture; a scaphoid fracture may coexist and be overlooked once the more obvious distal radius fracture is found.
Imaging / WorkupStandard wrist series; assess for intra-articular extension and associated ulnar styloid fracture, both of which affect management; check and document median nerve function.

Perilunate Dislocation

MechanismHigh-energy FOOSH (fall from height, motor vehicle collision) causing progressive perilunate instability across a spectrum (scapholunate injury → perilunate dislocation → lunate dislocation) — a frequently MISSED injury on initial films.
Exam FindingsMarked swelling and deformity, may have median nerve symptoms (acute carpal tunnel from lunate displacement into the canal); the wrist may appear only modestly abnormal, contributing to missed diagnosis.
Typical PresentationHigh-energy trauma with significant wrist pain and swelling, sometimes with a deceptively unremarkable-looking wrist on cursory exam — a notoriously missed diagnosis if the lateral x-ray isn't carefully scrutinized.
Distinguishing MimickersA simple distal radius fracture or wrist sprain — this is exactly the trap; ANY high-energy FOOSH with significant swelling deserves a careful lateral view assessment of carpal alignment.
Imaging / WorkupA true lateral wrist x-ray is essential — look for disruption of the normal 'three C's' collinear alignment of radius-lunate-capitate; CT if plain films are equivocal given how commonly this is missed.

The single highest-yield discriminator in hip pain is LOCATION: groin pain means the joint itself, lateral pain means the trochanteric/abductor region, and buttock pain means posterior structures or the lumbar spine/SI joint. Get the patient to point with one finger before you do anything else.

Anterior Hip / Groin 16 conditions

Femoroacetabular Impingement (FAI) — Cam / Pincer

MechanismAbnormal bony morphology (cam: aspherical femoral head-neck junction; pincer: acetabular overcoverage) causes repetitive abutment between the femoral neck and acetabular rim during hip flexion/internal rotation, leading to labral and cartilage damage over time.
Exam FindingsPositive FADIR test (flexion-adduction-internal rotation reproduces anterior groin pain); often decreased internal rotation in flexion compared to the other side.
Typical PresentationYoung, active adult (often 20s-40s) with insidious anterior groin pain, worse with prolonged sitting, deep flexion (getting out of a low car), or pivoting sports.
Distinguishing MimickersLabral tear (frequently coexists — FAI is the most common cause of labral tears); iliopsoas tendinopathy (more anterior, snapping component); early hip OA (older patient, x-ray changes).
Imaging / WorkupAP pelvis and lateral hip films assess cam/pincer morphology; MRI/MR arthrogram to assess for an associated labral tear.

Acetabular Labral Tear

MechanismMost commonly secondary to FAI (chronic abutment) or a twisting/pivoting traumatic event; can also occur with hip dysplasia.
Exam FindingsPositive FADIR test; may have a mechanical click/catch with certain hip positions; anterior groin pain reproduced with combined flexion-adduction-internal rotation.
Typical PresentationYoung athlete with anterior groin pain and a sense of catching, clicking, or instability, sometimes after a specific pivoting injury but often insidious given the FAI association.
Distinguishing MimickersFAI alone (often coexists, hard to separate clinically); iliopsoas snapping (more of a discrete snap than a catch).
Imaging / WorkupMR arthrogram is more sensitive than standard MRI for labral tears.

Hip Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative cartilage loss, idiopathic or secondary to FAI, dysplasia, prior trauma, or avascular necrosis.
Exam FindingsLOSS OF INTERNAL ROTATION is the earliest and most sensitive finding; groin pain with the log roll test; may have an antalgic gait and flexion contracture in advanced disease.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (50s+) with chronic, progressive groin pain radiating to the anterior thigh/knee (referred pain via the obturator nerve is common and can distract from the hip), worse with weight-bearing and prolonged walking, morning stiffness.
Distinguishing MimickersFAI/labral tear (younger patient, less radiographic OA); referred lumbar radiculopathy (but hip IR loss and groin-specific pain point to the joint).
Imaging / WorkupWeight-bearing AP pelvis and frog-leg lateral views show joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis.

Iliopsoas Tendinopathy / Bursitis

MechanismRepetitive hip flexion (running, kicking sports, cycling) causing tendinopathy of the iliopsoas at its lesser trochanter insertion, with an associated bursa that can become secondarily inflamed.
Exam FindingsTenderness deep in the groin/femoral triangle, pain with resisted hip flexion, positive Thomas test may show tightness; may have an associated snap (internal snapping hip).
Typical PresentationRunner, dancer, or kicking-sport athlete with anterior groin/hip ache worse with active hip flexion (climbing stairs, kicking, sit-ups).
Distinguishing MimickersFAI (more positional/impingement-based pain rather than activity/resisted-flexion pain); athletic pubalgia (more medial, adductor-based).
Imaging / WorkupUltrasound or MRI can show tendinosis/bursal fluid; often a clinical diagnosis.

Internal Snapping Hip (Iliopsoas Tendon Snap)

MechanismThe iliopsoas tendon snaps over the iliopectineal eminence or femoral head as the hip moves from a flexed-abducted-externally-rotated position to extension.
Exam FindingsA reproducible, often palpable/audible snap in the anterior groin/hip with specific hip motion (extending the hip from a flexed, abducted, externally rotated position); patient can often voluntarily reproduce it.
Typical PresentationDancer or athlete reporting a snapping/clunking sensation in the front of the hip with certain movements, sometimes painless and just a nuisance, sometimes progressing to pain with repetition.
Distinguishing MimickersExternal snapping hip (IT band over the greater trochanter — lateral rather than anterior/groin location); labral tear (catching rather than a discrete reproducible snap).
Imaging / WorkupDynamic ultrasound can visualize the tendon snap in real time; usually a clinical diagnosis given the reproducibility.

Athletic Pubalgia ("Sports Hernia")

MechanismA spectrum of injury to the pubic joint/abdominal-adductor aponeurotic complex from repetitive twisting/cutting loads (soccer, hockey), causing attenuation or tearing of the rectus abdominis/adductor longus common origin at the pubis — NOT a true hernia (no bulge).
Exam FindingsTenderness at the pubic tubercle/rectus abdominis insertion, pain with resisted sit-up AND resisted hip adduction (the combination is characteristic); NO palpable hernia bulge (a true inguinal hernia would have one).
Typical PresentationSoccer, hockey, or field-sport athlete with deep groin pain worse with cutting, twisting, and sprinting, often insidious and progressive, classically WORSE with sit-ups/core activation.
Distinguishing MimickersTrue inguinal hernia (palpable bulge, cough impulse); osteitis pubis (more midline symphyseal tenderness); adductor strain (more purely muscular, less core/sit-up provocation).
Imaging / WorkupMRI of the pelvis (dedicated 'sports hernia protocol') shows characteristic aponeurotic injury; dynamic ultrasound/herniography if a true hernia is in question.

Osteitis Pubis

MechanismOveruse/repetitive shear stress across the pubic symphysis from asymmetric loading (kicking sports, running), causing a stress-reaction-like inflammatory process at the symphysis.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the pubic symphysis, pain with resisted adduction and with single-leg stance/hopping.
Typical PresentationRunner or kicking-sport athlete with midline lower abdominal/groin pain centered on the pubic symphysis, worse with running, kicking, and change of direction.
Distinguishing MimickersAthletic pubalgia (frequently coexists, more lateral/rectus insertion); adductor strain (more purely at the adductor origin, less midline symphyseal).
Imaging / WorkupMRI shows symphyseal edema/irregularity; plain films may show chronic symphyseal changes in longstanding cases.

Adductor Strain

MechanismSudden eccentric overload of the adductor muscle group, classic in sports requiring rapid direction change or forceful hip abduction against resistance (soccer, hockey, skating).
Exam FindingsTenderness along the adductor longus origin/muscle belly, pain with resisted adduction, possible palpable defect/ecchymosis in a complete tear.
Typical PresentationAcute, sharp medial groin/thigh pain during a cutting or kicking motion, sometimes with an audible pop in severe tears.
Distinguishing MimickersAthletic pubalgia (more insidious, sit-up provocation); osteitis pubis (more midline, insidious onset).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; MRI/ultrasound for grading if a complete tear or surgical candidate is suspected.

Rectus Femoris Strain / AIIS Avulsion

MechanismForceful eccentric contraction during kicking or sprinting; in skeletally immature athletes, the AIIS (anterior inferior iliac spine, the rectus femoris origin) can avulse rather than the muscle tearing.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the AIIS/proximal rectus femoris, pain with resisted hip flexion with the knee extended, may have swelling/ecchymosis; adolescents may show a bony fragment.
Typical PresentationAdolescent kicking-sport athlete (soccer) with sudden anterior hip pain during a forceful kick, or an adult sprinter with acute anterior thigh/hip pain.
Distinguishing MimickersIliopsoas strain (more medial/deep); hip flexor strain generally.
Imaging / WorkupPlain films in adolescents to assess for an avulsed apophyseal fragment; MRI for muscle-tendon grading in adults.

Femoral Neck Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive submaximal loading exceeding bone remodeling capacity, classic in military recruits and endurance runners, especially with relative energy deficiency (RED-S/female athlete triad risk factors).
Exam FindingsPain with hip flexion and internal rotation, pain with single-leg hop test, may have minimal findings early; TENSION-SIDE (superior) fractures are unstable and high-risk; COMPRESSION-SIDE (inferior) fractures are more stable.
Typical PresentationRunner or military recruit with insidious, activity-related groin/anterior hip pain that progressively worsens and eventually occurs at rest or with weight-bearing.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple hip flexor strain (this is the dangerous mimicker — a missed femoral neck stress fracture can progress to a completed, displaced fracture); athletic pubalgia.
Imaging / WorkupPlain films are often normal early; MRI is the study of choice for suspected stress fracture given sensitivity for early bone marrow edema — have a LOW THRESHOLD to image a runner with persistent groin pain rather than assuming a strain.

Avascular Necrosis of the Femoral Head

MechanismDisrupted femoral head blood supply from corticosteroid use, alcohol use disorder, sickle cell disease, prior hip fracture/dislocation, or idiopathic causes.
Exam FindingsPain with internal rotation, may be relatively preserved ROM early with pain being the dominant early feature before mechanical collapse occurs.
Typical PresentationPatient with a known risk factor (steroids, alcohol, sickle cell) presenting with gradually worsening groin pain, sometimes bilateral.
Distinguishing MimickersHip OA (older, more insidious, no specific risk factor needed); transient osteoporosis of the hip (self-limited, seen in pregnancy/middle-aged men).
Imaging / WorkupMRI is far more sensitive than plain films for early-stage AVN, which can appear entirely normal on x-ray.

Septic Arthritis of the Hip

MechanismHematogenous seeding (most common in children/immunosuppressed), direct inoculation, or contiguous spread.
Exam FindingsThe hip is held in FLEXION, ABDUCTION, and EXTERNAL ROTATION at rest (the position that maximizes capsular volume and minimizes pressure/pain); severe pain with ANY passive motion, especially internal rotation; often febrile.
Typical PresentationFever and severe hip/groin pain with refusal to bear weight or move the hip, classically in a child (consider septic hip in ANY febrile child refusing to walk) but also in at-risk adults (IVDU, immunosuppressed, prosthetic joint).
Distinguishing MimickersTransient synovitis (a key pediatric distinction — the Kocher criteria (fever >38.5°C, non-weight-bearing, ESR>40, WBC>12,000) help differentiate); Legg-Calve-Perthes/SCFE (more insidious, afebrile).
Imaging / WorkupEmergent ultrasound to assess for effusion, followed by arthrocentesis (often under fluoroscopic/ultrasound guidance given joint depth); blood cultures, inflammatory markers; do not delay for imaging if suspicion is high.

Transient Synovitis (Pediatric)

MechanismSelf-limited, presumed post-viral reactive synovitis of the hip; the most common cause of hip pain/limp in children 3–8 years.
Exam FindingsRestricted, painful hip ROM (especially internal rotation) but the child is generally well-appearing, afebrile or low-grade fever, and often CAN bear some weight (unlike septic arthritis).
Typical PresentationYoung child with acute-onset limp or hip/groin/knee pain (referred), often following a recent viral URI, but appearing clinically well.
Distinguishing MimickersSeptic arthritis (this is THE critical distinction — use Kocher criteria and have a low threshold to aspirate if any uncertainty); Legg-Calve-Perthes disease (more insidious onset).
Imaging / WorkupClinical + Kocher criteria; ultrasound may show a small effusion; if any red flags (high fever, inability to bear any weight, elevated inflammatory markers), aspirate to exclude septic arthritis.

Legg-Calvé-Perthes Disease (Pediatric)

MechanismIdiopathic avascular necrosis of the femoral epiphysis in children, typically age 4–8, boys more than girls.
Exam FindingsPainless or mildly painful limp, restricted internal rotation and abduction, may have leg length discrepancy/thigh atrophy in established disease.
Typical PresentationYoung child with an insidious, often painless limp over weeks, sometimes with referred knee pain, no systemic illness.
Distinguishing MimickersTransient synovitis (more acute onset); SCFE (older age group, obese adolescent profile).
Imaging / WorkupAP and frog-leg lateral pelvis x-rays show femoral epiphyseal changes (may be subtle early); MRI is more sensitive for early disease.

Slipped Capital Femoral Epiphysis (SCFE)

MechanismMechanical failure through the proximal femoral physis in an adolescent, strongly associated with obesity and rapid growth; the epiphysis displaces posteriorly and inferiorly relative to the femoral neck.
Exam FindingsPainful, restricted internal rotation; the leg is often held in an externally rotated, shortened position; obligate external rotation with passive hip flexion is a classic finding.
Typical PresentationObese adolescent (typically 10–14) with groin, thigh, OR REFERRED KNEE PAIN (frequently the presenting complaint, leading to a missed diagnosis if the hip isn't examined) and a limp, sometimes after minor trauma but often insidious.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple knee pain of another cause (ALWAYS examine the hip in an adolescent presenting with knee pain and no clear knee pathology — this is a classic missed-diagnosis trap); transient synovitis.
Imaging / WorkupAP and FROG-LEG LATERAL pelvis x-rays (the lateral view is essential and can show slippage missed on AP alone); this is urgent — unstable SCFE risks avascular necrosis and requires prompt orthopedic referral, with the patient made non-weight-bearing immediately.

Inguinal Hernia

MechanismProtrusion of abdominal contents through a weakness in the inguinal canal (indirect: through the internal ring, often congenital; direct: through Hesselbach's triangle, more acquired/degenerative).
Exam FindingsA palpable, often reducible bulge in the groin, accentuated by Valsalva/standing; positive cough impulse.
Typical PresentationGroin bulge/fullness, sometimes with a dragging discomfort, worse with standing/straining, better lying down; acute severe pain with a firm, non-reducible, tender bulge suggests incarceration/strangulation — a surgical emergency.
Distinguishing MimickersAthletic pubalgia (NO palpable bulge — this is the key differentiator); femoral hernia (more inferior/lateral to the pubic tubercle).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound if uncertain; emergent surgical evaluation if signs of incarceration/strangulation (severe pain, non-reducible, overlying skin changes, systemic illness).

Lateral Hip 3 conditions

Greater Trochanteric Pain Syndrome (Gluteal Tendinopathy / Trochanteric Bursitis)

MechanismDegenerative tendinopathy of the gluteus medius/minimus at their greater trochanter insertion (the modern understanding, replacing the older 'bursitis-only' model), from repetitive loading, altered biomechanics, or direct trauma from a fall onto the hip.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the greater trochanter, pain with resisted hip abduction, positive single-leg stance test (pain/Trendelenburg sign after 30 seconds of single-leg standing).
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged to older patient (more common in women) with lateral hip pain, often worse lying on that side at night, worse with prolonged walking or climbing stairs — BY FAR the most common cause of 'lateral hip pain' and frequently mistaken for hip joint (OA) pathology.
Distinguishing MimickersHip OA (that pain is in the GROIN, not laterally — this location distinction is the single most useful discriminator); IT band syndrome (more distal, at the knee, though proximal ITB tightness can contribute to trochanteric irritation); L4-L5 radiculopathy (referred lateral pain, but with other radicular features).
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical; MRI/ultrasound can confirm gluteal tendinopathy/tearing if refractory to conservative treatment.

Gluteus Medius/Minimus Tear

MechanismChronic degenerative tearing (an extension of the tendinopathy spectrum above, especially in older women) or acute traumatic tear from a fall.
Exam FindingsWeakness with resisted abduction, positive Trendelenburg sign (pelvis drops to the contralateral side during single-leg stance, indicating weak abductors on the STANCE side), point tenderness at the trochanter.
Typical PresentationOlder patient with chronic lateral hip pain PLUS a limp/Trendelenburg gait pattern, sometimes called 'rotator cuff tear of the hip' given the analogous tendon degeneration pattern.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple trochanteric bursitis/tendinopathy without a full tear (less weakness, no Trendelenburg); hip OA.
Imaging / WorkupMRI to characterize tear size and plan for possible surgical repair.

External Snapping Hip (IT Band over Greater Trochanter)

MechanismThe IT band or gluteus maximus tendon snaps over the greater trochanter with hip flexion/extension, particularly in patients with a tight ITB or prominent trochanter.
Exam FindingsA palpable/visible/audible snap over the LATERAL hip (over the greater trochanter) with hip flexion-extension, often reproducible by the patient.
Typical PresentationOften young, active patients (dancers, runners) reporting a snapping sensation on the outside of the hip, sometimes painless and merely a nuisance, sometimes progressing to bursitis-type pain with repetition.
Distinguishing MimickersInternal snapping hip (anterior/groin location rather than lateral); loose body (locking rather than snapping).
Imaging / WorkupDynamic ultrasound can visualize the ITB snapping over the trochanter in real time.

Posterior Hip / Buttock 8 conditions

Piriformis Syndrome

MechanismCompression or irritation of the sciatic nerve by the piriformis muscle (from spasm, hypertrophy, or an anomalous nerve course through/around the muscle), often triggered by prolonged sitting or direct trauma to the buttock.
Exam FindingsDeep buttock tenderness over the piriformis, positive FAIR test (flexion-adduction-internal rotation reproduces buttock/posterior thigh pain), possible positive seated piriformis stretch test.
Typical PresentationDeep buttock ache, often radiating down the posterior thigh (can mimic true sciatica), worse with prolonged sitting (a wallet in the back pocket is a classic aggravator) or hip internal rotation/adduction.
Distinguishing MimickersLumbar radiculopathy (the most important distinction — look for back pain, positive SLR, and a dermatomal/myotomal pattern pointing to the spine rather than the buttock); ischiofemoral impingement; hamstring origin tendinopathy.
Imaging / WorkupA diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out lumbar spine pathology (MRI lumbar spine); MR neurography can sometimes show piriformis/sciatic nerve abnormality; diagnostic injection can be both therapeutic and confirmatory.

Deep Gluteal Syndrome (Non-Discogenic Sciatic Nerve Entrapment)

MechanismAn umbrella term broader than piriformis syndrome alone, encompassing sciatic nerve entrapment anywhere in the subgluteal space from fibrous bands, obturator internus/gemellus pathology, or other space-occupying causes — an increasingly recognized, previously underdiagnosed entity.
Exam FindingsButtock pain and/or dysesthesias radiating into the posterior thigh, often with tenderness deep in the buttock and positive seated piriformis-type stretch tests; symptoms often WORSE WITH SITTING.
Typical PresentationChronic buttock/posterior thigh pain, sometimes with radicular-appearing symptoms, that has failed to respond to typical lumbar spine-directed treatment — this is often a diagnosis considered after lumbar pathology has been reasonably excluded.
Distinguishing MimickersLumbar radiculopathy (must be excluded first); piriformis syndrome specifically (deep gluteal syndrome is the broader category); ischiofemoral impingement and hamstring pathology (specific subtypes within this category).
Imaging / WorkupLumbar MRI first to exclude discogenic causes; MR neurography of the sciatic nerve through the subgluteal space; diagnostic injection.

Ischiofemoral Impingement

MechanismNarrowing of the space between the ischial tuberosity and the lesser trochanter, compressing the quadratus femoris muscle, often from an abnormally narrow ischiofemoral space (congenital or acquired, e.g., after proximal hamstring injury/surgery altering anatomy).
Exam FindingsPositive 'ischiofemoral impingement test' (pain reproduced with hip extension and adduction with the knee flexed, relieved by hip flexion/abduction); tenderness deep in the buttock/posterior hip.
Typical PresentationDeep buttock/posterior hip pain, sometimes radiating down the leg mimicking sciatica, often in runners or patients with a history of proximal hamstring injury.
Distinguishing MimickersPiriformis syndrome/deep gluteal syndrome generally (this is one specific cause within that category); proximal hamstring tendinopathy (more distinctly at the ischial tuberosity itself).
Imaging / WorkupMRI showing a narrowed ischiofemoral space and quadratus femoris edema is characteristic.

Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy / Tear

MechanismChronic overload (running, especially sprinting and hill work) causing tendinopathy at the ischial tuberosity origin, or an acute traumatic tear (forceful hip flexion with the knee extended, classic in sprinters and water-skiers) which can be a complete avulsion in severe cases.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness directly over the ischial tuberosity, pain with resisted knee flexion/hip extension, pain with a prolonged/deep hip flexion stretch (e.g., sitting on a hard surface); a palpable gap and significant ecchymosis suggest a complete avulsion.
Typical PresentationRunner with deep 'lower buttock' pain at the sit bone, worse with sitting on hard surfaces and with sprinting, OR an acute, severe posterior thigh pain with a 'pop' during a forceful kicking/sprinting motion (suggesting a tear/avulsion).
Distinguishing MimickersIschial bursitis (more superficial, less activity/resisted-flexion provoked); piriformis syndrome/sciatic irritation (more radiating, less discretely point-tender at the ischial tuberosity); sciatica (hamstring tendinopathy can also cause local sciatic nerve irritation given anatomic proximity, blurring the picture).
Imaging / WorkupMRI to grade tendinopathy vs. partial vs. complete tear — a complete avulsion with significant retraction (>2 cm) generally warrants surgical referral.

Ischial Bursitis ("Weaver's Bottom")

MechanismRepetitive pressure/friction over the ischial tuberosity, classically from prolonged sitting on hard surfaces (cycling, rowing).
Exam FindingsTenderness over the ischial tuberosity, less activity-provoked than proximal hamstring tendinopathy (resisted knee flexion is less specifically painful).
Typical PresentationProlonged sitting/cycling history with 'sit bone' pain, worse with continued sitting.
Distinguishing MimickersProximal hamstring tendinopathy (more resisted-flexion pain).

Sacroiliac (SI) Joint Dysfunction

MechanismMechanical dysfunction or inflammation of the SI joint from asymmetric loading, pregnancy-related ligamentous laxity, trauma, or as part of an inflammatory spondyloarthropathy.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the SI joint/PSIS, positive FABER test with POSTERIOR (buttock) pain specifically (as opposed to groin pain, which localizes to the hip joint instead), positive SI provocation test cluster (thigh thrust, compression, distraction).
Typical PresentationButtock/lower back pain, often unilateral, sometimes radiating into the posterior thigh (but typically not past the knee, unlike true radiculopathy), worse with prolonged sitting/standing or transitional movements.
Distinguishing MimickersLumbar radiculopathy (SI pain typically doesn't go past the knee and lacks a dermatomal/neurologic pattern); hip OA (FABER-positive but with GROIN, not buttock, pain); piriformis syndrome.
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical with a positive provocative test cluster; plain films/MRI if an inflammatory spondyloarthropathy is suspected (check for sacroiliitis, especially in a young patient with inflammatory back pain features).

Referred Sciatica (Lumbar Radiculopathy)

MechanismNerve root compression (disc herniation, foraminal stenosis) most often at L4-L5 or L5-S1, referring pain into the buttock and leg.
Exam FindingsPositive straight leg raise reproducing pain BELOW THE KNEE, dermatomal sensory change, myotomal weakness, diminished reflexes depending on the level involved; low back pain often (though not always) present.
Typical PresentationButtock and posterior leg pain radiating below the knee, often with associated low back pain, paresthesias, or weakness in a specific nerve root distribution.
Distinguishing MimickersPiriformis syndrome/deep gluteal syndrome (pain that stays more confined to the buttock/proximal thigh without a clear dermatomal pattern below the knee); hip joint pathology (groin location, not buttock).
Imaging / WorkupLumbar spine MRI if red flags, progressive weakness, or failure of conservative treatment.

Hip Fracture (Femoral Neck / Intertrochanteric) — Elderly Fall

MechanismGround-level fall in an osteoporotic older adult (low-energy) or high-energy trauma in younger patients; femoral neck fractures risk avascular necrosis given the retrograde blood supply, while intertrochanteric fractures generally do not.
Exam FindingsClassic finding: the affected leg is SHORTENED AND EXTERNALLY ROTATED, with significant pain on any attempted motion and inability to bear weight; groin/hip pain, may have thigh/knee referred pain.
Typical PresentationElderly patient after a fall, unable to bear weight, with the leg shortened and externally rotated — a hip fracture until proven otherwise, EVEN WITH NEGATIVE INITIAL X-RAYS in a patient with a classic mechanism and exam.
Distinguishing MimickersHip contusion/simple fall without fracture (would retain the ability to bear at least some weight and lack the classic shortened/externally rotated posture); occult (non-displaced) fracture with deceptively normal-appearing initial films.
Imaging / WorkupAP pelvis and cross-table lateral hip films; if clinical suspicion remains high with negative x-rays (occult fracture), obtain MRI (or CT if MRI unavailable) — missed occult hip fractures that then displace carry major morbidity.

Nerve Entrapment & Other 3 conditions

Meralgia Paresthetica (Lateral Femoral Cutaneous Nerve Entrapment)

MechanismCompression of the purely sensory lateral femoral cutaneous nerve as it passes under the inguinal ligament near the ASIS, from obesity, pregnancy, tight belts/waistbands, or prolonged sitting.
Exam FindingsSensory change (numbness, tingling, burning) over the anterolateral thigh in a well-demarcated patch, NO motor weakness (this is a purely sensory nerve), often reproduced with hip extension or direct pressure near the ASIS.
Typical PresentationOverweight patient or someone wearing tight belts/tool belts, with burning/numbness over the outer thigh, no weakness, no back pain.
Distinguishing MimickersL2-L3 radiculopathy (would have some motor involvement, e.g., hip flexion weakness, and typically more back pain).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; nerve conduction studies rarely needed; resolves with weight loss/removing the offending compression source in most cases.

Obturator Hernia

MechanismHerniation of abdominal contents through the obturator canal, classically in thin, elderly, multiparous women.
Exam FindingsPositive Howship-Romberg sign (pain along the medial thigh with hip extension/internal rotation from obturator nerve irritation); often minimal external findings since this hernia has no palpable bulge.
Typical PresentationElderly, thin woman with intermittent medial thigh/groin pain and, if incarcerated, signs of bowel obstruction — a rare but classically difficult and delayed diagnosis.
Distinguishing MimickersNothing external to palpate makes this one of the trickiest hernias to diagnose clinically — high suspicion in the classic demographic with obstructive symptoms is key.
Imaging / WorkupCT abdomen/pelvis is the diagnostic study of choice.

Pubic Ramus Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive submaximal loading in runners, military recruits, or postpartum/osteoporotic women, similar risk profile to femoral neck stress fractures.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the pubic ramus, pain with single-leg stance/hopping, pain with resisted hip adduction/flexion.
Typical PresentationRunner or postpartum/osteoporotic patient with insidious groin/pubic pain worsening with impact activity.
Distinguishing MimickersAdductor strain, osteitis pubis — all can present similarly and may need imaging to distinguish.
Imaging / WorkupMRI is most sensitive; plain films often normal early.

Knee pain is the textbook example of location-based differential diagnosis: anterior, medial, lateral, and posterior pain each have their own short list, and pes anserine bursitis vs. medial meniscus tear vs. medial compartment OA is exactly the kind of distinction that location plus a few exam maneuvers resolves quickly.

Anterior Knee 11 conditions

Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (PFPS)

MechanismAbnormal patellar tracking/loading within the trochlear groove from quadriceps (especially VMO) weakness or imbalance, hip abductor weakness altering dynamic limb alignment, or simple overload — the single most common cause of anterior knee pain, especially in young, active women.
Exam FindingsDiffuse peripatellar tenderness (not a discrete point), positive patellar grind/compression test, pain with resisted knee extension through the terminal arc, often a positive 'theater sign' (pain after prolonged sitting with knees flexed).
Typical PresentationYoung, active patient (often a runner or someone who recently increased activity) with diffuse anterior knee ache, worse going down stairs/hills, after prolonged sitting, or with squatting.
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar tendinopathy (more focal at the inferior pole, not diffuse); chondromalacia patella (essentially overlaps/represents a cartilage-specific subset of the same process); plica syndrome (more medial, mechanical snapping).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; plain films (sunrise/Merchant view) if instability or malalignment is suspected; MRI not routinely needed.

Patellar Tendinopathy (Jumper's Knee)

MechanismRepetitive eccentric loading of the patellar tendon during jumping/landing sports (basketball, volleyball), causing tendinosis at the inferior pole of the patella.
Exam FindingsFocal, point tenderness at the INFERIOR POLE of the patella (distinguishing it from the diffuse tenderness of PFPS), pain with resisted knee extension and with a single-leg decline squat.
Typical PresentationJumping-sport athlete with focal pain just below the kneecap, worse with jumping/landing and after activity, classically improving with warm-up then worsening afterward.
Distinguishing MimickersPFPS (diffuse rather than focal tenderness); Sinding-Larsen-Johansson syndrome (the pediatric equivalent, at the inferior pole apophysis in adolescents); patellar tendon rupture (acute, with an extensor lag — far more dramatic).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound/MRI shows tendinosis changes at the inferior pole if imaging is pursued.

Quadriceps Tendinopathy / Rupture

MechanismTendinopathy from chronic overload (jumping athletes); RUPTURE from a sudden eccentric load on a contracting quadriceps (classic: stumbling and catching oneself, or a fall with the knee partially flexed) — rupture is more common in patients OVER 40, often with an underlying tendinopathy, chronic steroid use, or systemic disease (CKD, diabetes) weakening the tendon.
Exam FindingsRupture: palpable suprapatellar gap/defect, inability to actively extend the knee or maintain a straight-leg raise against gravity, patella often sits LOWER than normal (patella baja) due to loss of the proximal pull.
Typical PresentationTendinopathy: activity-related pain at the superior pole in a jumping athlete. Rupture: sudden pain and inability to straighten/bear weight after a stumble or fall, often in a patient over 40 with risk factors.
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar tendon rupture (patella sits HIGH — patella alta — rather than low, the opposite direction, and the palpable gap is at the INFERIOR rather than superior pole); PFPS/patellar tendinopathy (no true extensor lag or palpable gap).
Imaging / WorkupThe straight-leg raise test is the critical bedside screen — inability to perform it after knee trauma demands urgent evaluation for an extensor mechanism rupture; MRI or ultrasound confirms and grades the tear; missed ruptures lead to much worse outcomes, so this is a cannot-miss diagnosis in an older patient who fell and can't extend the knee.

Patellar Tendon Rupture

MechanismSudden forceful eccentric quadriceps contraction on a flexed knee (classic: landing from a jump, or a fall), typically in patients UNDER 40 (as opposed to quadriceps rupture, more common over 40), often with a background of chronic patellar tendinopathy or steroid use.
Exam FindingsPalpable gap at the INFERIOR pole of the patella, patella sits HIGH (patella alta), inability to actively extend the knee or perform a straight-leg raise.
Typical PresentationSudden 'pop' and collapse during a jump or fall, with immediate inability to straighten the leg or bear weight normally, in a younger patient (often with a jumper's-knee history).
Distinguishing MimickersQuadriceps tendon rupture (patella LOW rather than high, gap at the superior rather than inferior pole, older patient population).
Imaging / WorkupSame as quadriceps rupture — straight-leg raise test at the bedside, then MRI/ultrasound confirmation; surgical repair is time-sensitive.

Prepatellar Bursitis ("Housemaid's Knee")

MechanismDirect trauma or repetitive kneeling (carpet layers, gardeners, plumbers — hence the name) causing inflammation/effusion of the prepatellar bursa; can also become septic from an overlying abrasion.
Exam FindingsA distinct, fluctuant, well-circumscribed swelling directly OVER the patella (superficial to it), typically with relatively preserved, only mildly painful knee ROM — unlike a true joint effusion, which is deeper and more diffusely limits motion.
Typical PresentationVisible swelling over the kneecap after kneeling or direct trauma; if red, warm, and associated with fever, raises concern for septic bursitis.
Distinguishing MimickersTrue intra-articular knee effusion (deeper, more globally limits ROM, ballottement test positive); septic bursitis specifically (warmth, fever, more pain — changes management to aspiration and antibiotics).
Imaging / WorkupAspirate if any concern for infection (cell count, gram stain, culture); otherwise largely clinical.

Osgood-Schlatter Disease (Pediatric)

MechanismTraction apophysitis at the tibial tubercle from repetitive quadriceps pull through the patellar tendon during the adolescent growth spurt, when the apophysis is most vulnerable.
Exam FindingsTenderness and often visible prominence/swelling directly over the tibial tubercle, pain with resisted knee extension and with direct pressure over the tubercle.
Typical PresentationAdolescent (typically 10–15, active in jumping/running sports) with anterior knee pain localized to the bump below the kneecap, worse with activity, better with rest.
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar tendinopathy (more proximal, at the tendon rather than the bony tubercle); Sinding-Larsen-Johansson (at the inferior pole of the patella rather than the tibial tubercle).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; lateral knee x-ray can show tibial tubercle fragmentation/irregularity if imaging is obtained, but is often unnecessary.

Sinding-Larsen-Johansson Syndrome (Pediatric)

MechanismTraction apophysitis at the INFERIOR POLE of the patella (as opposed to Osgood-Schlatter's tibial tubercle location), same mechanism of repetitive quadriceps/patellar tendon traction during growth.
Exam FindingsTenderness at the inferior pole of the patella, pain with resisted extension and jumping activities.
Typical PresentationAdolescent athlete with anterior knee pain localized just below the kneecap itself (rather than further down at the tibial tubercle as in Osgood-Schlatter).
Distinguishing MimickersOsgood-Schlatter disease (more distal, at the tibial tubercle); patellar tendinopathy (adult equivalent).

Patellar Instability / Dislocation

MechanismLateral patellar dislocation from a twisting injury with the foot planted and the knee in valgus/external rotation, or recurrent instability in patients with predisposing anatomy (trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, ligamentous laxity); tears the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) with the first dislocation.
Exam FindingsPositive apprehension test (lateral patellar translation reproduces a guarding/apprehensive response); may have a palpable effusion (often hemarthrosis after a first-time dislocation) and medial-sided tenderness over the torn MPFL.
Typical PresentationAdolescent or young adult (often female, with some generalized ligamentous laxity) with a twisting injury, a visible/felt dislocation (sometimes self-reduced by the time of evaluation), and an effusion.
Distinguishing MimickersACL tear (both cause hemarthrosis after a twisting injury — the apprehension test and medial-sided MPFL tenderness point toward patellar instability instead).
Imaging / WorkupMRI to assess the MPFL, check for osteochondral fracture (common after dislocation from the patella/lateral femoral condyle shearing against each other), and assess trochlear morphology for recurrence risk.

Patellar Fracture

MechanismDirect blow to the patella (dashboard injury in a motor vehicle collision is classic) or an indirect forceful quadriceps contraction against a flexed knee.
Exam FindingsTenderness, swelling, and palpable defect/gap over the patella; assess extensor mechanism integrity (straight-leg raise) since displaced fractures disrupt it.
Typical PresentationDirect knee trauma (fall onto the knee, dashboard injury) with anterior knee pain, swelling, and possible inability to extend the knee if displaced.
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar/quadriceps tendon rupture (both can cause extensor lag, but a fracture will show on x-ray).
Imaging / WorkupAP/lateral/sunrise knee views; CT if comminution needs characterization for surgical planning.

Medial Patellar Plica Syndrome

MechanismA congenital synovial fold (medial plica) becomes thickened/fibrotic and mechanically catches or snaps over the medial femoral condyle, often after minor trauma or repetitive flexion-extension activity.
Exam FindingsA palpable, tender band medial to the patella, mechanical snap/catch with knee flexion-extension, tenderness at the medial femoral condyle where the plica rubs.
Typical PresentationYounger, active patient with anteromedial knee pain and a sense of catching/snapping, sometimes after a minor twisting injury or simply from repetitive activity.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial meniscus tear (deeper joint-line tenderness, different provocative tests — McMurray/Thessaly rather than a palpable snapping band); PFPS (more diffuse, no discrete snap).
Imaging / WorkupMRI can show a thickened plical band; often confirmed at arthroscopy.

Hoffa's Syndrome (Infrapatellar Fat Pad Impingement)

MechanismThe infrapatellar (Hoffa's) fat pad becomes impinged and secondarily inflamed/hypertrophied between the femur and tibia during knee extension, often following direct trauma or hyperextension injury.
Exam FindingsTenderness on either side of the patellar tendon (anterior joint line), pain WORSE with full extension/hyperextension (as opposed to most anterior knee pain, which is flexion-provoked).
Typical PresentationHistory of a hyperextension injury or direct blow to the front of the knee, with anterior knee pain specifically worse in terminal extension (opposite the usual pattern of anterior knee pain conditions).
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar tendinopathy (flexion-loading pain rather than extension pain — this reversal is the key distinguishing feature).

Medial Knee 5 conditions

Pes Anserine Bursitis / Tendinopathy

MechanismOveruse or direct irritation of the pes anserine bursa (deep to the conjoined sartorius/gracilis/semitendinosus tendons at their tibial insertion) from repetitive knee flexion-extension (running, cycling), obesity, or coexisting medial compartment OA (which alters mechanics and irritates the bursa).
Exam FindingsTenderness centered ~5 CM BELOW the medial joint line on the anteromedial tibia (this distance is the single most useful discriminator from meniscal pathology, which is tender AT the joint line); no true joint line tenderness, no mechanical locking/catching.
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged or overweight patient, often with coexisting knee OA, or a runner, with medial knee ache below the joint line, worse going up/down stairs and at night when lying with knees touching.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial meniscus tear (tenderness AT the joint line, not 5 cm below it, plus mechanical catching/locking and a positive McMurray/Thessaly — THIS is exactly the distinction from the examples you referenced); medial collateral ligament sprain (tenderness along the ligament course, positive valgus stress).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis based on the tenderness location; ultrasound/MRI if refractory to confirm bursal fluid/tendinopathy and exclude meniscal pathology.

Medial Meniscus Tear

MechanismTwisting injury on a planted, weight-bearing foot (often with the knee flexed) in younger patients, or degenerative tearing from minimal/no trauma in older patients with underlying chondral wear — the medial meniscus is torn far more often than the lateral given its lesser mobility and firmer capsular attachment.
Exam FindingsTenderness directly AT the medial joint line, positive McMurray test (click/pain with combined flexion, external rotation, and valgus stress during extension) and Thessaly test (discomfort/catching with weight-bearing rotation), possible mechanical locking or a sensation of the knee 'giving way.'
Typical PresentationTwisting/pivoting injury with immediate or delayed medial knee pain and swelling, sometimes with mechanical locking or catching, OR an older patient with insidious medial knee pain and no clear trauma (degenerative tear).
Distinguishing MimickersPes anserine bursitis (tenderness clearly below the joint line rather than at it, no mechanical locking); MCL sprain (more diffuse along the ligament, positive valgus stress rather than McMurray); medial compartment OA (more chronic, x-ray changes, less discrete mechanical catching).
Imaging / WorkupMRI is the study of choice for meniscal tear characterization; plain films first to exclude fracture/assess for OA in older patients.

Medial Collateral Ligament (MCL) Sprain

MechanismValgus stress to the knee, either from a direct lateral blow (classic contact sports injury) or a non-contact valgus/twisting load.
Exam FindingsTenderness along the MCL course (from the medial femoral epicondyle to the proximal medial tibia — palpate the WHOLE length to localize the injury level), laxity to valgus stress tested at BOTH 0° and 30° of flexion (laxity at 0°/full extension implies an associated cruciate/capsular injury and a much more serious knee).
Typical PresentationContact or twisting injury with medial-sided knee pain, variable swelling depending on grade, and a sense of instability in more severe (grade III) injuries.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial meniscus tear (joint-line tenderness rather than along the ligament's course, positive McMurray rather than valgus laxity); pes anserine bursitis (more distal, no valgus laxity).
Imaging / WorkupClinical exam grades severity (I: tender, no laxity; II: laxity with a firm endpoint; III: laxity with no endpoint); MRI if a concomitant ACL/meniscal injury is suspected, especially with laxity at full extension.

Medial Compartment Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative cartilage loss in the medial compartment, the most common OA pattern in the knee, often accelerated by varus (bow-legged) alignment which concentrates load medially.
Exam FindingsMedial joint line tenderness, crepitus, varus deformity often visible, restricted terminal flexion/extension, may have a small effusion.
Typical PresentationOlder patient (50s+) with chronic, progressive medial knee pain worse with weight-bearing/walking, morning stiffness lasting under 30 minutes, gradual varus deformity progression over years.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial meniscus tear (can coexist — degenerative meniscal tears are extremely common WITHIN osteoarthritic knees, making the two hard to separate clinically); pes anserine bursitis (frequently COEXISTS with medial OA and can be missed as 'just the arthritis').
Imaging / WorkupWeight-bearing AP knee x-ray (the standing view is essential to show true joint space narrowing) shows medial compartment narrowing, osteophytes.

Saphenous Nerve Entrapment (Adductor Canal)

MechanismCompression of the saphenous nerve (a purely sensory branch of the femoral nerve) as it exits the adductor (Hunter's) canal, from direct trauma, surgery (common after knee arthroscopy/TKA), or fascial entrapment.
Exam FindingsSensory change (burning, numbness) over the medial knee, distal thigh, and medial leg in the saphenous distribution, NO motor weakness; tenderness at the exit point of the adductor canal (mid-distal medial thigh).
Typical PresentationMedial knee/leg burning pain or numbness, sometimes after a knee surgery, running injury, or without clear trauma, with normal knee joint exam.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial meniscus tear or MCL sprain (both would show joint-specific mechanical findings, which this lacks); L3-L4 radiculopathy (would have some motor involvement).
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical with a sensory exam confirming the distribution; nerve blocks can be diagnostic and therapeutic.

Lateral Knee 7 conditions

Iliotibial (IT) Band Syndrome

MechanismRepetitive friction/compression of the distal IT band against the lateral femoral epicondyle during repeated knee flexion-extension (running, especially downhill, and cycling), often related to hip abductor weakness altering dynamic alignment.
Exam FindingsTenderness ~2–3 cm proximal to the lateral joint line over the lateral femoral epicondyle (NOT at the joint line itself — this location distinguishes it from a lateral meniscus tear), positive Noble compression test (pain reproduced with the knee moving through ~30° of flexion while compressing over the epicondyle), often normal joint line exam otherwise.
Typical PresentationRunner (especially increasing mileage or downhill running) or cyclist with lateral knee pain that characteristically WORSENS the longer they run (often pain-free at the start, then a sharp lateral pain after a consistent distance), improving with rest.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral meniscus tear (tenderness AT the joint line rather than proximal to it, mechanical catching, positive McMurray); LCL sprain (more posterolateral, positive varus stress); biceps femoris tendinopathy (more posterior).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis based on tenderness location and the classic 'pain after a consistent distance' running history; MRI/ultrasound if refractory.

Lateral Meniscus Tear

MechanismTwisting injury similar to medial meniscus tears but less common given the lateral meniscus's greater mobility; more frequently associated with ACL tears (given shared injury mechanisms) and with discoid meniscus variants in younger patients.
Exam FindingsTenderness AT the lateral joint line, positive McMurray (testing with internal rotation and varus stress for the lateral side) and Thessaly test, possible mechanical locking/catching.
Typical PresentationTwisting/pivoting injury with lateral knee pain and swelling, sometimes with mechanical catching; consider a discoid meniscus in a child/adolescent with recurrent lateral knee snapping/locking without a clear traumatic cause.
Distinguishing MimickersIT band syndrome (tenderness proximal to the joint line rather than at it, no mechanical catching, activity-pattern differs); LCL sprain (ligament-course tenderness, varus laxity rather than McMurray positivity).
Imaging / WorkupMRI is the study of choice; consider discoid meniscus in a young patient with atypical/recurrent symptoms without trauma.

Lateral Collateral Ligament (LCL) Sprain

MechanismVarus stress to the knee, either a direct medial-to-lateral blow or a non-contact varus/twisting injury; less common in isolation than MCL injury, and when significant, raises concern for posterolateral corner injury.
Exam FindingsTenderness along the LCL course (best palpated in a figure-4 position, where it stands out as a distinct cord from the lateral epicondyle to the fibular head), laxity to varus stress at 0° and 30°; ALWAYS check peroneal nerve function (sensation in the first dorsal web space, EHL strength) given the nerve's proximity to the fibular head.
Typical PresentationContact or twisting injury with lateral-sided pain and, in more severe injuries, a sense of instability; check gait for a subtle varus thrust in chronic/missed injuries.
Distinguishing MimickersIT band syndrome (overuse rather than acute traumatic mechanism, different tenderness location); lateral meniscus tear (joint-line tenderness rather than along the ligament).
Imaging / WorkupMRI if higher-grade injury or any neurovascular concern; always document peroneal nerve function given the anatomic risk.

Biceps Femoris Tendinopathy

MechanismOveruse of the biceps femoris (lateral hamstring) at its fibular head insertion, from repetitive sprinting/cutting activity.
Exam FindingsTenderness at the fibular head/posterolateral knee, pain with resisted knee flexion and external tibial rotation.
Typical PresentationSprinting/cutting-sport athlete with posterolateral knee pain, worse with acceleration and deceleration activities.
Distinguishing MimickersLCL sprain (more discrete ligament tenderness, positive varus stress); common peroneal nerve irritation (paresthesias rather than pure tendinous pain, though anatomically close and can coexist).

Lateral Compartment Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative cartilage loss in the lateral compartment, less common than medial OA, often associated with valgus (knock-kneed) alignment.
Exam FindingsLateral joint line tenderness, crepitus, valgus deformity often visible.
Typical PresentationOlder patient with chronic lateral knee pain and a valgus alignment, similar pattern to medial OA but on the opposite side.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral meniscus tear (can coexist, degenerative tears common within an arthritic compartment).
Imaging / WorkupWeight-bearing knee x-rays show lateral joint space narrowing.

Common Peroneal Nerve Entrapment (at the Fibular Neck)

MechanismCompression of the common peroneal nerve as it wraps superficially around the fibular neck, from prolonged leg crossing, a tight cast/brace, prolonged squatting, significant weight loss (less protective fat padding), or a direct blow/fibular neck fracture.
Exam FindingsFOOT DROP (weak ankle dorsiflexion and toe extension) and sensory change over the dorsum of the foot and lateral leg; NO weakness of ankle plantarflexion or inversion (which would implicate the tibial nerve/a more proximal sciatic lesion instead); Tinel's sign at the fibular neck.
Typical PresentationNew-onset foot drop or lateral leg/dorsal foot numbness, often after prolonged leg crossing, squatting, sitting with legs dangling over a hard edge, significant recent weight loss, or a fibular neck injury.
Distinguishing MimickersL5 radiculopathy (would also affect hip abduction/gluteus medius via the superior gluteal nerve, which peroneal entrapment does not, and typically has back pain); deep peroneal-only entrapment (anterior tarsal tunnel — more distal, sensory limited to the first web space, motor sparing of the peroneals themselves).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS localizes the lesion to the fibular neck; MRI/ultrasound if a structural cause (ganglion cyst, mass) is suspected; plain films if a fibular neck fracture is a possible cause.

Popliteus Tendinopathy

MechanismOveruse of the popliteus, which controls tibial internal rotation and unlocks the knee from extension, classically irritated by downhill running (eccentric control demand).
Exam FindingsTenderness just anterior to the LCL at the popliteus tendon origin (posterolateral knee), pain with resisted tibial internal rotation.
Typical PresentationDownhill runner or hiker with posterolateral knee pain, worse specifically going downhill.
Distinguishing MimickersLCL sprain (more discrete ligamentous tenderness/laxity); lateral meniscus posterior horn tear (deeper joint-line pain).

Posterior Knee 6 conditions

Baker's (Popliteal) Cyst

MechanismFluid distension of the gastrocnemius-semimembranosus bursa, communicating with the joint space, almost always SECONDARY to an underlying intra-articular process (meniscal tear, OA, inflammatory arthritis) that increases joint fluid production.
Exam FindingsA palpable, often fluctuant mass in the popliteal fossa, most prominent with the knee extended and often less prominent/softer in flexion (Foucher's sign); assess for the underlying joint pathology driving it.
Typical PresentationPosterior knee fullness/mass, sometimes with mild discomfort, often noticed incidentally or with activity; a RUPTURED Baker's cyst can mimic a DVT with acute calf pain/swelling ('pseudothrombophlebitis').
Distinguishing MimickersDVT (a ruptured Baker's cyst is a classic DVT mimicker — both cause acute calf swelling/pain; ultrasound distinguishes them and can be done simultaneously); popliteal artery aneurysm (pulsatile, requires vascular imaging before any aspiration).
Imaging / WorkupUltrasound confirms the cystic nature and can simultaneously assess for DVT if the presentation is acute; investigate and treat the UNDERLYING intra-articular pathology (this is the actual driver, not just the cyst itself).

Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) Injury

MechanismA direct blow to the anterior tibia with the knee flexed (classic: a dashboard injury in a motor vehicle collision, or a fall onto a flexed knee with the foot plantarflexed) driving the tibia posteriorly.
Exam FindingsPositive posterior drawer test (most accurate PCL test) and posterior sag sign (observe from the side for posterior tibial droop with the knee at 90° — CHECK THIS FIRST, since a sagging tibia creates a false-positive anterior drawer if missed).
Typical PresentationDashboard-type injury or a fall onto a flexed knee with posterior knee pain and swelling, often less dramatic swelling than an ACL tear and sometimes under-recognized initially.
Distinguishing MimickersACL tear (anterior rather than posterior translation, different mechanism); posterolateral corner injury (frequently coexists with PCL tears — assess varus stability and peroneal nerve function too).
Imaging / WorkupMRI to confirm and assess for associated posterolateral corner or meniscal injury; plain films to exclude an avulsion fracture at the tibial PCL insertion.

Popliteal Artery Entrapment Syndrome

MechanismAn anatomic variant where the popliteal artery is displaced/compressed by an abnormal relationship with the gastrocnemius or popliteus muscle, causing dynamic (activity-related) vascular compromise — typically presents in YOUNG, athletic patients (distinguishing it from atherosclerotic claudication in older patients).
Exam FindingsDiminished or absent distal pulses specifically WITH ACTIVE PLANTARFLEXION/dorsiflexion provocative maneuvers (pulses may be normal at rest), calf pain/cramping with exertion.
Typical PresentationYoung, otherwise healthy athlete with exertional calf/posterior knee pain and cramping that resembles claudication but occurs in a patient far too young for typical atherosclerotic peripheral arterial disease.
Distinguishing MimickersChronic exertional compartment syndrome (also causes exertional calf pain in young athletes — distinguished by compartment pressure testing vs. vascular studies); simple gastrocnemius strain.
Imaging / WorkupAnkle-brachial index with provocative positioning, followed by CT angiography or MR angiography with the foot in active plantarflexion/dorsiflexion to demonstrate dynamic compression.

Deep Vein Thrombosis (Popliteal/Calf)

MechanismVenous stasis, endothelial injury, or hypercoagulability (post-surgical, immobility, malignancy, oral contraceptives, prior DVT) leading to thrombus formation, often in the calf or popliteal veins.
Exam FindingsCalf swelling, warmth, tenderness, sometimes a palpable cord; Homan's sign is neither sensitive nor specific and should not be relied upon; unilateral swelling with a positive Wells score should prompt formal testing.
Typical PresentationUnilateral calf/posterior knee swelling and pain, often with a risk factor (recent surgery, immobilization, long travel, malignancy, hormone therapy) — MUST be considered and excluded in any acute posterior knee/calf pain presentation.
Distinguishing MimickersRuptured Baker's cyst (pseudothrombophlebitis — clinically can look identical; this is exactly why compressive ultrasound, which evaluates both, is the right test); gastrocnemius strain/tear (more discrete traumatic onset, often with a palpable defect and ecchymosis tracking distally).
Imaging / WorkupCompression ultrasound is the study of choice and evaluates for both DVT and a Baker's cyst simultaneously; D-dimer if pretest probability is low.

Popliteal Artery Aneurysm

MechanismDegenerative arterial wall weakening, often in older patients with atherosclerotic risk factors; the popliteal artery is the most common peripheral site for a true aneurysm, and these are BILATERAL in a substantial proportion of cases.
Exam FindingsA pulsatile mass in the popliteal fossa (may be subtle if thrombosed); distal pulses may be diminished if there's associated thromboembolism.
Typical PresentationOften asymptomatic and found incidentally, or presenting with acute limb ischemia symptoms if it thromboses/embolizes, or as a vague posterior knee fullness in an older patient with vascular risk factors.
Distinguishing MimickersBaker's cyst (the pulsatile quality is the key differentiator — never attempt to aspirate a posterior knee mass without first confirming it isn't vascular).
Imaging / WorkupUltrasound confirms the diagnosis and size; given the high rate of bilaterality and coexisting aortic/other peripheral aneurysms, further vascular imaging workup is typically pursued.

Gastrocnemius Strain / Tear ("Tennis Leg")

MechanismSudden forceful push-off with the knee extended and ankle dorsiflexed (classic: a tennis player lunging forward, hence the name), causing a strain or tear at the medial gastrocnemius musculotendinous junction.
Exam FindingsSudden sharp posterior calf/knee pain with a palpable defect and ecchymosis (often tracking distally toward the ankle over subsequent days), positive Thompson test should be NEGATIVE (distinguishing it from an Achilles rupture, which is more distal).
Typical PresentationSudden 'pop' or sharp pain in the back of the calf/knee during a forceful push-off (tennis, sprinting), with immediate difficulty walking and later bruising.
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles tendon rupture (more distal location, positive Thompson test); DVT (no discrete traumatic onset, no palpable defect); popliteal cyst rupture.
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis usually sufficient; ultrasound can confirm and grade the tear and exclude a coexisting DVT.

Intra-Articular & Diffuse 9 conditions

Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Tear

MechanismNon-contact pivoting/deceleration injury (cutting, landing from a jump with the knee in valgus and slight flexion) or a direct blow to the knee; classically associated with an audible/felt 'pop' and rapid hemarthrosis.
Exam FindingsPositive Lachman test (most sensitive), anterior drawer, and pivot shift (most specific but hard to perform acutely); large, tense effusion developing WITHIN 1–2 HOURS of injury (rapid hemarthrosis is characteristic).
Typical PresentationAthlete with a pivoting/landing injury, an audible pop, immediate swelling, and a sense the knee 'gave way'; often unable to continue the activity.
Distinguishing MimickersPatellar dislocation (also causes rapid hemarthrosis after a twisting injury — check for apprehension and medial-sided MPFL tenderness); PCL injury (different mechanism, posterior rather than anterior translation).
Imaging / WorkupMRI confirms and assesses for concomitant meniscal/collateral ligament injury (very common); plain films first to exclude an associated tibial plateau or avulsion fracture.

Meniscal Tear (General)

MechanismSee medial/lateral sections above for compartment-specific details; general principle is a twisting/pivoting mechanism in younger patients vs. degenerative tearing with minimal trauma in older patients.
Exam FindingsJoint line tenderness (medial or lateral depending on which meniscus), positive McMurray and Thessaly tests, possible mechanical locking.
Typical PresentationTwisting injury with joint-line pain and swelling, or insidious onset in an older patient with underlying OA.
Distinguishing MimickersSee medial/lateral knee sections for the specific location-based mimickers.
Imaging / WorkupMRI is the imaging study of choice.

Osteochondritis Dissecans

MechanismFocal disruption of subchondral bone blood supply, most commonly at the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle, in adolescents/young adults, likely related to repetitive microtrauma in a susceptible area.
Exam FindingsVague activity-related knee pain and effusion, positive Wilson's test (pain with internal rotation and extension from ~90°, relieved by external rotation — classic but not highly sensitive), may have mechanical locking if a fragment becomes a loose body.
Typical PresentationAdolescent or young adult athlete with vague, activity-related knee pain, intermittent swelling, and sometimes mechanical catching/locking if the lesion progresses to an unstable fragment.
Distinguishing MimickersPFPS (this is often the default initial diagnosis given the vague activity-related pain — persistent symptoms or an effusion should prompt imaging); loose body from another cause.
Imaging / WorkupPlain films (including a tunnel/notch view) may show the lesion; MRI is essential to assess STABILITY of the fragment, which determines management.

Loose Body

MechanismA fragment of bone/cartilage (from OCD, a fracture, or degenerative OA) breaks free and moves within the joint space.
Exam FindingsIntermittent mechanical LOCKING (a true block to motion, as opposed to a meniscal 'catch') that may resolve with repositioning, sometimes a palpable mobile mass.
Typical PresentationEpisodic true locking of the knee (unable to fully extend/flex at times, then resolving), often with a history of prior knee injury or known OA/OCD.
Distinguishing MimickersMeniscal tear (a bucket-handle meniscal tear can also cause true locking and is a key mimicker requiring MRI to distinguish).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may show a radiopaque loose body; MRI/CT for cartilaginous (radiolucent) loose bodies.

Septic Arthritis of the Knee

MechanismHematogenous seeding, direct inoculation (injection, penetrating trauma), or contiguous spread; risk factors include IVDU, immunosuppression, diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, and prosthetic joints.
Exam FindingsWarmth, large effusion, severe pain with ANY motion (both active and passive) through even a small arc, often systemically ill/febrile.
Typical PresentationAcute, severely painful, hot, swollen knee with global restriction of motion, often with fever, in an at-risk host.
Distinguishing MimickersGout/pseudogout (can look nearly identical — arthrocentesis with crystal analysis AND culture is required since crystals do not exclude coexisting infection); reactive arthritis.
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis is mandatory (cell count, gram stain, culture, crystals); blood cultures and inflammatory markers; do not delay for imaging.

Gout / Pseudogout of the Knee

MechanismMonosodium urate (gout) or calcium pyrophosphate (pseudogout/CPPD) crystal deposition triggering an acute inflammatory synovitis; the knee is a common site for both, especially pseudogout.
Exam FindingsWarmth, effusion, significant pain with motion, may have visible tophi (chronic gout) or associated chondrocalcinosis on imaging (pseudogout).
Typical PresentationAcute, severely painful, swollen, warm knee, sometimes with a history of prior similar episodes or known gout/CPPD, sometimes a first presentation that looks identical to septic arthritis.
Distinguishing MimickersSeptic arthritis (the critical distinction — crystals on aspirate do NOT rule out concurrent infection, so send culture regardless of crystal findings).
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis with crystal analysis under polarized light AND culture; serum uric acid is not reliable acutely (can be normal or low during an acute gout flare).

Rheumatoid Arthritis of the Knee

MechanismAutoimmune synovial inflammation, usually as part of established or new-onset polyarticular RA rather than an isolated knee presentation.
Exam FindingsSymmetric involvement (check other joints — hands, wrists), boggy synovial thickening rather than just a simple effusion, morning stiffness >1 hour.
Typical PresentationKnown RA patient with a knee flare, or new knee swelling as part of a broader polyarticular presentation with morning stiffness and other joint involvement.
Distinguishing MimickersOsteoarthritis (RA has more inflammatory features — prolonged morning stiffness, symmetric small joint involvement, systemic symptoms); septic arthritis (single hot joint in an RA patient still requires exclusion of superimposed infection, since RA patients are often immunosuppressed).
Imaging / WorkupInflammatory markers, RF/anti-CCP, arthrocentesis to exclude infection/crystals especially if monoarticular or acutely worse than baseline.

Traumatic Hemarthrosis

MechanismAny significant intra-articular injury (ACL tear, patellar dislocation, tibial plateau fracture, peripheral meniscal tear with a vascularized zone) bleeding into the joint.
Exam FindingsRapid effusion onset (within 1–2 hours, unlike the slower 12–24 hour effusion typical of an isolated meniscal tear or synovitis), often tense and painful; may see fat globules on aspirate (lipohemarthrosis) suggesting an intra-articular fracture.
Typical PresentationAcute knee injury with rapid, significant swelling — the TIMING of effusion onset is itself diagnostically useful (immediate/rapid = hemarthrosis = think ACL/fracture/dislocation).
Distinguishing MimickersThis is a finding rather than a specific diagnosis — the differential for the underlying cause includes ACL tear, patellar dislocation, peripheral meniscal tear, and occult fracture.
Imaging / WorkupPlain films first (look for lipohemarthrosis suggesting occult fracture even without a visible fracture line); MRI for the soft tissue injury causing the bleed; aspiration can relieve pressure/pain and allow better exam.

Referred Pain from Hip Pathology (Pediatric — SCFE/Perthes)

MechanismThe obturator nerve supplies sensation to both the hip joint and the medial knee, so hip pathology can present as isolated knee pain, particularly in children — SCFE and Legg-Calve-Perthes disease are the classic culprits.
Exam FindingsThe KNEE EXAM IS COMPLETELY NORMAL; the hip exam reveals the true pathology (restricted internal rotation, obligate external rotation with flexion in SCFE).
Typical PresentationA child or adolescent (particularly an obese adolescent for SCFE) presenting with knee pain and a normal knee exam — this should ALWAYS trigger a hip exam.
Distinguishing MimickersAny primary knee pathology — the tell is a normal knee exam despite the complaint, which should redirect the workup to the hip.
Imaging / WorkupAP and frog-leg lateral pelvis/hip x-rays if the knee exam is unremarkable in a child with knee pain, especially an adolescent (SCFE risk) or younger child (Perthes risk).

Ankle/foot pain sorts by lateral, medial, posterior/heel, midfoot, and forefoot location — and two of the most commonly MISSED diagnoses (Maisonneuve fracture, Lisfranc injury) hide inside what looks like a routine ankle sprain.

Lateral Ankle 6 conditions

Lateral Ankle Sprain (ATFL/CFL)

MechanismInversion (plantarflexion + inversion) injury, by far the most common ankle injury; the ATFL fails first (weakest, most commonly injured in isolation), followed by the CFL with increasing force.
Exam FindingsTenderness anteroinferior to the lateral malleolus (ATFL) and/or directly inferior to it (CFL); anterior drawer and talar tilt tests are more reliable 4–5 DAYS after injury once acute swelling/guarding subsides.
Typical PresentationAcute inversion injury (rolling the ankle on uneven ground or landing from a jump) with immediate lateral ankle pain and swelling.
Distinguishing MimickersPeroneal tendon injury/subluxation (more posterolateral, snapping); anterolateral impingement (more chronic, post-sprain); fifth metatarsal fracture (palpate the base separately); Maisonneuve fracture (ALWAYS palpate the proximal fibula).
Imaging / WorkupApply the Ottawa Ankle Rules to determine imaging need; if positive for imaging criteria, standard ankle series; re-examine ligamentous stability at 5 days rather than acutely.

Chronic Lateral Ankle Instability

MechanismIncomplete healing or repetitive re-injury following an initial lateral ankle sprain, leading to mechanical (true ligamentous laxity) and/or functional (proprioceptive deficit) instability.
Exam FindingsRecurrent giving-way episodes, positive anterior drawer/talar tilt compared to the contralateral side, may have chronic mild swelling.
Typical PresentationHistory of multiple prior ankle sprains with ongoing episodes of the ankle 'giving way,' especially on uneven surfaces, sometimes with chronic low-grade pain/swelling.
Distinguishing MimickersPeroneal tendon subluxation (also causes a sense of instability, but with a discrete snapping sensation); sinus tarsi syndrome (more of a deep ache than frank instability).
Imaging / WorkupStress radiographs or MRI to assess ligament integrity if surgical stabilization is being considered.

Peroneal Tendinopathy / Subluxation

MechanismTendinopathy from overuse/chronic ankle instability; SUBLUXATION from an acute forceful dorsiflexion-eversion injury tearing the superior peroneal retinaculum, allowing the tendons to sublux anteriorly over the fibula.
Exam FindingsTendinopathy: tenderness posterior to the lateral malleolus, pain with resisted eversion. Subluxation: a palpable/visible SNAP of the tendons over the fibula with active eversion/circumduction — the hallmark distinguishing subluxation from simple tendinopathy.
Typical PresentationChronic lateral ankle pain in someone with a history of instability (tendinopathy), or an acute snap/pop with a forced dorsiflexion injury (subluxation), sometimes misdiagnosed initially as a simple sprain.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple lateral ankle sprain (no snapping component); CFL injury (deeper, no dynamic tendon snap).
Imaging / WorkupDynamic ultrasound can show the subluxation in real time; MRI assesses the retinaculum and tendon integrity.

Os Trigonum Syndrome / Posterior Ankle Impingement

MechanismAn accessory ossicle (os trigonum) or prominent Stieda's process becomes compressed between the posterior talus and calcaneus during forced plantarflexion (ballet, kicking sports), or develops from chronic repetitive microtrauma.
Exam FindingsTenderness deep in the posterolateral ankle, pain reproduced with forced passive plantarflexion, often coexisting FHL tenosynovitis (pain with resisted great toe flexion).
Typical PresentationDancer (classic: relevé/pointe work) or kicking-sport athlete with posterior ankle pain specifically with forced plantarflexion, sometimes after a discrete hyperplantarflexion injury.
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles tendinopathy (more midline/posterior rather than deep posterolateral, different provocative position); sinus tarsi syndrome (more lateral, sinus tarsi-specific tenderness).
Imaging / WorkupLateral ankle x-ray may show the os trigonum (present in ~7–14% of people, so its presence alone doesn't confirm symptomatic impingement); MRI to confirm edema/inflammation at the ossicle and assess for FHL tenosynovitis.

Anterolateral Ankle Impingement

MechanismPost-traumatic synovitis/fibrosis (following an incompletely healed ankle sprain) or a bony spur causing soft tissue impingement in the anterolateral ankle gutter during dorsiflexion.
Exam FindingsTenderness in the anterolateral ankle gutter, pain reproduced with forced dorsiflexion, often a history of a prior sprain that never fully resolved.
Typical PresentationPersistent anterolateral ankle pain months after a sprain that was supposed to have healed, worse with forced dorsiflexion activities (squatting, cutting).
Distinguishing MimickersChronic lateral instability (more giving-way than pain-with-dorsiflexion); sinus tarsi syndrome.
Imaging / WorkupMRI can show synovitis/scar tissue in the anterolateral gutter.

Sinus Tarsi Syndrome

MechanismInflammation/scarring within the sinus tarsi (a small canal between the talus and calcaneus containing ligaments and proprioceptive nerve endings) usually following an inversion ankle sprain.
Exam FindingsTenderness directly over the sinus tarsi (anterolateral hindfoot, just anterior to the lateral malleolus and slightly inferior), a sense of hindfoot instability/'giving way' on uneven ground.
Typical PresentationPersistent lateral hindfoot pain and a subjective sense of instability following an ankle sprain, sometimes long after the original ligaments seem to have healed.
Distinguishing MimickersChronic lateral ligamentous instability (overlaps significantly — often coexists); subtalar joint arthritis.
Imaging / WorkupMRI can show sinus tarsi fat obliteration/inflammation; diagnostic injection into the sinus tarsi can help confirm.

Medial Ankle 4 conditions

Deltoid Ligament Sprain

MechanismEversion injury (much less common than inversion, given the deltoid's inherent strength) or occurring as part of a more severe combined injury (Maisonneuve, high ankle sprain) rather than in isolation.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the deltoid ligament (medial malleolus to talus/navicular/calcaneus), pain with eversion stress.
Typical PresentationEversion mechanism with medial ankle pain — ALWAYS treat medial ankle tenderness after an ankle injury as a signal to check the syndesmosis and proximal fibula, since isolated deltoid injuries are uncommon and often signal a more complex injury pattern.
Distinguishing MimickersMedial malleolus fracture (bony tenderness, positive imaging); Maisonneuve fracture (the deltoid injury is often part of this pattern — palpate the proximal fibula in every ankle injury with medial tenderness).
Imaging / WorkupStandard ankle series; palpate the ENTIRE fibula given the Maisonneuve association; consider syndesmosis stress views if instability is suspected.

Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (Adult-Acquired Flatfoot)

MechanismProgressive degenerative tendinopathy/tearing of the posterior tibial tendon, most common in middle-aged, overweight women, leading to progressive collapse of the medial longitudinal arch over time.
Exam FindingsTenderness along the posterior tibial tendon course (behind the medial malleolus), progressive flatfoot deformity, positive 'too many toes' sign (viewed from behind, more toes visible laterally on the affected side due to forefoot abduction), inability to perform a single-leg heel raise or failure to invert the heel with a heel raise (in advanced disease).
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged, overweight patient with insidious medial ankle/arch pain and a progressively flattening arch over months to years, difficulty with prolonged standing/walking.
Distinguishing MimickersTarsal tunnel syndrome (more distinctly neuropathic — burning/tingling rather than pure tendinous pain, though can coexist as the tendon dysfunction alters mechanics); medial ankle sprain (acute rather than insidious).
Imaging / WorkupWeight-bearing foot x-rays to assess deformity; MRI to grade tendon pathology and guide staging/treatment.

Tarsal Tunnel Syndrome

MechanismCompression of the posterior tibial nerve (or one of its branches) within the tarsal tunnel behind the medial malleolus, from a space-occupying lesion (ganglion, varicosities), a flatfoot deformity altering mechanics, or idiopathic causes.
Exam FindingsPositive Tinel's sign over the tarsal tunnel (behind and below the medial malleolus), burning/tingling in the plantar foot (medial and/or lateral plantar nerve distribution), symptoms often worse at night or with prolonged standing.
Typical PresentationBurning, tingling, or numbness in the sole of the foot, often worse at night, sometimes in a patient with an underlying flatfoot deformity or a palpable mass near the tarsal tunnel.
Distinguishing MimickersPlantar fasciitis (more mechanical/sharp first-step pain rather than burning/tingling, no positive Tinel's); posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (more purely mechanical/tendinous, though flatfoot from PTTD can secondarily cause tarsal tunnel compression).
Imaging / WorkupEMG/NCS can support the diagnosis though false negatives occur; MRI/ultrasound to look for a space-occupying cause.

Medial Malleolus Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive submaximal loading in runners/jumping athletes.
Exam FindingsFocal tenderness over the medial malleolus, pain with hopping/impact loading, may have minimal swelling.
Typical PresentationRunner with insidious medial ankle pain worsening with continued training, without a clear traumatic sprain history.
Distinguishing MimickersDeltoid ligament sprain (would have a clear traumatic mechanism, this doesn't).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films often initially normal; MRI is more sensitive for early stress reaction/fracture.

Posterior Ankle & Heel 8 conditions

Achilles Tendinopathy (Non-Insertional / Insertional)

MechanismRepetitive overload from running/jumping (non-insertional, typically 2–6 cm above the insertion, in a relatively hypovascular zone) or degenerative change at the insertion itself (insertional, often with an associated Haglund's deformity), causing tendinosis rather than true inflammation.
Exam FindingsFusiform thickening and tenderness of the tendon (non-insertional) or focal tenderness right at the calcaneal insertion (insertional), pain with resisted plantarflexion and with hopping; Thompson test NEGATIVE (preserved plantarflexion, distinguishing tendinopathy from rupture).
Typical PresentationRunner or recreational athlete with gradual-onset posterior ankle/heel pain, classically worse with the first steps after rest (morning or after sitting) and during push-off activities.
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles rupture (positive Thompson test, palpable gap — the critical distinction); retrocalcaneal bursitis (more anterior to the tendon, at the bursa specifically); os trigonum/posterior impingement (deeper, more plantarflexion-position-specific).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound/MRI shows tendinosis changes and can grade partial tearing if the picture is unclear.

Achilles Tendon Rupture

MechanismSudden forceful push-off or dorsiflexion of a plantarflexed ankle (classic: a middle-aged 'weekend warrior' pushing off during a sudden sprint or jump in a recreational sport, or landing awkwardly) — often on a background of pre-existing tendinopathy, and NOTABLY more common with fluoroquinolone antibiotic use.
Exam FindingsPalpable gap in the tendon (2–6 cm above the insertion), positive Thompson test (squeezing the calf produces NO passive plantarflexion), weak/absent active plantarflexion though the patient may still be able to weakly plantarflex using the FHL/FDL/peroneals — this partial function does NOT exclude rupture.
Typical PresentationSudden 'pop' or sensation of being struck in the back of the ankle during push-off/jumping, immediate difficulty walking, often in a middle-aged recreational athlete.
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles tendinopathy/partial tear (Thompson test remains at least partially positive/preserved, no complete palpable gap); gastrocnemius strain (more proximal, at the musculotendinous junction in the calf rather than the tendon itself).
Imaging / WorkupThis is a classic MISSED diagnosis in the ED if the patient's residual weak plantarflexion is mistaken for an intact tendon — always palpate for a gap and perform Thompson's test explicitly; ultrasound/MRI confirms if exam is equivocal.

Retrocalcaneal Bursitis

MechanismInflammation of the bursa between the Achilles tendon and the calcaneus, often associated with a Haglund's deformity (a bony prominence) rubbing against shoe heel counters, or inflammatory arthropathy.
Exam FindingsTenderness and swelling ANTERIOR to the Achilles tendon at the level of the bursa (squeezing from both sides, medial and lateral to the tendon, reproduces pain — distinguishing it from tendinopathy which is tender directly on the tendon itself).
Typical PresentationPosterior heel pain and swelling, often related to shoe wear (rigid heel counters), sometimes with a visible bony prominence (Haglund's 'pump bump').
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles tendinopathy (tender on the tendon itself, not anterior to it in the bursa); os trigonum syndrome (deeper, more plantarflexion-provoked).
Imaging / WorkupLateral foot x-ray can show a Haglund's deformity; ultrasound/MRI confirms bursal fluid.

Haglund's Deformity

MechanismA bony enlargement of the posterosuperior calcaneus, often congenital/structural, that mechanically irritates the retrocalcaneal bursa and Achilles tendon against shoe heel counters.
Exam FindingsA palpable, sometimes visible bony prominence at the posterior heel, tenderness over the prominence and adjacent bursa.
Typical PresentationChronic posterior heel discomfort and a visible/palpable 'pump bump,' worse with certain shoes (especially rigid-backed shoes/heels).
Distinguishing MimickersIsolated retrocalcaneal bursitis (frequently coexists as the secondary inflammatory response to the bony prominence).
Imaging / WorkupLateral foot x-ray demonstrates the bony prominence and can measure the relevant angles.

Sever's Disease (Calcaneal Apophysitis — Pediatric)

MechanismTraction apophysitis at the calcaneal growth plate from repetitive Achilles/plantar fascia pull during the growth spurt, analogous to Osgood-Schlatter at the knee.
Exam FindingsTenderness with medial-lateral compression (squeeze) of the calcaneus, pain with resisted plantarflexion, often bilateral.
Typical PresentationActive child/early adolescent (typically 8–12) with heel pain worse after running/jumping sports, often bilateral, no history of acute trauma.
Distinguishing MimickersAchilles tendinopathy (adult equivalent, different age group); stress fracture of the calcaneus (more focal, consider if pain persists/worsens despite rest).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; x-rays not typically needed and calcaneal apophyseal fragmentation on imaging is often a normal variant, not diagnostic.

Plantar Fasciitis

MechanismChronic overload/microtearing at the plantar fascia origin on the medial calcaneal tubercle, associated with obesity, prolonged standing occupations, tight gastrocnemius/Achilles, and pes planus or pes cavus.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness at the medial calcaneal tubercle (plantar fascia origin), pain with passive dorsiflexion of the toes (windlass test) stretching the fascia.
Typical PresentationClassic FIRST-STEP PAIN in the morning or after prolonged sitting, improving somewhat with continued walking then worsening again later in the day, insidious onset.
Distinguishing MimickersCalcaneal stress fracture (more diffuse, positive squeeze test, worsens progressively with activity rather than the classic first-step pattern); tarsal tunnel syndrome (burning/tingling rather than the sharp mechanical first-step pain, positive Tinel's).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis; ultrasound/MRI if refractory shows fascial thickening; x-ray may show a heel spur, though this is often an incidental finding unrelated to symptoms.

Calcaneal Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive impact loading exceeding bone remodeling capacity, common in runners and military recruits, especially with a sudden increase in training volume.
Exam FindingsPositive squeeze/compression test of the calcaneus (medial-lateral compression reproduces pain, unlike plantar fasciitis which is tender at a specific point rather than diffusely compressible), diffuse heel tenderness.
Typical PresentationRunner or military recruit with progressively worsening heel pain that, unlike plantar fasciitis, tends to WORSEN throughout activity rather than easing after the first few steps.
Distinguishing MimickersPlantar fasciitis (the classic first-step-then-improves pattern vs. this progressively-worsens-with-activity pattern is the key distinguisher; also the squeeze test is specific to stress fracture).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films often normal for 2–4 weeks; MRI is the most sensitive study for early stress fracture/stress reaction.

Plantar Fascia Rupture

MechanismSudden forceful loading of the plantar fascia, sometimes in a patient with a history of chronic plantar fasciitis or prior corticosteroid injection (which weakens the fascia).
Exam FindingsAcute sharp plantar pain often with a palpable/visible gap or defect, sudden bruising along the sole, marked local tenderness.
Typical PresentationSudden 'pop' or tearing sensation in the arch during a forceful push-off, often in someone with pre-existing chronic plantar fasciitis (especially if previously treated with steroid injections).
Distinguishing MimickersSimple plantar fasciitis flare (this is far more acute/dramatic in onset, with a discrete traumatic moment).

Midfoot 5 conditions

Lisfranc Injury (Tarsometatarsal Fracture-Dislocation)

MechanismAxial load through a plantarflexed foot (a classic mechanism: a fall with the foot caught in a stirrup/pedal, or an axial load through a plantarflexed forefoot in sports) disrupting the tarsometatarsal joint complex; can range from a subtle ligamentous sprain to a frank fracture-dislocation.
Exam FindingsMidfoot swelling and tenderness, PLANTAR ECCHYMOSIS is nearly pathognomonic when present, pain with passive pronation-abduction of the forefoot, inability to bear weight/pain with attempted single-leg heel raise.
Typical PresentationTwisting or axial-load injury to the midfoot with swelling and difficulty bearing weight; this is a COMMONLY MISSED injury on initial non-weight-bearing films, which frequently appear deceptively normal.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple midfoot sprain (the plantar ecchymosis and mechanism should raise suspicion and prompt more specific imaging rather than accepting a 'sprain' diagnosis)
Imaging / WorkupStandard non-weight-bearing films are frequently NORMAL — obtain WEIGHT-BEARING or bilateral comparison views, or a CT, if suspicion is high based on mechanism and exam; missed Lisfranc injuries lead to significant long-term disability, so a low threshold for advanced imaging or orthopedic referral is warranted.

Midfoot Osteoarthritis

MechanismDegenerative change at the tarsometatarsal joints, often post-traumatic (following a prior, sometimes subtle, Lisfranc-spectrum injury) or from cavus foot mechanics.
Exam FindingsDorsal midfoot tenderness and often a visible dorsal osteophyte/bump, pain with midfoot motion, worse with push-off activities.
Typical PresentationChronic, progressive dorsal midfoot pain and stiffness, sometimes with a history of a remote foot injury.
Distinguishing MimickersExtensor tendinopathy (more superficial, along the tendon rather than joint-based).

Navicular Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive loading in running/jumping athletes; the navicular has a watershed blood supply centrally, making this a HIGH-RISK stress fracture prone to delayed union/nonunion if missed.
Exam FindingsThe 'N-spot' — focal tenderness directly over the navicular on the dorsomedial midfoot, often the only exam finding.
Typical PresentationRunner or jumping-sport athlete with vague, poorly localized midfoot/dorsal foot pain that is easy to dismiss as a simple strain, gradually worsening with continued training.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple midfoot strain/tendinopathy (this is exactly the danger — a high-risk stress fracture masquerading as a benign overuse strain).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films are often normal; MRI or CT is warranted given the high-risk nature of this fracture and the poor sensitivity of plain films — have a low threshold to image a runner with focal navicular tenderness.

Cuboid Syndrome

MechanismSubtle subluxation/dysfunction of the calcaneocuboid joint, often following an inversion ankle sprain or repetitive lateral foot loading (dancers, dancers en pointe).
Exam FindingsTenderness over the cuboid (lateral midfoot), pain with passive forefoot adduction/supination, sometimes a palpable 'step-off' or asymmetry.
Typical PresentationLateral midfoot pain following an inversion injury that doesn't fit the typical pattern of a lateral ligament sprain, sometimes in a dancer.
Distinguishing MimickersLateral ankle sprain (more proximal, at the ligaments rather than the midfoot); peroneal tendinopathy.

Tarsal Coalition

MechanismA congenital fibrous, cartilaginous, or bony bridge between two tarsal bones (most commonly calcaneonavicular or talocalcaneal), restricting normal subtalar/midfoot motion; often becomes symptomatic in adolescence as the coalition ossifies.
Exam FindingsRigid, painful flatfoot with limited subtalar motion, peroneal muscle spasm may be present (a protective response, sometimes called 'peroneal spastic flatfoot'), recurrent ankle sprains from the altered mechanics.
Typical PresentationAdolescent with a rigid, painful flatfoot and recurrent 'ankle sprains,' often with a family history, symptoms typically starting as the coalition ossifies (early-mid teens).
Distinguishing MimickersSimple flexible flatfoot (painless, flexible, no restricted motion — this is the key distinguisher); recurrent simple ankle sprains (the underlying rigid mechanics driving 'recurrent sprains' is the tell).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may show the coalition (a calcaneonavicular bar is often visible on an oblique view); CT is the best study to fully characterize a suspected coalition.

Forefoot & Toes 11 conditions

Morton's Neuroma

MechanismPerineural fibrosis of an interdigital plantar nerve, most commonly the third web space (between the 3rd and 4th metatarsal heads), from chronic compression/irritation, often related to tight/narrow toe-box footwear or high heels.
Exam FindingsTenderness in the affected web space, positive Mulder's click (a palpable/audible click with mediolateral forefoot compression while palpating the web space dorsally and plantarly), may have numbness in the affected toes.
Typical PresentationBurning, tingling, or a sensation of 'a pebble in my shoe' in the ball of the foot, radiating into the adjacent toes, worse with tight shoes/high heels.
Distinguishing MimickersMetatarsalgia (more diffuse forefoot pain without the specific web-space neural symptoms/Mulder's click); stress fracture of the metatarsal (more bony, point-tender on the shaft rather than in the web space).
Imaging / WorkupClinical diagnosis with a positive Mulder's click; ultrasound or MRI can confirm if needed, particularly before considering injection or surgery.

Metatarsalgia

MechanismGeneral overload/inflammation of the metatarsal heads and surrounding structures from abnormal forefoot loading (high heels, pes cavus, prior forefoot surgery, or simply increased activity).
Exam FindingsDiffuse tenderness across the metatarsal heads (plantar forefoot), worse with direct palpation and weight-bearing, no discrete web-space neural findings.
Typical PresentationDiffuse ball-of-foot pain, worse with walking/standing, especially in tight or high-heeled shoes.
Distinguishing MimickersMorton's neuroma (more specific web-space/neural symptoms with Mulder's click); metatarsal stress fracture (more focal bony tenderness).

Metatarsal Stress Fracture

MechanismRepetitive loading, most commonly affecting the SECOND metatarsal (the longest and most rigidly fixed, bearing disproportionate load), common in runners and military recruits ("march fracture").
Exam FindingsFocal point tenderness over the affected metatarsal shaft, swelling, pain with weight-bearing/hopping.
Typical PresentationRunner or military recruit with insidious forefoot pain worsening with continued training, sometimes with visible swelling over the shaft.
Distinguishing MimickersMetatarsalgia (more diffuse rather than focal on a single shaft); Morton's neuroma (web-space rather than bony shaft tenderness).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films may be normal for 2–3 weeks; MRI or bone scan for earlier detection if clinical suspicion is high.

Fifth Metatarsal Avulsion Fracture (Pseudo-Jones / Dancer's Fracture)

MechanismSudden inversion injury avulsing the base of the 5th metatarsal via the peroneus brevis tendon/lateral band pull, often occurring alongside a lateral ankle sprain.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness at the very base/tip of the 5th metatarsal styloid.
Typical PresentationInversion ankle injury with additional lateral midfoot pain at the base of the 5th metatarsal.
Distinguishing MimickersJones fracture (more distal, at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction — a critical distinction since the two have very different healing potential and treatment).
Imaging / WorkupStandard foot x-rays clearly distinguish the location; this avulsion type generally heals well with a walking boot/hard-soled shoe, unlike a true Jones fracture.

Jones Fracture

MechanismAdduction force on the forefoot with the ankle plantarflexed, fracturing the proximal diaphysis of the 5th metatarsal at the metaphyseal-diaphyseal junction — a watershed vascular zone.
Exam FindingsPoint tenderness at the proximal 5th metatarsal shaft, roughly 1.5–2 cm distal to the styloid tip (more distal than the pseudo-Jones avulsion).
Typical PresentationAthlete (basketball, football — push-off/cutting sports) with lateral midfoot pain, sometimes after an inversion-type injury but distinctly located more distally than a simple avulsion.
Distinguishing MimickersPseudo-Jones/avulsion fracture (more proximal, at the very base/tip — heals reliably with conservative care, unlike this fracture).
Imaging / WorkupStandard foot x-rays localize the fracture precisely; given the poor blood supply and high nonunion rate at this location, MANY Jones fractures (especially in athletes) are treated with early surgical fixation rather than casting alone — orthopedic referral is appropriate.

Turf Toe (First MTP Sprain)

MechanismHyperextension injury to the first metatarsophalangeal joint, classically occurring on artificial turf (hence the name) when the forefoot is fixed and the heel rises, forcing the big toe into extreme dorsiflexion.
Exam FindingsTenderness and swelling over the plantar first MTP joint, pain with passive dorsiflexion of the great toe, may have instability in severe (grade III) injuries.
Typical PresentationAthlete (football, especially on artificial turf) with acute great toe pain and swelling after a forced hyperextension injury, difficulty push-off during running/cutting.
Distinguishing MimickersSesamoiditis (more focal to the sesamoid bones plantar to the first metatarsal head, less of a discrete hyperextension mechanism); gout (first MTP is the classic gout location — but typically without a clear traumatic mechanism and with more redness/warmth).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films to exclude a sesamoid fracture or significant joint subluxation; MRI for grading in higher-level athletes given return-to-play implications.

Hallux Rigidus

MechanismDegenerative osteoarthritis of the first MTP joint, causing progressive loss of dorsiflexion, often related to a long first metatarsal, prior trauma, or repetitive stress.
Exam FindingsDorsal osteophyte often palpable at the first MTP, pain and crepitus at the extremes of motion (especially dorsiflexion), progressively restricted dorsiflexion.
Typical PresentationMiddle-aged or older patient with progressive great toe stiffness and pain, worse with push-off during walking, often with a visible dorsal bump.
Distinguishing MimickersTurf toe (acute traumatic onset rather than this insidious degenerative course); gout (acute, red, warm, episodic rather than chronic progressive stiffness).
Imaging / WorkupWeight-bearing foot x-rays show joint space narrowing and dorsal osteophytes.

Hallux Valgus (Bunion) with Bursitis

MechanismProgressive medial deviation of the first metatarsal with lateral deviation of the great toe, related to footwear (narrow toe boxes, high heels), genetics, and ligamentous laxity; the resulting bony prominence can develop a secondarily inflamed bursa.
Exam FindingsVisible medial prominence at the first MTP with lateral deviation of the great toe, tenderness and sometimes overlying erythema/swelling if the bursa is acutely inflamed.
Typical PresentationProgressive bunion deformity, often with acute flares of pain/redness over the prominence related to shoe irritation.
Distinguishing MimickersGout (acute flare can mimic an inflamed bunion, though gout is typically more diffusely red/hot and lacks the chronic structural deformity).

Sesamoiditis

MechanismOveruse/repetitive loading of the sesamoid bones beneath the first metatarsal head, common in runners, dancers, and those wearing high heels (increased forefoot loading).
Exam FindingsFocal plantar tenderness directly beneath the first metatarsal head (over the sesamoids), pain with passive dorsiflexion of the great toe and with direct weight-bearing on the forefoot.
Typical PresentationRunner or dancer with focal plantar pain under the base of the great toe, worse with push-off and forefoot-loading activities.
Distinguishing MimickersTurf toe (more of an acute hyperextension mechanism); sesamoid stress fracture (would show on imaging; clinically can be very similar).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films (including sesamoid axial views) to assess for fracture or bipartite sesamoid variants.

Gout (First MTP — Podagra)

MechanismMonosodium urate crystal deposition, with the first MTP joint being the single most classic site (podagra), related to hyperuricemia from underexcretion or overproduction, often triggered by alcohol, purine-rich foods, or dehydration.
Exam FindingsDramatic warmth, erythema, swelling, and exquisite tenderness of the first MTP joint, often so severe the patient cannot tolerate even a bedsheet touching the toe.
Typical PresentationSudden, severe, often nocturnal onset of great toe pain, redness, and swelling, sometimes with a triggering event (alcohol, rich meal, illness, dehydration, or starting/stopping certain medications).
Distinguishing MimickersCellulitis (less joint-centric, more diffuse skin involvement); septic arthritis of the first MTP (less common at this joint but must be considered, especially with fever or an atypical course); turf toe (traumatic mechanism, not this dramatic inflammatory presentation).
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis with polarized light microscopy for negatively birefringent urate crystals is definitive when feasible; clinical diagnosis is often made without aspiration given the classic presentation, but aspirate if diagnosis is uncertain or infection is a concern.

Claw Toe / Hammer Toe Deformity

MechanismMuscle imbalance between the intrinsic and extrinsic toe flexors/extensors, from neuromuscular disease (claw toe, e.g., Charcot-Marie-Tooth), diabetic neuropathy, or simple footwear-related deformity (hammer toe, often from tight shoes).
Exam FindingsFixed or flexible flexion deformity at the PIP (hammer toe) or at both PIP/DIP with MTP hyperextension (claw toe), often with painful dorsal callus formation from shoe friction over the prominent joint.
Typical PresentationProgressive toe deformity, often with painful corns/calluses over the prominent joints from shoe irritation, sometimes in a patient with known diabetic neuropathy or a neuromuscular condition.
Distinguishing MimickersSimply a structural/mechanical finding rather than a diagnosis requiring extensive differential — the key is recognizing when it signals an underlying neuromuscular disease (bilateral, progressive, associated with other neurologic findings) versus a purely mechanical/footwear-related deformity.

Fracture, Infection & Systemic 8 conditions

Ankle Fracture (Bimalleolar / Trimalleolar)

MechanismRotational force (typically supination-external rotation, the most common pattern) through the ankle mortise, with increasing energy progressing from an isolated malleolar fracture to bi- and tri-malleolar patterns as more structures fail.
Exam FindingsMarked swelling/deformity, tenderness at both the medial and lateral malleoli (and posterior malleolus in trimalleolar patterns), inability to bear weight.
Typical PresentationSignificant trauma (fall, twisting injury, sports) with obvious ankle deformity/swelling and inability to bear weight.
Distinguishing MimickersIsolated lateral ankle sprain (bony tenderness/deformity and inability to bear weight point toward fracture — apply Ottawa rules).
Imaging / WorkupStandard ankle series; these are generally UNSTABLE injuries (unlike an isolated non-displaced lateral malleolus fracture) and frequently require surgical fixation — assess mortise congruity carefully.

High Ankle Sprain (Syndesmotic Injury) / Maisonneuve Fracture

MechanismExternal rotation and/or hyperdorsiflexion force disrupting the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis; in a MAISONNEUVE fracture, the force propagates all the way up the interosseous membrane to fracture the PROXIMAL fibula, with the ankle injury being the visible/symptomatic tip of a much more proximal problem.
Exam FindingsTenderness over the anterior syndesmosis (above the ankle joint proper), positive squeeze test (compressing the tibia and fibula together at mid-calf reproduces distal ankle pain) and external rotation stress test; CRITICALLY, palpate the ENTIRE FIBULA including the proximal fibular head/neck — tenderness there with an otherwise unremarkable-looking ankle sprain is a Maisonneuve fracture until proven otherwise.
Typical PresentationExternal rotation-type ankle injury (often in cutting/pivoting sports) with pain ABOVE the ankle joint (rather than the more typical lateral sprain location) and disproportionate difficulty bearing weight; the proximal fibula fracture component may go completely unnoticed if not specifically palpated.
Distinguishing MimickersSimple lateral ankle sprain — THIS IS THE CLASSIC MISSED DIAGNOSIS; any ankle injury with tenderness above the joint line, medial ankle tenderness, or a mechanism involving external rotation should prompt palpation of the entire fibula.
Imaging / WorkupStandard ankle films plus DEDICATED TIBIA/FIBULA FILMS if proximal fibular tenderness is found; syndesmotic widening on mortise view; this is inherently unstable and typically requires surgical fixation.

Ankle Fracture-Dislocation

MechanismHigh-energy rotational or axial force causing both fracture and frank displacement of the talus relative to the mortise.
Exam FindingsGross deformity, often with skin tenting over a prominent malleolus (risk of skin necrosis/open conversion if not promptly reduced), neurovascular compromise possible if severe.
Typical PresentationObvious severe deformity after significant trauma, often with the foot visibly displaced relative to the leg.
Distinguishing MimickersN/A — this is a clinically obvious emergency.
Imaging / WorkupEMERGENT closed reduction should not be delayed for imaging if skin is tenting/threatened or there's neurovascular compromise; post-reduction films and CT for surgical planning.

Septic Arthritis of the Ankle

MechanismHematogenous seeding, direct inoculation, or contiguous spread from adjacent infection (e.g., an infected ulcer in a diabetic).
Exam FindingsWarmth, effusion, severe pain with any motion, often febrile/systemically ill.
Typical PresentationAcute, severely painful, swollen, warm ankle with global restriction of motion, especially in an at-risk host (diabetic, immunosuppressed, IVDU).
Distinguishing MimickersGout/pseudogout of the ankle; severe cellulitis without true joint involvement.
Imaging / WorkupArthrocentesis (often image-guided given the joint's anatomy); blood cultures, inflammatory markers.

Charcot Arthropathy (Neuropathic Joint)

MechanismProgressive joint destruction in a patient with peripheral neuropathy (most commonly diabetic), where loss of protective sensation allows repetitive unrecognized trauma to progressively destroy the joint.
Exam FindingsMarked swelling, warmth, and sometimes deformity of the foot/ankle that can appear alarmingly similar to infection, but the patient often reports SURPRISINGLY LITTLE PAIN relative to the degree of visible findings, given underlying neuropathy.
Typical PresentationDiabetic patient with peripheral neuropathy presenting with a hot, swollen, sometimes deformed foot/ankle, often after unrecognized minor trauma, with pain out of proportion to LOW rather than high relative to the exam findings.
Distinguishing MimickersOsteomyelitis/septic arthritis (this is THE critical and difficult distinction in a diabetic foot — both can present with warmth and swelling; the relatively preserved function and disproportionately mild pain lean toward Charcot, but infection must be actively excluded, especially if there's an overlying ulcer).
Imaging / WorkupPlain films show characteristic destructive/fragmentation changes; MRI can help distinguish Charcot from osteomyelitis though there is overlap; inflammatory markers and clinical context (presence of a wound/ulcer, systemic illness) guide the distinction; when in doubt, treat empirically for possible infection while pursuing further workup.

Peripheral Neuropathy (Diabetic / Other)

MechanismLength-dependent peripheral nerve damage from diabetes, alcohol use, vitamin deficiency (B12), chemotherapy, or other systemic causes.
Exam FindingsSymmetric, stocking-distribution sensory loss (vibration/monofilament testing), diminished/absent ankle reflexes, may have associated weakness in advanced cases.
Typical PresentationGradual, symmetric numbness/burning/tingling in both feet, often 'stocking' distribution, in a patient with diabetes or another risk factor; may present indirectly via a complication (unrecognized ulcer, Charcot joint, injury).
Distinguishing MimickersTarsal tunnel syndrome (unilateral, more focal, positive Tinel's rather than symmetric/diffuse); this is a systemic diagnosis rather than a focal structural one.
Imaging / WorkupClinical exam (monofilament, vibration, reflexes); consider EMG/NCS and lab workup (A1c, B12, TSH) for the underlying cause if not already established.

Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)

MechanismA poorly understood pain-amplification syndrome typically following a seemingly minor injury or surgery to the foot/ankle, involving dysregulated autonomic and inflammatory processes.
Exam FindingsPain grossly out of proportion to the inciting injury, allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli), autonomic changes (temperature/color asymmetry, sweating changes), and later trophic changes (skin, hair, nail changes) in the affected limb.
Typical PresentationPersistent, severe, disproportionate pain following a relatively minor ankle/foot injury or surgery, with associated autonomic/trophic changes, poor response to typical analgesics.
Distinguishing MimickersOngoing structural injury not yet fully diagnosed (must exclude occult fracture, nerve entrapment, or infection before settling on this diagnosis) — CRPS should generally be a diagnosis reached after reasonable structural causes are excluded.
Imaging / WorkupLargely clinical (Budapest criteria); imaging to exclude other structural causes; bone scan may show characteristic periarticular uptake in some cases.

Referred Pain — L5/S1 Lumbar Radiculopathy

MechanismNerve root compression at L5 (foot dorsum/great toe, weak dorsiflexion/EHL) or S1 (lateral foot/heel, weak plantarflexion, diminished ankle reflex).
Exam FindingsPositive straight leg raise, dermatomal sensory change, myotomal weakness pattern, diminished ankle reflex for S1; the ANKLE/FOOT EXAM ITSELF may be entirely normal aside from the neurologic findings.
Typical PresentationFoot/ankle pain or numbness with an associated back pain history, radiating in a dermatomal pattern, sometimes presenting primarily as foot symptoms with the back complaint being minor or absent.
Distinguishing MimickersAny primary ankle/foot pathology — a normal local structural exam with positive neurologic findings (reflex, dermatomal sensory change, SLR) should redirect the workup to the lumbar spine.
Imaging / WorkupLumbar spine MRI if red flags or the clinical picture points to radiculopathy rather than local pathology.

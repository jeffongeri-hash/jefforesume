01 · Blood Pressure

How It's Actually Measured

Three technologies you'll use interchangeably — they don't always agree, and knowing why matters.

“The machine says 168/94 but I couldn't hear the Korotkoff sounds clearly — which number do I trust?”

MethodHow it worksWhere it's usedPitfalls

Cuff size matters more than people think: a cuff that's too small for the arm circumference falsely elevates the reading (sometimes by 10–20 mmHg); a cuff that's too large falsely lowers it. If a BP doesn't fit the clinical picture, checking cuff size before reaching for medication is often the highest-yield move.

White coat effect (BP elevated only in a clinical setting, normal on home/ambulatory monitoring) and its inverse, masked hypertension (normal in clinic, elevated at home), are both common enough that a single ED reading should be interpreted with that context in mind, especially for a patient without other findings and no prior documented hypertension.

01 · Blood Pressure

Pulse Pressure & the Arterial Waveform

The gap between systolic and diastolic — and the shape of the pressure curve itself — both carry diagnostic information beyond the two numbers on the monitor.

Pulse pressure (SBP − DBP, normally roughly 40 mmHg) reflects stroke volume and arterial compliance. A pulse pressure that's narrow (roughly <25% of the systolic value) suggests a low stroke-volume state; one that's wide suggests either high stroke volume/low peripheral resistance or a stiff, non-compliant arterial tree.

Narrow pulse pressureWide pulse pressure
Cardiac tamponade (part of Beck's triad)Aortic regurgitation (“water-hammer” pulse)
Severe aortic stenosisElevated ICP — widening pulse pressure is part of the Cushing reflex, with bradycardia and irregular respirations
Hypovolemic/hemorrhagic shockSepsis / fever / any vasodilatory state
Restrictive/constrictive physiologyThyrotoxicosis, AV fistula
Stiff arteries of aging — isolated systolic hypertension

The arterial line waveform

For a patient on an arterial line, the waveform itself is a continuous physical exam finding. The rapid upstroke reflects left ventricular contractility (dP/dt); the systolic peak approximates SBP; the dicrotic notch marks aortic valve closure, and its clarity and timing can hint at vascular compliance; the diastolic runoff reflects peripheral vascular resistance. A useful, underused fact: as you move from a central (aortic) to a more peripheral (radial, dorsalis pedis) arterial site, the measured systolic pressure actually increases and the waveform narrows and sharpens — a phenomenon called distal pulse pressure amplification — which is why a radial arterial line SBP can legitimately read 5–20 mmHg higher than a central aortic pressure in the same patient at the same moment.

Figure 1. Arterial pressure waveform — upstroke, systolic peak, dicrotic notch, and diastolic runoff.
Damping check: the fast-flush (“square wave”) test distinguishes an accurate waveform from an over- or under-damped system. Flush the line and watch the return to baseline: 1–2 oscillations before settling is properly damped; no oscillation (sluggish return) is over-damped and underestimates SBP; multiple ringing oscillations is under-damped and overestimates SBP. Air bubbles, kinked tubing, and clot are the usual culprits.
01 · Blood Pressure

Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)

The number that actually drives organ perfusion — and, on most monitors, the one that's measured most directly.

MAP is the average pressure across a full cardiac cycle, not a simple average of SBP and DBP. Because diastole lasts roughly twice as long as systole at a normal heart rate, diastolic pressure is weighted more heavily:

The calculation

MAP ≈ DBP + ⅓(SBP − DBP)  =  (SBP + 2×DBP) / 3

This ⅓/⅔ weighting assumes a normal heart rate. As heart rate rises, diastole shortens proportionally more than systole, so the true MAP in a tachycardic patient runs slightly higher than this simplified formula predicts.

Underused fact: an automated oscillometric cuff (the default triage method) doesn't measure SBP or DBP directly at all — it measures MAP first, as the cuff pressure at which oscillation amplitude is greatest, and then derives the SBP and DBP you actually see on the screen algorithmically from that anchor point. The number clinicians pay the least attention to is often the one the machine is most confident about.

Organ perfusion depends on driving pressure through the vascular bed, not on the peak systolic number — which is why MAP, not SBP, is the target in most perfusion-critical protocols. Cerebral blood flow is held roughly constant by autoregulation across a MAP of approximately 60–150 mmHg in a normotensive patient (this range shifts upward with chronic hypertension, which is part of why rapid correction in a chronically hypertensive patient can drop perfusion below their new, higher autoregulatory floor — see the discharge discussion below).

ContextMAP targetWhy
Septic shock resuscitation≥65 mmHg (Surviving Sepsis Campaign)Threshold below which renal and other organ perfusion falls off sharply; cross-reference the Sepsis Atlas for full resuscitation detail

01 · Blood Pressure

Asymptomatic Severe Hypertension: the Discharge Decision

A genuinely useful case where the evidence has moved meaningfully in the last two years — and where an older mental model can lead you astray.

“BP is 196/110, no chest pain, no headache, no visual changes, exam is normal — can I just send them home?”

The short answer your reading landed on is correct as far as it goes: in a truly asymptomatic patient with severely elevated BP and no evidence of acute target organ injury, routine ED screening and routine ED pharmacologic treatment are not required, and the patient can be safely discharged. But the evidence behind that statement has evolved meaningfully, and the current (2025) guidance adds a layer the 2013 policy didn't have: whether to start an oral agent before that patient walks out the door.

Figure 2. How the evidence and guidance on asymptomatic severe hypertension evolved, 2013–2025.

The foundational policy (2013)

Wolf SJ, Lo B, Shih RD, et al. Clinical Policy: Critical Issues in the Evaluation and Management of Adult Patients in the ED With Asymptomatic Elevated Blood Pressure. Ann Emerg Med. 2013;62(1):59–68.

ACEP's original Level C recommendations: routine screening for acute target organ injury (creatinine, UA, ECG) is not required in truly asymptomatic patients; routine ED medical intervention is not required; and the patient should be referred for outpatient follow-up. Select higher-risk patients (very poor follow-up access, markedly elevated readings) could reasonably get a creatinine check. This is the policy most training programs still teach as “the” answer — and it's the source of the framing in your original question.

Why aggressive same-visit lowering can actively harm

Bress AP, Anderson TS, Flack JM, et al. The Management of Elevated Blood Pressure in the Acute Care Setting: A Scientific Statement From the American Heart Association. Hypertension. 2024;81(8):e94–e106.

This statement formally retired the term “hypertensive urgency” and replaced it with severe asymptomatic elevated BP (>180/120 without organ injury), explicitly to stop the reflex referral pattern the old term encouraged. Its central safety point: in a patient whose cerebral blood flow autoregulation has adapted to chronically elevated pressures, a rapid drop in BP — whether from parenteral agents or aggressive oral dosing — can drop cerebral perfusion below the new, higher autoregulatory floor and precipitate an ischemic stroke. The recommendation is to avoid parenteral BP-lowering therapy and avoid aggressively intensifying oral therapy in the acute setting; instead, reinstitute or modestly adjust the patient's oral regimen, ideally coordinated with outpatient follow-up.

Myth: “A very high number in an asymptomatic patient is itself an emergency I need to bring down before they leave.” The 2024 AHA statement's core message is close to the opposite: for the truly asymptomatic patient, rapid correction is the intervention most likely to cause harm, not the elevated number itself.

Does referral/admission actually change outcomes?

Patel KK, Young L, Howell EH, et al. Characteristics and Outcomes of Patients Presenting With Hypertensive Urgency in the Office Setting. JAMA Intern Med. 2016;176(7):981–988.

Retrospective cohort with propensity matching, Cleveland Clinic system, 2008–2013: of nearly 2.2 million office visits, 58,535 (4.6%) met the definition of hypertensive urgency (SBP ≥180 and/or DBP ≥110). Major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) at short-term follow-up were rare in asymptomatic patients regardless of disposition. Referral to the ED/hospital was associated with more hospitalizations but no improvement in outcomes compared with outpatient management. The less reassuring finding: roughly two-thirds of patients still had uncontrolled hypertension six months later — the real failure here is long-term control, not any short-term catastrophe.

The study that changed the 2025 ACEP recommendation

Brody A, Rahman T, Reed B, Millis S, Ference B, Flack JM, Levy PD. Safety and Efficacy of Antihypertensive Prescription at Emergency Department Discharge. Acad Emerg Med. 2015;22(5):632–635.

Retrospective analysis pooling data from two prospective, longitudinal RCTs enrolling ED patients with asymptomatic hypertension. Patients who received a new antihypertensive prescription at ED discharge had a significantly greater systolic BP reduction at short-term follow-up (difference of 19 mmHg, 95% CI 12–26) than those who didn't — without a corresponding increase in adverse effects: no new neurologic deficits, no ischemic events, no severe hypotension (no patient dropped below an SBP of 100 at follow-up) in either group. This was the only study of the studies ACEP's 2025 committee reviewed that met their methodological bar for inclusion (graded Class III), which is itself worth knowing — the evidence base here is real but still thin.

The current (2025) ACEP recommendation

Gemme S, Meltzer AC, et al. Clinical Policy: A Critical Issue in the Outpatient Management of Adult Patients Presenting to the ED With Asymptomatic Elevated Blood Pressure. Approved by the ACEP Board of Directors, January 22, 2025. Ann Emerg Med. 2025;86(1):e1–e11.

Building on the Brody et al. data, ACEP amended its 2013 stance: rather than treatment being optional and typically deferred, the updated policy gives a Level C recommendation to consider initiating an oral outpatient antihypertensive medication at ED discharge, paired with a referral for follow-up. This is framed as a reasonable option given how often the ED visit is a patient's only near-term point of contact with the healthcare system — not a mandate, and explicitly not a reason to reach for IV or aggressive oral titration in the department itself.

Practical synthesis

Confirm this is genuinely asymptomatic hypertension — a good history and exam (headache, chest pain, dyspnea, visual changes, focal neuro deficit, and a pregnancy test in a woman of childbearing age) rules out hypertensive emergency without needing routine labs or an ECG in the truly asymptomatic patient. If asymptomatic and no organ injury: don't chase the number with IV or aggressive oral agents. Do consider starting or restarting a first-line oral agent (thiazide, ACEI/ARB, dihydropyridine CCB depending on comorbidities) with a concrete, written follow-up plan — particularly for patients whose access to primary care is uncertain, since the ED visit may be their most reliable point of contact. Frame the conversation as partnership rather than dismissal: telling a patient “you don't need to be here” measurably discourages future follow-up and adherence.

Verify before use: specific first-line agent selection, starting doses, and required baseline labs (e.g., potassium/creatinine before starting an ACEI/ARB) should be confirmed against current formulary and renal function before prescribing; the studies above address the ED-treat-vs-defer decision, not agent-specific dosing.

02 · Heart Rate

The Combined Hypertension + Tachycardia Patient

Two abnormal numbers that showed up together are a clue, not two separate problems to treat in parallel.

“BP 178/104, HR 118 — do I treat the pressure, the rate, or figure out what's driving both?”

The instinct to reach for a beta-blocker because it will “fix both numbers” is exactly the trap here. Elevated BP and HR occurring together are very often both downstream of the same driver — pain, anxiety, a sympathomimetic, thyroid storm, alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal, or an evolving hypertensive emergency with reflex sympathetic activation — and treating that driver resolves both numbers at once far more reliably than treating either number in isolation.

Myth, worth re-examining: “Never give a beta-blocker if cocaine/stimulant use is suspected — unopposed alpha stimulation will cause a hypertensive crisis or coronary vasospasm.” The concern originated from a small 1990 catheterization study, and subsequent meta-analyses and a 2024 Journal of Hospital Medicine “Things We Do for No Reason” review have found little clinical evidence of harm, particularly with cardioselective or combined alpha/beta agents like labetalol. That said, current AHA guidance still advises against beta-blocker monotherapy in this setting, and benzodiazepines remain the correct first-line agent regardless — this is a genuinely unsettled area to watch rather than a clean reversal.
Verify before use: agent selection and the beta-blocker-in-stimulant-toxicity question specifically should be checked against your institution's current toxicology guidance — this is an area where practice is visibly shifting but formal society recommendations haven't fully caught up to the newer observational data.

03 · Respiratory Rate

The A-a Gradient

The single calculation that tells you whether hypoxemia lives inside the lung or outside it.

The alveolar-arterial (A-a) gradient compares the oxygen pressure the alveolus should have (calculated) against what the arterial blood actually has (measured on ABG). A normal gradient with hypoxemia means the lung itself is doing its job correctly and the problem is extrapulmonary (hypoventilation, or low ambient FiO₂ at altitude). An elevated gradient localizes the problem to the lung itself: V/Q mismatch, shunt, or a diffusion defect.

Figure 4. The oxygen cascade from atmosphere to arterial blood — the A-a gradient is the gap between the calculated alveolar value and the measured arterial value.

The calculation

PAO₂ = FiO₂ × (Patm − PH2O) − (PaCO₂ / R)

On room air at sea level, this simplifies to roughly: PAO₂ ≈ 150 − (PaCO₂ / 0.8)

A-a gradient = PAO₂ (calculated) − PaO₂ (measured on ABG). Expected normal gradient widens with age — a commonly used estimate is roughly (age / 4) + 4 mmHg.

Normal A-a gradient + hypoxemiaElevated A-a gradient + hypoxemia
Hypoventilation (opioid overdose, neuromuscular weakness, severe obesity hypoventilation)V/Q mismatch — PE, COPD, asthma, pneumonia

Verify before use: the age-adjustment formula for expected normal A-a gradient varies slightly across references — treat it as a rough anchor, not a precise cutoff, and always interpret alongside the clinical picture.
04 · Pulse Oximetry

How It Actually Works

A light-absorption trick that measures a proxy for oxygenation — not oxygenation itself.

A pulse oximeter shines red (≈660nm) and infrared (≈940nm) light through a perfused tissue bed (fingertip, earlobe) and measures differential absorption: oxyhemoglobin absorbs more infrared and less red light than deoxyhemoglobin. The device isolates the pulsatile component of the absorbed signal — arterial blood, which changes volume with each heartbeat — from the non-pulsatile background (venous blood, tissue, bone), and calculates SpO₂ from the ratio of that pulsatile absorption at the two wavelengths. Critically, this means SpO₂ is a measure of hemoglobin saturation, not oxygen content or delivery — a severely anemic patient can have a normal SpO₂ while carrying dangerously little total oxygen.

04 · Pulse Oximetry

The Plethysmography Waveform

The waveform on the monitor is a real physical exam finding, not just decoration next to the number.

Figure 5. The pulse oximetry (plethysmography) waveform — the pulsatile AC signal riding on a non-pulsatile DC baseline.

The waveform's amplitude reflects the perfusion index (PI) — the ratio of pulsatile (AC) to non-pulsatile (DC) signal, typically reported as a percentage. A low PI (roughly <1–2% depending on device) signals poor peripheral perfusion — vasoconstriction, shock, hypothermia, or a poorly positioned probe — and is one of the most common reasons for an unreliable or intermittently dropping-out SpO₂ reading, independent of true oxygenation status.

An underused trick: respirophasic variation in the plethysmography waveform amplitude — the waveform visibly growing and shrinking with the respiratory cycle — is a non-invasive surrogate for pulsus paradoxus, and can be a useful adjunct clue in severe asthma/COPD exacerbation or cardiac tamponade when a blood pressure cuff isn't practical to use breath-by-breath.

04 · Pulse Oximetry

Ambulatory / Exertional Pulse Oximetry

Resting SpO₂ can be reassuringly normal while exertion tells a completely different story.

“Resting sat is 97% on room air — does that mean this patient is actually safe to send home?”

The clearest illustration of why this matters became widely appreciated during the COVID-19 pandemic: patients with significant pneumonitis could have a normal resting SpO₂ (“silent” or “happy” hypoxia) that dropped meaningfully only with exertion. This isn't unique to COVID — the same principle underlies decades of pulmonary practice using the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) to detect exertional desaturation in interstitial lung disease and COPD.

COPD exertional desaturation cohort (Cardiology Research and Practice literature; various COPD/ILD 6MWT cohorts)Exertional desaturation (SpO₂ <90% or a drop ≥4% during a 6-minute walk test) is associated with significantly higher mortality in COPD, and shorter 3-minute walk protocols detect the great majority (roughly 87–91% sensitivity in ILD cohorts) of desaturation events identified by the full 6-minute test.
Ambulatory vitals in the workup of pulmonary embolism using a standardized 3-minute walk test — CJEM literatureA resting room-air SpO₂ cutoff around 95% has been used to help risk-stratify suspected PE into higher- versus lower-risk groups; structured ambulatory (walking) vital sign protocols have also been studied as an adjunct in the ED PE workup, on the logic that a hemodynamically borderline PE may look reassuring at rest and decompensate with exertion.
Caution, not a clean endorsement: a 2021 rapid systematic review specific to COVID-19 found that exertional desaturation tests (40-step walk, 1-minute sit-to-stand, 6MWT) had not been formally validated in COVID-19 specifically at the time, despite widespread clinical adoption — the underlying physiology is sound and the tool is reasonable to use, but treat it as a useful bedside gestalt tool rather than a validated, guideline-mandated discharge gatekeeper.
Verify before use: specific desaturation thresholds and walk-test protocols (distance, duration, stop criteria) vary by indication and institution — confirm your local protocol before using a specific numeric cutoff as a hard discharge criterion.
05 · Discharge Decision-Making

Ambulation Trials & Orthostatic Vitals

Widely practiced, weakly validated — worth understanding exactly where the evidence is thin so you can use the tool for what it's actually good at.

“I had them stand up and walk the hallway before discharge and they felt fine — how much does that actually buy me?”

Two related but distinct practices get bundled together at the bedside: orthostatic vital signs (measuring BP/HR supine then standing, looking for a numeric drop) and a broader ambulation trial (walking the patient and watching for symptoms — dizziness, altered mental status, or vision changes — rather than chasing a specific number). The evidence for these two is not the same, and conflating them leads to over-trusting a maneuver that's actually a fairly weak diagnostic test.

What the numbers alone are worth

Koziol-McLain J, et al. Orthostatic vital signs in emergency department patients. Ann Emerg Med. 1991.In 132 presumed euvolemic ED patients, the statistically normal range of orthostatic change was far wider than commonly assumed: heart rate varied from −5 to +39 bpm on standing, and systolic BP from −20 to +26 mmHg. Using conventional “positive” cutoffs, 43% of known-euvolemic patients would have been classified as having abnormal orthostatics.
Multiple community cohorts (Raiha 1995; Ooi et al. 1997)In a random sample of well elderly adults, roughly 28% had orthostatic changes meeting standard positive criteria with no association to 10-year outcomes. In asymptomatic nursing home residents, more than half met criteria for orthostatic hypotension at baseline.
White JL, et al. Orthostatic vital signs do not predict 30-day serious outcomes in older emergency department patients with syncope. Am J Emerg Med. 2019.Secondary analysis of a multicenter prospective cohort of patients ≥60 years old with syncope/near-syncope: abnormal orthostatic vitals did not predict 30-day serious outcomes. As a diagnostic test for clinically significant volume loss, orthostatics have been reported with sensitivity as low as ~22% (95% CI 6–48%) for moderate blood loss, and are not reliably sensitive to volume losses under roughly 1000 mL (Level B evidence).
What this doesn't mean: it doesn't mean orthostatic vitals are useless — it means a numeric cutoff, used alone, is a poor rule-in/rule-out test. A 2022 Journal of Emergency Medicine review reframes their proper use: most valuable in a handful of specific presentations (suspected blood loss, dehydration, unexplained dizziness, weakness, falls) and most informative when interpreted together with symptoms rather than as a number in isolation.

Why the ambulation trial (symptom-based) is the more defensible half

The strongest evidence-based signal in this whole space isn't the millimeters of mercury — it's whether the patient becomes symptomatic on standing and walking: dizziness, altered mental status, or new vision changes. This reframes the ambulation trial from “a diagnostic test with a pass/fail number” to “a provocative maneuver that surfaces the same instability the patient would otherwise experience at home, alone, without a monitor and a nurse two feet away.” That distinction matters for documentation and for how much weight the trial should carry in a discharge decision:

FindingWhat it's worth
Numeric orthostatic drop, patient feels fineWeak signal alone — seen in a large minority of normal, euvolemic people; do not anchor a disposition decision on the number in isolation

Practical takeaway for the discharge note: document what you actually observed — “ambulated 20 feet in the hallway without dizziness, altered mentation, or visual change, vitals stable” — rather than leaning on “orthostatics negative” as if it were a validated rule-out test. The former is defensible and clinically meaningful; the latter overstates what the maneuver is actually shown to do.
Verify before use: evidence quality here is mixed and somewhat dated (much of the core orthostatic vitals literature is from the 1990s–2019); confirm against your institution's current syncope/fall-risk discharge protocol, which may specify its own required ambulation or observation criteria regardless of the underlying evidence quality.

Normal Pediatric Vital Signs by Age

The single most common source of a missed pediatric deterioration is applying adult intuition to a pediatric number.

Unlike adults, there is no single normal range — heart rate and respiratory rate both fall steadily from infancy through adolescence, and a number that's reassuring in a toddler is a red flag in a school-age child. These ranges follow the commonly used PALS reference values.

Tachycardia is the earliest and most sensitive sign of pediatric distress — pain, fever, anxiety, hypovolemia, hypoxia, and early compensated shock all present with tachycardia first, often well before any blood pressure change. Because children maintain blood pressure through vasoconstriction and tachycardia far more effectively than adults, hypotension in a child is a late and ominous finding that indicates decompensated shock — do not wait for a low blood pressure to recognize a sick child.
Verify before use: pediatric normal ranges vary modestly across PALS editions and reference sources — confirm against your institution's current PALS-aligned reference chart, particularly at the edges of each age band.

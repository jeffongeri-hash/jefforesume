Acid Suppression & Peptic Ulcer Disease

Every drug here targets one specific point in the gastrin-histamine-ACh convergence on the parietal cell established in GI Atlas Vol. 1 — and current H. pylori guidance has shifted decisively away from clarithromycin.

Proton Pump Inhibitors

Omeprazole, esomeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, rabeprazole, dexlansoprazole. Irreversibly bind the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase (Vol. 1) via covalent disulfide bond formation — since the pump must be newly synthesized to restore activity, effect outlasts the drug's short plasma half-life considerably. Prodrugs requiring an acidic environment for activation (accumulating specifically in the acid-secreting canaliculus), which is why they're dosed ~30–60 minutes before a meal — timed to peak plasma concentration when the maximum number of pumps are actively secreting and available to trap the activated drug.

ConsiderationDetail
Long-term risksHypomagnesemia (impaired intestinal magnesium absorption), B12 deficiency (acid is needed to release B12 from food — see GI Atlas Vol. 2), increased fracture risk (reduced calcium absorption efficiency), increased risk of enteric infections including C. difficile (loss of the gastric acid barrier that normally kills ingested pathogens), and community-acquired pneumonia (theorized oropharyngeal/gastric bacterial overgrowth)
Clopidogrel interactionOmeprazole/esomeprazole inhibit CYP2C19, the enzyme required to activate clopidogrel (a prodrug) — theoretical reduced antiplatelet effect; pantoprazole is generally preferred when concurrent clopidogrel therapy is needed given weaker CYP2C19 inhibition
Rebound acid hypersecretionAbrupt discontinuation after prolonged use can cause a transient hypergastrinemic rebound — chronic acid suppression causes G-cell hyperplasia (Vol. 1) that persists briefly after the drug is stopped, arguing for a taper rather than abrupt cessation after long-term use

H2 Receptor Antagonists

Famotidine, cimetidine, nizatidine (ranitidine withdrawn from the US market in 2020 over NDMA contamination concerns). Block the histamine H2 receptor directly — recall from Vol. 1 that histamine potentiates gastrin/ACh signaling rather than just contributing independently, which is why blocking this one receptor produces disproportionate acid suppression. Cimetidine is a potent CYP450 inhibitor (multiple isoenzymes) with clinically significant interaction potential — largely why famotidine is preferred in practice today. Tolerance can develop with continuous H2 blocker use (receptor downregulation) in a way PPIs don't exhibit, given the PPIs' irreversible mechanism.

Potassium-Competitive Acid BlockersVonoprazan

A newer class blocking the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase via reversible, potassium-competitive binding rather than the PPIs' covalent mechanism — faster onset (doesn't require acid-triggered activation) and more consistent acid suppression not dependent on meal timing. Increasingly incorporated directly into first-line H. pylori regimens (below) given its acid-suppression reliability independent of CYP2C19 genotype variability that affects some PPIs.

H. pylori EradicationCurrent (2024 ACG) Guidance

Clarithromycin triple therapy is no longer preferred first-line
Clarithromycin resistance now exceeds 15–20% in most of North America and Europe, dropping standard triple therapy (PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin) to unacceptably low eradication rates in most regions. Current ACG guidance recommends against empiric clarithromycin-based triple therapy when local resistance patterns are unknown.

Post-treatment eradication testing is now recommended for all patients, not just those with persistent symptoms.

Antiemetics

Five mechanistically distinct drug classes, each hitting a different receptor along the vomiting reflex pathway — matching the right class to the right trigger is the whole game.

5-HT3 Antagonists

Ondansetron, granisetron, palonosetron. Block serotonin signaling at vagal afferents and the chemoreceptor trigger zone — directly exploiting the 5-HT3 mechanism established in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section. First-line for chemotherapy-induced and postoperative nausea/vomiting. QT prolongation is a class effect worth monitoring, particularly with IV dosing or in patients on other QT-prolonging drugs. Palonosetron has a notably longer half-life and higher receptor-binding affinity than the others, giving it an edge for delayed (rather than acute) chemotherapy-induced nausea.

D2 Antagonists

AgentNotes
MetoclopramideAlso has prokinetic (5-HT4 agonist) activity — see this volume's Prokinetics section. Boxed warning for tardive dyskinesia with prolonged use (>12 weeks generally avoided).
Prochlorperazine, promethazinePhenothiazines — central D2 antagonism plus antihistamine/anticholinergic activity contributing to sedation; extrapyramidal side effects (acute dystonia, akathisia) are a real risk, particularly in younger patients

All D2 antagonists act at the chemoreceptor trigger zone (area postrema) — a brainstem region outside the blood-brain barrier, allowing it to directly sense circulating toxins/drugs and trigger vomiting without needing those substances to cross into the CNS proper.

NK1 (Substance P) Antagonists

Aprepitant, fosaprepitant, netupitant (often combined with palonosetron as a fixed-dose combination). Block substance P/neurokinin-1 receptor signaling — the same excitatory tachykinin introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section, here acting centrally rather than enterically. Particularly effective for the delayed phase of chemotherapy-induced nausea (days 2–5 post-chemotherapy), a phase 5-HT3 antagonists cover less completely on their own — the two classes are frequently combined for exactly this complementary coverage.

Antihistamines & Anticholinergics

AgentPrimary use
Diphenhydramine, meclizine, dimenhydrinateMotion sickness, vertigo-associated nausea (vestibular pathway-mediated, distinct from the chemotherapy/postoperative triggers above)
Scopolamine (transdermal)Motion sickness prophylaxis — anticholinergic blockade of vestibular-to-vomiting-center signaling

Cannabinoids

Dronabinol, nabilone — synthetic THC analogs, used for refractory chemotherapy-induced nausea and appetite stimulation. Mechanism is less precisely receptor-mapped than the classes above (CB1 receptor activity in the CNS emesis pathways), reserved generally for cases refractory to the more targeted agents.

Corticosteroids as Antiemetic Adjuncts

Dexamethasone is a standard component of chemotherapy antiemetic regimens despite not fitting neatly into any single mechanistic category above — proposed mechanisms include reduced peritumoral edema, prostaglandin synthesis inhibition, and direct effects on the chemoreceptor trigger zone. Its long half-life (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a) makes it convenient for multi-day chemotherapy antiemetic coverage.

Prokinetics & Motility-Targeted Agents

Each prokinetic drug exploits one specific physiologic mechanism from the GI Atlas Vol. 1/3 motility story — and the PAMORAs are the cleanest example in this entire series of a receptor-level problem getting a receptor-level fix.

Metoclopramide

Dual mechanism: D2 receptor antagonism (both antiemetic and prokinetic — dopamine normally inhibits upper GI motility, so blocking it disinhibits) plus 5-HT4 agonism (directly promoting ACh release from enteric neurons — GI Atlas Vol. 1). First-line pharmacologic option for gastroparesis (GI Atlas Vol. 3) given its dual antiemetic/prokinetic action, but limited to short-term use (generally <12 weeks) given the tardive dyskinesia boxed warning shared with its antiemetic use above.

Domperidone

Peripherally-restricted D2 antagonist — doesn't cross the blood-brain barrier appreciably, avoiding the CNS extrapyramidal/tardive dyskinesia risk of metoclopramide. Not FDA-approved in the US (available via expanded access/compounding in specific circumstances) but widely used internationally. Requires baseline and periodic ECG monitoring given dose-dependent QT prolongation risk.

Erythromycin

Direct motilin receptor agonist (GI Atlas Vol. 1) — an antibiotic side effect turned deliberate prokinetic tool, used at sub-antimicrobial doses for gastroparesis and to clear gastric contents before urgent endoscopy in upper GI bleeding. Tachyphylaxis develops rapidly with continued use (receptor downregulation), limiting it to short-term/intermittent use rather than chronic daily therapy.

Prucalopride

Selective 5-HT4 agonist — directly promotes the peristaltic reflex (GI Atlas Vol. 1's serotonin mechanism) without the D2-related side effects of metoclopramide/domperidone. Approved for chronic idiopathic constipation; part of a class lineage (following cisapride and tegaserod, both withdrawn/restricted for cardiac and ischemic safety signals) that prucalopride's more selective 5-HT4 binding was specifically designed to avoid.

Peripherally-Acting Mu-Opioid Receptor Antagonists (PAMORAs)

Methylnaltrexone, naloxegol, naldemedine. Directly reverse opioid-induced constipation by blocking the same enteric μ-opioid receptors introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section (the enkephalin system opioids hijack) — engineered to be either excluded by the blood-brain barrier (methylnaltrexone, a permanently charged quaternary amine) or to have minimal CNS penetration (naloxegol, naldemedine — PEGylated/structurally modified to be P-glycoprotein substrates, actively pumped back out of the CNS), preserving central analgesia while reversing the peripheral GI effect. Alvimopan, from the same drug class, is FDA-approved specifically to accelerate GI recovery after bowel resection surgery — directly addressing the pharmacologic component of postoperative ileus (GI Atlas Vol. 3's closing section).

Why this class is worth understanding as a category, not memorizing drug-by-drug
Every PAMORA solves the identical problem (peripheral opioid receptor blockade without reversing central analgesia) with a different pharmacokinetic trick to stay out of the CNS — recognizing this shared design logic makes the whole class far easier to reason about than treating each drug as a separate fact to memorize.

Laxatives — Full Spectrum

Five distinct mechanisms, matched to the constipation subtypes established in GI Atlas Vol. 3 — bulk/osmotic/stimulant agents work on any cause, while the secretagogues target the specific channels driving fluid into the lumen.

Bulk-Forming

Psyllium, methylcellulose, wheat dextrin. Non-digestible fiber absorbs water, increasing stool bulk and softness, which mechanically stimulates peristalsis via distension — the most physiologic of the laxative classes, generally recommended as first-line for chronic constipation given a favorable long-term safety profile. Requires adequate concurrent fluid intake; can worsen bloating in slow-transit constipation or significant dysmotility (GI Atlas Vol. 3), where the added bulk sits in an already sluggish colon longer than intended.

Osmotic

AgentMechanismNote
Polyethylene glycol (PEG)Non-absorbable, osmotically draws water into the lumen without electrolyte disturbanceFirst-line osmotic agent given minimal systemic absorption/electrolyte risk; used at high doses for full bowel prep before colonoscopy
LactuloseNon-absorbable disaccharide, osmotic effect plus colonic bacterial fermentation to short-chain fatty acids (GI Atlas Vol. 2) lowering colonic pHAlso first-line for hepatic encephalopathy — the lowered colonic pH traps ammonia as non-absorbable ammonium, and the resulting catharsis directly removes nitrogenous waste, a dual mechanism beyond simple laxative effect
Magnesium citrate/hydroxide, sorbitolPoorly absorbed osmotic agentsMagnesium salts require caution in renal impairment given systemic absorption risk and hypermagnesemia

Stimulant

Senna, bisacodyl. Directly stimulate enteric neurons and colonic smooth muscle contraction, plus increase colonic secretion — the most reliably potent class for acute constipation relief, appropriate for short-term/intermittent use. The old teaching that chronic stimulant laxative use causes irreversible "cathartic colon" or colonic nerve damage has not held up well under modern scrutiny, but they're still generally positioned after bulk-forming/osmotic agents given their less physiologic mechanism and higher cramping potential.

Stool Softeners

Docusate. A surfactant that allows water/fat to penetrate stool more effectively, softening it without directly stimulating motility — modest efficacy as monotherapy in trials, generally used as a low-risk adjunct (e.g., post-surgically or post-partum to reduce straining) rather than a primary constipation treatment.

SecretagoguesChannel-Specific Mechanisms

AgentTargetMechanism
LubiprostoneClC-2 chloride channel (apical enterocyte membrane)Directly activates the channel, increasing luminal chloride (and secondarily fluid) secretion — independent of the CFTR pathway covered in GI Atlas Vol. 2, a distinct chloride channel entirely
Linaclotide, plecanatideGuanylate cyclase-C receptor (apical enterocyte membrane)Agonism raises intracellular cGMP, which activates CFTR directly (this time genuinely the same channel from GI Atlas Vol. 2's Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion section, here expressed on intestinal rather than pancreatic ductal epithelium) — increasing intestinal chloride/fluid secretion and also reducing visceral afferent pain signaling, giving these agents a dual constipation-relief and pain-reduction benefit particularly useful in IBS-C (GI Atlas Vol. 3)
Why linaclotide/plecanatide are more than "just" laxatives
Their guanylate cyclase-C/cGMP/CFTR mechanism directly overlaps with the CFTR-mediated pancreatic bicarbonate secretion pathway from GI Atlas Vol. 2 — the same channel, expressed in a different segment of gut epithelium, now deliberately activated pharmacologically rather than via the endogenous secretin pathway. This is part of why these agents also reduce visceral hypersensitivity/pain in IBS-C beyond their laxative effect — cGMP signaling in this pathway independently dampens peripheral pain-sensing afferent nerve activity.

Matching Class to MechanismCallback to GI Atlas Vol. 3

Slow-transit constipation (GI Atlas Vol. 3) generally responds best to secretagogues or prokinetics (prucalopride, this volume's Prokinetics section) that directly drive motility, while dyssynergic defecation requires biofeedback rather than any pharmacologic escalation — no laxative class here fixes a coordination/learned-behavior problem, which is exactly why correctly distinguishing the two before treatment matters as much pharmacologically as it did diagnostically in Volume 3.

Antidiarrheals & Bile Acid Therapeutics

Matching agent to mechanism using the secretory-vs-osmotic framework from GI Atlas Vol. 2 — and two bile-acid-targeted drugs that work by opposite logic.

Loperamide

Peripherally-restricted opioid agonist — activates the same enteric μ-opioid receptors covered throughout this atlas (GI Atlas Vol. 1's enkephalin discussion, this volume's PAMORA section) without meaningful CNS penetration at standard doses, slowing transit and increasing anal sphincter tone without central opioid effects. First-line for uncomplicated acute diarrhea; should be avoided in suspected invasive/inflammatory diarrhea (bloody stool, high fever) given a theoretical risk of prolonging pathogen contact time or precipitating toxic megacolon in that setting.

Diphenoxylate-Atropine

A centrally-active opioid (diphenoxylate) combined with a subtherapeutic dose of atropine specifically to discourage deliberate overdose/misuse (atropine's unpleasant anticholinergic effects at supratherapeutic doses act as a built-in deterrent) — unlike loperamide, diphenoxylate does cross into the CNS at normal doses, carrying genuine potential for opioid-type effects and dependence with prolonged use.

Bismuth Subsalicylate

Multiple proposed mechanisms: direct antimicrobial activity, anti-inflammatory (salicylate component), and possibly reduced intestinal fluid secretion. Also a component of bismuth quadruple H. pylori therapy (this volume's Acid Suppression section) — the same molecule serving both a symptomatic antidiarrheal role and a direct antimicrobial role depending on context. Contains salicylate — avoid in children/teens with viral illness (Reye syndrome risk, the same concern as aspirin) and use cautiously alongside other salicylate-containing products.

Octreotide

Somatostatin analog — exploiting the universal inhibitory brake introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Stomach section, redeployed here as a direct antisecretory agent. First-line for hormone-driven secretory diarrhea (VIPoma, carcinoid syndrome — both Vol. 1) and useful adjunctively in refractory secretory diarrhea of other causes given its broad inhibitory effect on GI hormone secretion, motility, and splanchnic blood flow.

Bile Acid Sequestrants

Cholestyramine, colesevelam, colestipol. Bind bile acids in the intestinal lumen, preventing their colonic-irritant/secretory effect — direct treatment for bile acid malabsorption-driven diarrhea (GI Atlas Vol. 2's Biliary Secretion section), most commonly post-cholecystectomy or from terminal ileal disease/resection. Also used for hyperlipidemia (interrupting enterohepatic bile acid recirculation forces the liver to convert more cholesterol into new bile acids, lowering circulating LDL) — the same mechanism, two different indications. Can bind and reduce absorption of other oral medications and fat-soluble vitamins (Vol. 2) if not appropriately spaced in timing.

Ursodeoxycholic AcidThe Opposite Bile Acid Strategy

Rather than removing bile acids (sequestrants above), ursodeoxycholic acid is itself a hydrophilic bile acid that — when given exogenously — displaces more toxic, hydrophobic endogenous bile acids from the circulating pool and reduces hepatic cholesterol secretion into bile (directly opposing the cholesterol-supersaturation mechanism behind gallstone formation from GI Atlas Vol. 2). Used for cholesterol gallstone dissolution in select patients who aren't surgical candidates, and as a mainstay therapy in primary biliary cholangitis, where it's thought to reduce cholestatic bile acid-mediated hepatocyte injury.

IBD Pharmacology — 5-ASA, Immunomodulators & Corticosteroids

The foundational IBD drug classes — cheaper, older, and still central to mild-moderate disease management even as the biologics/small-molecule landscape (next section) has come to dominate moderate-severe care.

5-Aminosalicylates (5-ASA)

AgentDelivery mechanism
SulfasalazineProdrug — colonic bacteria cleave the azo bond, releasing active 5-ASA plus sulfapyridine (the carrier molecule responsible for most of the drug's side effects: rash, hemolysis in G6PD deficiency, reversible oligospermia)
Mesalamine5-ASA itself, formulated with pH-dependent (Asacol — releases in terminal ileum/colon) or time-dependent (Pentasa — releases throughout small and large bowel) coatings to target specific gut regions without needing the sulfapyridine carrier
Balsalazide, olsalazineAlternative prodrug carriers, azo-bond-cleaved by colonic bacteria like sulfasalazine but without the sulfapyridine moiety — better tolerated

Topical anti-inflammatory action on the colonic/ileal mucosa (mechanism incompletely defined, likely multiple pathways including reduced prostaglandin/leukotriene synthesis). First-line for mild-moderate ulcerative colitis; notably less effective for Crohn's disease, reflecting UC's continuous colonic mucosal inflammation pattern versus Crohn's transmural, skip-lesion pattern that a topically-acting colonic drug reaches less completely.

Immunomodulators

AgentMechanismKey monitoring
Azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurinePurine analog prodrugs, metabolized to 6-thioguanine nucleotides that incorporate into DNA, suppressing lymphocyte proliferationTPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) genotype/phenotype testing before initiation — poor metabolizers accumulate toxic drug levels at standard doses, risking severe myelosuppression; NUDT15 testing is an increasingly recognized second relevant pharmacogenomic marker, particularly in certain ethnic populations

Both classes have a slow onset (weeks to months) — not useful for inducing remission in an acute flare, but valuable as steroid-sparing maintenance therapy, often used as a bridge or combined with biologics (next section) rather than as monotherapy in more severe disease.

CorticosteroidsGI-Specific Notes

Full corticosteroid pharmacology (potency, adrenal suppression, tapering) is covered in the Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a — the GI-specific point worth adding here is budesonide's targeted formulations: an ileal-release formulation for Crohn's disease limited to the terminal ileum/right colon, and a MMX (multi-matrix) colonic-release formulation for ulcerative colitis. Both exploit budesonide's ~90% first-pass hepatic metabolism (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a) to achieve strong local anti-inflammatory effect with substantially less systemic glucocorticoid exposure than prednisone — appropriate for mild-moderate, anatomically-limited disease, but not a substitute for systemic steroids in severe or extensive disease given the lower systemic drug levels achieved by design.

IBD Pharmacology — Biologics & Small Molecules

Five mechanistically distinct targeted-therapy classes, each blocking a different node in the inflammatory cascade — this is where modern IBD management actually lives for moderate-severe disease.

Anti-TNF

Infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab pegol, golimumab. Neutralize tumor necrosis factor-alpha, a central pro-inflammatory cytokine in IBD. Infliximab is chimeric (mouse/human) and IV-infused; adalimumab and golimumab are fully human and subcutaneously self-injected; certolizumab pegol is a PEGylated Fab fragment lacking the Fc region (reduced placental transfer, a relevant consideration in pregnancy). Immunogenicity (anti-drug antibody formation, reducing efficacy over time) is a class-wide concern, part of why concurrent immunomodulator therapy (previous section) is frequently used to reduce antibody formation and extend durability of response.

Anti-Integrin

Vedolizumab — blocks α4β7 integrin, a gut-specific adhesion molecule that lymphocytes need to migrate from blood into intestinal tissue. This gut-selectivity is the drug's defining advantage — by not broadly suppressing systemic immune trafficking the way anti-TNF agents can, vedolizumab carries a more favorable systemic infection risk profile, at some cost of slower onset of action (given it takes time for existing gut-infiltrated lymphocytes to be affected, rather than an immediate cytokine-neutralizing effect).

Anti-IL-12/23 & Anti-IL-23

AgentTarget
Ustekinumabp40 subunit — shared by both IL-12 and IL-23, blocking both cytokine pathways simultaneously
Risankizumab, guselkumab, mirikizumabp19 subunit — IL-23-specific, sparing IL-12 signaling entirely

The therapeutic shift from dual IL-12/23 blockade toward IL-23-selective agents reflects growing evidence that IL-23 specifically (rather than IL-12) is the dominant driver of the chronic Th17-mediated inflammation in IBD — selective blockade aims to preserve IL-12's role in normal antimicrobial defense while still controlling disease.

JAK Inhibitors

Tofacitinib, upadacitinib (filgotinib approved in EU/UK/Japan, not US). Oral small molecules blocking Janus kinase-mediated intracellular signaling downstream of multiple cytokine receptors at once — broader immunosuppressive footprint than the more selective biologics above, reflected in boxed warnings for VTE, major cardiovascular events, and malignancy risk (derived largely from rheumatoid arthritis trial data, extrapolated with caution to the IBD population). Advantages include oral administration, rapid onset, and lack of immunogenicity (no anti-drug antibody formation, since these are small molecules rather than proteins).

S1P Receptor Modulators

Ozanimod, etrasimod. Block sphingosine-1-phosphate receptors on lymphocytes, trapping them in lymph nodes and preventing their egress into circulation and subsequent gut infiltration — a distinct trafficking-based mechanism from vedolizumab's integrin blockade, but similarly aimed at reducing lymphocyte access to gut tissue rather than neutralizing a cytokine directly. Oral, approved for ulcerative colitis; requires baseline cardiac conduction assessment given first-dose bradycardia risk (a class effect shared with other S1P modulators used in multiple sclerosis).

On the Horizon

Anti-TL1A antibodies represent an emerging mechanistic class in late-stage development, targeting a cytokine implicated in both inflammatory and fibrotic aspects of IBD — part of a broader trend toward increasingly precise cytokine/pathway targeting as the mechanistic understanding of IBD's heterogeneous inflammatory drivers continues to refine which patients respond best to which specific mechanism.

Antispasmodics, Enzyme Replacement, Antibiotics & Miscellaneous

The closing section rounds up everything that didn't fit the six major categories above — several of which are among the most directly mechanism-derived drugs in this entire atlas.

Antispasmodics

Dicyclomine, hyoscyamine — anticholinergic (antimuscarinic) agents blocking the ACh-driven smooth muscle contraction introduced throughout GI Atlas Vol. 1 — used for IBS-associated cramping/pain. Peppermint oil (enteric-coated formulations) works by a distinct mechanism — direct smooth muscle calcium-channel blockade — with genuine randomized-trial support for IBS symptom relief, one of the few "natural product" GI therapies with reasonably solid evidence behind it.

Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement

Pancrelipase — a mix of lipase, amylase, and protease, formulated in enteric-coated microspheres/minitablets specifically to survive gastric acid before releasing enzymes in the duodenum, directly mirroring the endogenous zymogen-activation timing established in GI Atlas Vol. 2's Pancreatic Exocrine Secretion section. Used for exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis — Vol. 2) and dosed to the meal's fat content, since lipase is the enzyme activity most clinically rate-limiting for symptom control (steatorrhea). Effectiveness depends on adequate gastric acid suppression in some patients — a very acidic stomach can dissolve the enteric coating prematurely, so concurrent PPI therapy is sometimes added specifically to protect the enzyme delivery mechanism, not for any independent acid-suppression indication.

BezlotoxumabA Monoclonal Antibody Against a Toxin, Not the Bacterium

A monoclonal antibody neutralizing C. difficile toxin B directly — unlike the antibiotics above, it doesn't kill bacteria at all; it binds and neutralizes the toxin responsible for mucosal damage, reducing the risk of recurrent infection when given alongside standard antibiotic treatment of an active episode. A genuinely different therapeutic strategy — targeting the pathogenic product rather than the organism producing it — worth contrasting directly against the antibiotic mechanisms in this section.

GLP-1/GIP-Based TherapiesA Pharmacology Postscript

Semaglutide, liraglutide, and tirzepatide (a dual GLP-1/GIP agonist) were introduced mechanistically in GI Atlas Vol. 1 — worth flagging here from a pure pharmacology-practice standpoint: their delayed gastric emptying effect (an on-target, expected consequence of the physiologic mechanism, not an idiosyncratic side effect) is the basis for current pre-procedural holding guidance before sedation/anesthesia, given aspiration risk from unexpectedly retained gastric contents despite standard NPO fasting intervals.

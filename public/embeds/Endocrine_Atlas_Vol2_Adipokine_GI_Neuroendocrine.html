Vol. 2 · Axis 1 · Adrenal Medulla

Catecholamines — Epinephrine, Norepinephrine, Dopamine

Not a hypothalamic-pituitary axis — the adrenal medulla is directly innervated by preganglionic sympathetic neurons, making it functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion that secretes rather than synapses.

ArchitectureA Sympathetic Ganglion That Secretes

Chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla are embryologically neural crest-derived — essentially postganglionic sympathetic neurons that lost their axons and gained a direct blood supply. They're activated by preganglionic sympathetic fibers releasing acetylcholine onto nicotinic receptors — one synapse fewer than the rest of the sympathetic chain, which is why the adrenal medullary response is faster and more diffuse (systemic hormone) than direct sympathetic nerve-to-organ signaling (localized neurotransmitter).

The human adrenal medulla secretes predominantly epinephrine (~80%) over norepinephrine (~20%) — the reverse ratio from sympathetic nerve terminals elsewhere in the body, which release norepinephrine almost exclusively as a neurotransmitter (not into systemic circulation in physiologically significant amounts).

Why cortisol and epinephrine are physiologically linked
The final enzyme, phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT), is induced by locally high cortisol concentration — adrenal medullary chromaffin cells sit bathed in cortisol draining directly from the adjacent cortex before it reaches systemic circulation (a portal-type vascular arrangement). This is why epinephrine synthesis specifically requires an intact, cortisol-producing adrenal cortex, and why extramedullary chromaffin tissue (organ of Zuckerkandl, some paragangliomas) — lacking this local cortisol bath — produces predominantly norepinephrine, not epinephrine. Tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting step, is itself feedback-inhibited by norepinephrine/epinephrine and acutely activated by sympathetic stimulation via phosphorylation.

GeneticsPheochromocytoma / Paraganglioma Susceptibility

Hereditary Pheochromocytoma/Paraganglioma Syndromes RET (MEN2) · VHL · SDHB/SDHC/SDHD · NF1
All autosomal dominant · ~30–40% of pheochromocytomas are hereditary

Roughly a third to nearly half of pheochromocytomas/paragangliomas arise from a germline mutation — a substantially higher hereditary fraction than most adult solid tumors, which is why current guidelines recommend genetic testing be offered to essentially all patients with a confirmed diagnosis, not just those with a suggestive family history. SDHB mutations carry the highest malignant/metastatic potential and are particularly associated with extra-adrenal paragangliomas. MEN2 (RET)-associated pheochromocytomas are more often bilateral and adrenal (rarely extra-adrenal), and — because MEN2 chromaffin tissue retains the cortisol-bathing relationship — tend to secrete predominantly epinephrine, in contrast to SDHx-associated tumors, which more often secrete predominantly norepinephrine or are biochemically silent.

Why ACE inhibitors raise creatinine and why that's often expected
Because angiotensin II preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole to protect GFR when renal perfusion falls, blocking its formation (ACEi/ARB) removes that protection — GFR falls and creatinine rises, typically by an expected/acceptable margin (~≤30%) reflecting the drug working as intended, not necessarily kidney injury. A rise beyond that threshold, or a rise in the setting of bilateral renal artery stenosis (where GFR is critically efferent-arteriolar-tone-dependent), is the scenario to watch for.

Aldosterone

Aldosterone acts on the mineralocorticoid receptor (nuclear, same superfamily as glucocorticoid receptor — see Volume 1 Foundational Principles for the shared mechanism) in the renal distal tubule/collecting duct: upregulates the apical ENaC channel and basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase → Na⁺ reabsorption, K⁺ secretion, and (via the resulting electrochemical gradient) H⁺ secretion through the intercalated cell H⁺-ATPase.

Aldosterone's other trigger: potassium

Zona glomerulosa cells also directly sense serum K⁺ — independent of angiotensin II — making aldosterone one of the few hormones with two structurally unrelated physiologic triggers (RAAS-driven and K⁺-driven), which is part of why aldosterone can rise appropriately in hyperkalemia even with a suppressed renin-angiotensin system.

ACTH's permissive role

ACTH provides acute permissive/trophic support to the zona glomerulosa but is not the primary chronic regulator (that's angiotensin II and K⁺) — this is why isolated ACTH deficiency (secondary adrenal insufficiency) causes cortisol deficiency without the severe salt-wasting seen in primary adrenal failure, where the entire cortex, including the RAAS-responsive glomerulosa, is destroyed.

GeneticsGenetic Disease Correlations

Glucocorticoid-Remediable Aldosteronism (Familial Hyperaldosteronism Type I) Chimeric CYP11B1/CYP11B2 gene · 8q24
Autosomal dominant

An unequal crossover fuses the ACTH-responsive promoter of CYP11B1 to the coding sequence of CYP11B2 (aldosterone synthase) — the result is aldosterone synthase expressed under ACTH control instead of angiotensin II/K⁺ control. Presents with early-onset hypertension and hypokalemia, suppressed renin, elevated aldosterone — and, diagnostically, the hypertension responds to glucocorticoid suppression of ACTH (low-dose dexamethasone), since that's what's actually driving aldosterone production.

Liddle Syndrome SCNN1B / SCNN1G (ENaC subunits) · 16p12
Autosomal dominant

Gain-of-function mutation causes constitutive ENaC activation independent of aldosterone — clinically mimics hyperaldosteronism (hypertension, hypokalemia) but with suppressed renin and suppressed aldosterone (since the channel no longer needs the hormone). Responds to ENaC blockers (amiloride, triamterene) rather than mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (spironolactone), since the receptor itself is not the problem.

Bartter & Gitelman Syndromes Bartter: NKCC2/ROMK/CLC-Kb (loop) · Gitelman: NCC (distal convoluted tubule)
Autosomal recessive

Loss-of-function transporter mutations mimicking chronic loop diuretic (Bartter) or thiazide diuretic (Gitelman) use — salt-wasting drives secondary hyperreninemic hyperaldosteronism with hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis, but normal-to-low blood pressure (unlike the hypertensive mineralocorticoid-excess syndromes above), since the primary defect is renal salt wasting rather than volume/mineralocorticoid excess.

🍽️ Lifestyle Modifiers — RAAS / Aldosterone
Dietary sodium
EstablishedCore renal physiology: low sodium intake activates renin/angiotensin II/aldosterone to conserve sodium; high intake suppresses the axis. This is the physiologic basis for low-sodium diet as first-line nonpharmacologic hypertension management, and for why RAAS activity can't be interpreted without knowing recent salt intake.
Dietary potassium
EstablishedDirectly stimulates zona glomerulosa aldosterone release independent of renin — part of why high-potassium diets (DASH-pattern) have a blood-pressure-lowering effect distinct from sodium restriction alone.
Licorice / glycyrrhizic acid
EstablishedInhibits 11β-HSD2 (see Volume 1, HPA section) causing cortisol-driven mineralocorticoid receptor activation — a genuine, well-documented cause of hypertension and hypokalemia with low renin and aldosterone (not a RAAS effect, but included here because it's commonly mistaken for one).
"Adrenal support" herbal blends for aldosterone balance
Not supportedMarketed supplement claims to "balance" aldosterone or "support adrenal RAAS function" are not backed by controlled clinical trial evidence — sodium/potassium intake and, where indicated, guideline-directed medical therapy are the evidence-based levers.
Vol. 2 · Axis 3 · Adipose Tissue as an Endocrine Organ

Adipokines — Leptin, Adiponectin, Resistin

Fat is not inert storage — it's one of the body's largest endocrine organs, and the hormones it releases report body energy status directly to the hypothalamus.

LeptinThe Adiposity Signal

Adipocytes
(proportional to fat mass)
→Leptin
Arcuate nucleus
(hypothalamus)
→
↓ appetite, ↑ energy expenditure

Leptin is secreted roughly in proportion to fat mass and signals through a class I cytokine receptor (JAK2/STAT3 pathway — mechanistically similar to the GH receptor system in Foundational Principles). In the arcuate nucleus, leptin activates POMC/CART neurons (anorexigenic — reduce appetite) and inhibits NPY/AgRP neurons (orexigenic — stimulate appetite). Falling leptin (starvation, low fat mass) does the opposite, and also suppresses the HPG, HPT, and immune axes — leptin is part of why severe caloric restriction or very low body fat causes hypothalamic amenorrhea and relative hypothyroidism (non-thyroidal illness-like adaptation): the body reads low leptin as a starvation signal and down-regulates energy-expensive reproductive and metabolic function.

Leptin resistance, not leptin deficiency, drives most human obesity
Contrary to the original hypothesis motivating leptin's discovery, most people with obesity have elevated, not deficient, leptin levels — the hypothalamus becomes resistant to leptin's signal (analogous to insulin resistance), which is why exogenous leptin therapy failed as a general obesity treatment and remains useful only in the rare monogenic leptin-deficient population and in lipodystrophy syndromes (where adipose tissue itself, and therefore leptin production, is absent).

AdiponectinThe Insulin-Sensitizing Adipokine

Unlike leptin, adiponectin runs in the opposite direction relative to fat mass — levels are lower in obesity and insulin resistance, and rise with weight loss. Acts via AdipoR1 (muscle, activates AMPK) and AdipoR2 (liver, activates PPAR-α) to increase fatty acid oxidation and insulin sensitivity, and reduce hepatic gluconeogenesis. This inverse relationship with adiposity is why low adiponectin is studied as an early biomarker of metabolic risk, and why thiazolidinediones (PPAR-γ agonists) raise adiponectin as part of their insulin-sensitizing mechanism.

ResistinThe Less-Settled Player

First described in rodents as a factor linking obesity to insulin resistance (hence the name) — human data are considerably less consistent than the mouse model suggested, with the source tissue itself differing (rodent adipocytes vs. predominantly human macrophages/monocytes). Elevated resistin correlates with inflammation and insulin resistance in some human studies, but its role is generally regarded as a less clinically actionable, more research-stage marker than leptin or adiponectin.

🍽️ Lifestyle Modifiers — Adipokines
Weight loss (fat mass reduction)
EstablishedReliably lowers leptin and raises adiponectin, proportional to fat mass change — the falling leptin component is part of why weight loss triggers compensatory hunger and metabolic adaptation, a well-documented physiologic headwind in sustained weight management.
Aerobic exercise training
EstablishedConsistently raises adiponectin and improves insulin sensitivity independent of weight change in multiple RCTs — one of the more robust exercise-hormone findings in this category.
Resistance exercise
Mixed evidenceSome trials show resistance training reduces leptin and interleukin-6 while raising adiponectin, but effect sizes are smaller and less consistent than for adiponectin's response to aerobic training.
Sleep restriction
Mixed evidenceThe original Spiegel et al. (2004) trial found sleep restriction lowered leptin substantially in young men, but subsequent controlled-feeding studies and a 2025 meta-analysis found no significant leptin/ghrelin change once energy balance is tightly controlled — the effect appears real but smaller and more context-dependent than the original landmark study suggested.
Specific "leptin-boosting" foods (supplement marketing)
Not supportedNo specific food has been shown in controlled human trials to meaningfully raise leptin sensitivity or output independent of overall fat mass and energy balance — treat individual "leptin superfood" claims skeptically.
Vol. 2 · Axis 4 · Gastrointestinal Endocrine System

GI & Incretin Hormones

The gut is the body's largest diffuse endocrine organ — ghrelin and the incretins (GLP-1, GIP) now sit at the center of modern obesity and diabetes pharmacology.

GhrelinThe Hunger Hormone

Secreted by gastric X/A-like (P/D1) cells in the fundus, rising before meals and falling sharply after eating — the only circulating hormone with a well-established, appetite-stimulating (orexigenic) role. Acts on the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus in the opposite direction from leptin: activates NPY/AgRP neurons. Also acts as the endogenous ligand for the growth hormone secretagogue receptor — meaning ghrelin directly stimulates GH release from the pituitary, an appetite-metabolism link that's part of the physiologic rationale behind ghrelin-mimetic drugs studied for cachexia.

Why bariatric surgery suppresses hunger beyond simple restriction
Sleeve gastrectomy removes the ghrelin-producing gastric fundus directly — circulating ghrelin drops substantially and durably post-op, which is now understood as a major contributor to the procedure's appetite-suppressing and weight-loss effect, distinct from and additive to the purely mechanical volume-restriction explanation.

Classic TrioCCK, Gastrin, Secretin

Cholecystokinin (CCK)

I-cells (duodenum/jejunum), triggered by fat and protein. Stimulates gallbladder contraction, pancreatic enzyme secretion, and slows gastric emptying; contributes to meal-related satiety.

Gastrin

G-cells (antrum), triggered by gastric distension, vagal input (GRP), and luminal peptides/amino acids — inhibited by antral pH <3 (negative feedback). Stimulates parietal cell HCl secretion (directly and via ECL-cell histamine release) and gastric mucosal growth. A gastrin-secreting tumor (gastrinoma, often part of MEN1 — see Volume 1 calcium section for the MEN1 triad) causes Zollinger-Ellison syndrome.

Secretin

S-cells (duodenum), triggered by luminal acid (pH <4.5). Stimulates pancreatic and biliary bicarbonate secretion to neutralize gastric acid entering the duodenum, and inhibits gastrin/gastric acid secretion — a direct negative-feedback partner to gastrin.

Motilin & PYY

Motilin drives the interdigestive migrating motor complex (fasting-state GI "housekeeping" contractions) — the physiologic basis for erythromycin's prokinetic (motilin-receptor agonist) off-label use. PYY (ileum/colon L-cells, co-secreted with GLP-1) is a strong satiety signal, released in proportion to caloric load and, like ghrelin, implicated in bariatric surgery's appetite-suppressing effect (levels rise substantially post-sleeve/bypass).

Vol. 2 · Axis 5 · Oxygen-Sensing & Red Cell Production

Erythropoietin & Erythropoiesis

The kidney's real-time oxygen sensor, the HIF-2α switch that turns individual fibroblasts on and off, and the full JAK2-STAT5 signaling cascade that turns a single hormone into two units of blood.

SourceWhere EPO Actually Comes From

~90% of adult erythropoietin is produced by renal erythropoietin-producing cells (REPCs) — peritubular interstitial fibroblast-like cells in the deep cortex and outer medulla, distinct from the tubular epithelium itself. The remaining ~10% comes from hepatocytes (a much larger fraction in the fetus, before renal EPO production matures — clinically relevant since neonates and infants with liver disease can have a different anemia physiology than adults with kidney disease).

EPO output is all-or-none per cell, not graded
Individual REPCs behave as a binary switch — "OFF-REPs" produce no EPO at baseline; under hypoxic stress they convert to "ON-REPs" producing a fixed quantity of EPO mRNA each. Total circulating EPO rises not because each cell makes more hormone, but because more cells switch on in proportion to the degree of hypoxia. This explains why chronic kidney disease causes a graded, progressive fall in EPO output as functioning REPC mass is lost to fibrosis — it's a cell-number problem, not a per-cell signaling defect, which is part of why recombinant EPO (rather than a signaling-pathway drug) became the direct replacement therapy.

EpoR is a class I cytokine receptor with no intrinsic kinase activity — it works exactly like the GH receptor mechanism introduced in Volume 1 Foundational Principles, via associated JAK2. The dominant physiologic effect is rescuing erythroid progenitors from apoptosis (rather than simply "make more cells divide") — EPO withdrawal triggers rapid apoptosis of CFU-E/proerythroblast stage cells, so EPO's job is best understood as a survival signal that permits an already-committed progenitor pool to complete maturation, with the size of that rescued pool determining red cell output.

EPO-responsiveness is confined to a specific developmental window (CFU-E through early normoblast) — this is why the reticulocyte count rises only after a lag of several days following an EPO stimulus (exogenous administration, altitude, hemorrhage): existing downstream cells must still finish maturing on their own timeline, and the EPO effect is only visible once a new cohort has been rescued from apoptosis and completed the several-day maturation sequence. This lag is the reason reticulocyte count, not hemoglobin itself, is the earliest lab marker of an appropriate marrow response.

ClearanceEPO Breakdown

Circulating EPO is cleared primarily by receptor-mediated endocytosis and degradation within erythroid progenitor cells themselves (the same cells it acts on) plus hepatic metabolism, with a native plasma half-life of only ~5–6 hours. This short half-life is why recombinant erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (epoetin alfa, darbepoetin, longer-acting pegylated forms) are engineered with modified glycosylation to extend half-life and reduce dosing frequency in clinical use.

Genetics & Acquired Correlations

Polycythemia Vera JAK2 V617F (~95%) or JAK2 exon 12 mutations
Acquired somatic mutation · Myeloproliferative neoplasm

A constitutively active JAK2 kinase makes erythroid progenitors EPO-independent — they proliferate maximally regardless of circulating EPO level. The diagnostic signature follows directly from the mechanism: elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit with a low (suppressed) serum EPO — the marrow is making red cells on its own, so the kidney's oxygen sensors, seeing a high hematocrit, appropriately shut EPO production down. This EPO level is the single most useful lab discriminator from secondary erythrocytosis.

Secondary / Familial Erythrocytosis VHL (Chuvash polycythemia), EPOR (truncating gain-of-function), EGLN1/PHD2, HIF2A
Various — autosomal recessive (Chuvash/VHL) or dominant (EPOR, HIF2A)

Mutations anywhere in the oxygen-sensing pathway (VHL, the PHD enzymes, HIF-2α itself) or in the EPO receptor can cause inappropriate EPO overproduction or EPO-independent receptor signaling — presenting as polycythemia with elevated or inappropriately normal EPO (unlike JAK2-driven polycythemia vera), a key distinguishing lab feature when JAK2 testing is negative in a polycythemic patient.

EPO-Secreting Tumors (Paraneoplastic Erythrocytosis) Acquired — renal cell carcinoma, hepatocellular carcinoma, cerebellar hemangioblastoma, uterine leiomyoma
Acquired, paraneoplastic

Tumors either ectopically produce EPO or, in the case of renal cell carcinoma and VHL-associated hemangioblastoma, disrupt local oxygen-sensing (loss of VHL function) to drive autonomous EPO transcription independent of true systemic hypoxia. Presents with elevated hemoglobin and elevated (not suppressed) EPO — worth considering in unexplained polycythemia with a negative JAK2 mutation.

Anemia of Chronic Kidney Disease Acquired — progressive REPC loss to interstitial fibrosis
Acquired

The most common cause of EPO deficiency encountered clinically — a graded fall in EPO output as functioning peritubular fibroblast mass is lost to fibrosis, typically becoming clinically significant around CKD stage 3–4. Treated with erythropoiesis-stimulating agents or, more recently, oral HIF-PHD inhibitors; iron sufficiency (ferritin and transferrin saturation) must be confirmed before/during treatment, since iron-deficient marrow cannot respond to EPO stimulation regardless of dose — a common cause of apparent ESA "resistance."

🍽️ Lifestyle Modifiers — Erythropoietin / Erythropoiesis
Altitude exposure/training
EstablishedHypoxia reliably and substantially raises serum EPO (the direct HIF-2α mechanism above) — well-documented across dozens of controlled altitude-training studies. However, whether this translates into a meaningfully larger hemoglobin mass or performance gain than equivalent sea-level training is genuinely debated in the sports-science literature — individual variability in the EPO response is substantial, and some reviews find no consistent hematological benefit despite the reliable EPO rise itself.
Iron sufficiency
EstablishedIron is an obligate cofactor for hemoglobin synthesis (and for the PHD enzymes themselves) — iron-deficient erythropoiesis blunts the marrow's ability to respond to a given EPO level, which is why iron studies are checked before attributing anemia purely to an EPO problem, and why iron repletion alone often raises hemoglobin without any EPO-directed therapy.
Endurance exercise training (sea level)
Mixed evidenceRegular endurance training increases total red cell mass and plasma volume over time, but this is driven by a combination of factors (including plasma volume expansion diluting hematocrit acutely) rather than a large, sustained resting EPO elevation at sea level — the altitude-hypoxia EPO stimulus is considerably stronger than the exercise-alone stimulus.
"Natural EPO-boosting" supplements (beet juice, cordyceps, etc.)
Not supportedNo dietary supplement has controlled-trial evidence for meaningfully raising endogenous EPO or hemoglobin in iron-replete, non-hypoxic individuals — claims marketed around these products conflate unrelated performance mechanisms (e.g., beet juice's nitrate/nitric-oxide effect on vascular efficiency) with actual erythropoietic stimulation.
Verify before relying
Recombinant EPO/ESA use is banned in competitive sport by WADA and detectable via isoelectric focusing/blood passport monitoring — worth knowing if evaluating unexplained polycythemia in an athletic patient alongside the genetic causes above.
Vol. 2 · Axis 6 · Posterior Pituitary

ADH (Vasopressin) & Oxytocin

Both hormones are synthesized in the hypothalamus, not the pituitary itself — the posterior pituitary is just an axonal storage/release terminal, unlike the anterior pituitary's own hormone-producing cells.

ArchitectureNeurohypophysis, Not a True Gland

Both hormones are synthesized as prohormones in magnocellular neuron cell bodies — ADH mainly in the supraoptic nucleus, oxytocin mainly in the paraventricular nucleus (with meaningful overlap in both) — packaged with a carrier protein called neurophysin, and transported down long axons through the pituitary stalk for storage and release directly into systemic circulation at axon terminals in the posterior pituitary. There's no local synthesis or independent gland tissue in the posterior pituitary itself — it is anatomically pituitary but functionally hypothalamus.

OxytocinTwo Classic Reflexes — Both Positive Feedback

Milk let-down reflex

Suckling → afferent signal to hypothalamus → oxytocin release → myoepithelial cell contraction around mammary alveoli → milk ejection. Classic positive feedback: continued suckling sustains the reflex until the infant stops.

Ferguson reflex (labor)

Cervical/vaginal stretch → oxytocin release → uterine smooth muscle contraction → further cervical stretch → more oxytocin. This escalating positive-feedback loop is the physiologic rationale for exogenous oxytocin (Pitocin) as a labor induction/augmentation agent.

Beyond the classic reproductive reflexes, oxytocin has receptor-mediated CNS effects on social affiliation, trust, and stress-axis modulation (it dampens ACTH/cortisol release under some conditions) — but human behavioral evidence is considerably more mixed and context-dependent than the popular "bonding hormone" framing suggests; effects vary with social context, dose, and route of administration, and several meta-analyses of intranasal oxytocin trials describe the behavioral evidence base as inconsistent.

Vol. 2 · Axis 7 · Anterior Pituitary — Growth & Lactation

Prolactin & the GH/IGF-1 Axis

Prolactin is the one anterior pituitary hormone under tonic inhibitory control by default — the opposite regulatory logic from every other axis in this atlas.

ProlactinThe Exception to the Rule

Every other anterior pituitary hormone in this atlas requires a stimulatory hypothalamic releasing hormone to be secreted. Prolactin is the exception: the hypothalamus tonically inhibits prolactin release via dopamine (acting on lactotroph D2 receptors, Gi-coupled). Cut the pituitary stalk, or block dopamine, and prolactin goes up — the opposite of what happens to every other anterior pituitary hormone under the same disconnection.

Why this matters at the bedside
A pituitary stalk lesion (compression from a large non-functioning macroadenoma, trauma, surgery) classically causes a mild-to-moderate prolactin elevation from lost dopamine inhibition — "stalk effect." This is a key diagnostic trap: a modestly elevated prolactin (roughly <200 ng/mL) in the setting of a large pituitary mass could be either a true prolactinoma or stalk effect from a different, non-prolactin-secreting mass compressing the stalk — very high levels (often >200 ng/mL) are much more specific for a genuine prolactin-secreting macroadenoma. Also explains why dopamine antagonist drugs (many antipsychotics, metoclopramide) reliably cause hyperprolactinemia (galactorrhea, amenorrhea) as a predictable class effect, and why dopamine agonists (cabergoline, bromocriptine) are first-line therapy for prolactinoma — unusual among pituitary tumors in being medically, rather than surgically, first-line.

Prolactin's other major physiologic action: it suppresses the HPG axis (inhibits GnRH pulsatility) — the mechanism behind lactational amenorrhea and behind the hypogonadism seen in any hyperprolactinemic state, independent of cause.

GH secretion is doubly controlled — stimulated by GHRH, inhibited by somatostatin (the same paracrine brake introduced in the Volume 1 pancreatic section) — and its major growth-promoting actions are largely indirect, mediated by hepatic IGF-1 production rather than GH acting on the growth plate directly (GH does have some direct, IGF-1-independent metabolic effects — see below). GH signals through the same JAK2/STAT5 cytokine-receptor mechanism introduced in the EPO section above; IGF-1 itself signals through a receptor tyrosine kinase structurally homologous to the insulin receptor (see Volume 1 Foundational Principles), which is why very high IGF-1 states can produce mild insulin-like metabolic cross-reactivity.

🍽️ Lifestyle Modifiers — GH/IGF-1 & Prolactin
Slow-wave (deep) sleep
EstablishedThe largest GH pulses of the day occur during slow-wave sleep in the first sleep cycle — a robust, repeatedly replicated finding in sleep endocrinology; chronic sleep restriction/fragmentation measurably blunts nocturnal GH secretion.
High-intensity resistance/interval exercise
EstablishedAcute high-intensity exercise (especially with short rest intervals and higher lactate accumulation) reliably produces a large transient GH pulse in controlled studies — one of the more consistent acute exercise-hormone findings.
Fasting / caloric restriction
EstablishedShort-term fasting raises GH pulse amplitude (a conserved evolutionary mechanism to mobilize fat stores while sparing protein) even as it lowers IGF-1 — a good example of GH and IGF-1 dissociating rather than moving together, since the liver's IGF-1 output also depends on adequate nutritional/protein status, not GH alone.
"Natural HGH releaser" supplements (amino acid blends, GABA, melatonin combos)
Not supportedOver-the-counter "HGH releaser" supplements have not demonstrated clinically meaningful GH or IGF-1 elevation in controlled trials at typical consumer doses — the sleep and exercise levers above have far stronger evidence.

Melatonin

Synthesized by the pineal gland on a signal relayed from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (the master circadian clock) via a multisynaptic pathway through the superior cervical ganglion — an unusually indirect route for an endocrine signal, reflecting melatonin's role as the body's "darkness hormone" rather than a classic feedback-regulated axis hormone. Light input directly suppresses AANAT activity; darkness permits it — melatonin secretion is essentially an inverted photograph of ambient light exposure, peaking in the middle of the biological night.

Acts on MT1 and MT2 Gi-coupled receptors, concentrated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus itself (a feedback loop onto the clock that generates the signal) and elsewhere. Physiologic roles: entrains circadian rhythm, promotes sleep onset, and — a less widely appreciated action — provides tonic inhibitory input to the HPG axis, part of why seasonal breeders in nature time reproduction to day length and why pathologically elevated melatonin (rare pineal disease) has been associated with delayed puberty.

proBNP is cleaved into equimolar active BNP and inactive NT-proBNP — clinically, NT-proBNP is often preferred as the lab marker specifically because it has a longer half-life (~60–120 min vs. ~20 min for BNP) and is cleared renally rather than via NPR-C receptor-mediated clearance/neutral endopeptidase degradation (the route for active BNP), making it a more stable, reproducible assay target, though it also means NT-proBNP rises more with reduced renal function independent of cardiac status — an important interpretive caveat in CKD patients.

Neprilysin inhibition — why ARNI drugs raise BNP but not NT-proBNP
Neprilysin (neutral endopeptidase) is the primary enzyme degrading active BNP (and several other vasoactive peptides) but does not touch NT-proBNP. Sacubitril, the neprilysin inhibitor component of ARNI therapy (sacubitril/valsartan) for heart failure, therefore causes measured BNP to rise as an expected drug effect unrelated to worsening heart failure — NT-proBNP remains the reliable trending marker in patients on ARNI therapy for exactly this reason.

Paracrine noteProstaglandins — the Atlas's One Purely Local Signaling Family

Prostaglandins don't fit the endocrine framework used throughout this atlas — they're synthesized on demand from membrane phospholipid arachidonic acid (via COX-1/COX-2), act locally at or near their site of synthesis, and are rapidly degraded (seconds to minutes), so they essentially never function as classic circulating hormones. Included here only because they illustrate the paracrine/autocrine signaling mode introduced in Volume 1's Foundational Principles: examples include PGE2 (fever, pain sensitization, gastric mucosal protection, ductus arteriosus patency in the fetus), PGI2/prostacyclin (vasodilation, platelet inhibition), and thromboxane A2 (vasoconstriction, platelet aggregation) — the opposing prostacyclin/thromboxane balance is the physiologic logic behind low-dose aspirin's antiplatelet effect (selective, irreversible COX-1/thromboxane suppression in platelets, which cannot resynthesize the enzyme, versus recoverable endothelial prostacyclin production).

Evening light exposure (especially blue-wavelength)
EstablishedSuppresses AANAT activity and delays melatonin onset in a dose- and wavelength-dependent manner — one of the best-replicated findings in circadian biology, and the direct physiologic rationale for evening screen-light reduction/blue-light filtering recommendations.
Morning bright light exposure
EstablishedAdvances circadian phase and reinforces the light-dark entrainment signal to the suprachiasmatic nucleus — core, well-replicated chronobiology, and first-line for circadian rhythm disorders and some cases of seasonal affective disorder.
Tart cherry juice
Mixed evidenceTart cherries do contain measurable melatonin and small RCTs show modest sleep-duration/quality improvements, but trials are few, small, and industry-funded in several cases — a plausible but not firmly established effect.
"Detox" or "circadian reset" supplement blends
Not supportedProprietary multi-ingredient "circadian reset" blends generally lack trial data as combination products — light timing remains the best-evidenced lever by a wide margin.

Thyroid Panel

TSH, free T4, free T3, and antibodies — mapped onto the HPT axis feedback loop from Volume 1.

TSHThe First-Line Screening Test

Standard reference range ~0.4–4.0 mIU/L, though this shifts with age (rises modestly in older adults — age-specific ranges reduce over-diagnosis of subclinical hypothyroidism in the elderly), pregnancy (lower, especially first trimester, from hCG's weak TSH-receptor cross-reactivity — see Volume 1), and some experts argue for a lower upper limit (~2.5) in specific contexts, though this isn't universally adopted.

PatternInterpretationCommon causes
High TSH + low free T4Overt primary hypothyroidismHashimoto's thyroiditis (most common), post-thyroidectomy/radioactive iodine, iodine deficiency, lithium, amiodarone, subacute thyroiditis (late hypothyroid phase)
High TSH + normal free T4Subclinical hypothyroidismSame causes, earlier/milder stage — monitor q3–6 months; treatment threshold debated, generally favored if TSH >10 or symptomatic/pregnant
Low TSH + high free T4/T3Overt hyperthyroidismGraves' disease (most common), toxic multinodular goiter, toxic adenoma, subacute/postpartum thyroiditis (early hyperthyroid phase), excess exogenous levothyroxine
Low TSH + normal free T4/T3Subclinical hyperthyroidismSame causes, milder — treatment more strongly favored in patients >65 or with atrial fibrillation/osteoporosis risk given independent cardiovascular and bone effects even at subclinical levels
Low TSH + low free T4Secondary (central) hypothyroidismPituitary/hypothalamic disease — the "paradox" pattern; TSH is low because the pituitary itself is failing, not because thyroid hormone is high
Normal/low TSH in critical illnessNon-thyroidal illness syndrome (see Volume 1)Not true thyroid disease — avoid over-testing/treating during acute illness

AntibodiesAnti-TPO & TSH-Receptor Antibodies

Anti-TPO (and anti-thyroglobulin)

Positive in Hashimoto's thyroiditis; predicts progression to overt hypothyroidism in patients with subclinical disease. Not useful as a treatment-monitoring tool — check once for diagnosis, not serially.

TSH-receptor antibody (TRAb/TSI)

Positive in Graves' disease; useful both diagnostically and for predicting relapse risk after a course of antithyroid drug therapy — a persistently high titer at the end of treatment predicts higher relapse likelihood.

MonitoringOn Levothyroxine or Antithyroid Therapy

Levothyroxine: recheck TSH 6–8 weeks after any dose change (steady state takes ~5 half-lives of T4, ~5 weeks) Stable replacement: recheck annually Pregnancy: recheck each trimester — requirements typically rise Antithyroid drugs (methimazole/PTU): TSH lags behind free T4/T3 early in treatment — trend free T4/T3, not TSH, in the first 2–3 months
Pitfall: total vs. free
Total T4/T3 move with binding-protein changes (pregnancy, oral estrogen, liver disease — see Volume 1) even when the patient is euthyroid. Always order free T4/T3, not total, unless specifically working around an assay limitation.

Adrenal, Cortisol & RAAS Labs

Builds directly on Volume 1's HPA axis and Volume 2's RAAS sections, plus the HPA-suppression testing already covered in Volume 3a.

CortisolCauses of High & Low

PatternCommon causes
High cortisolCushing's syndrome (exogenous steroid use is the most common cause overall; endogenous: pituitary ACTH-secreting adenoma = Cushing's disease, ectopic ACTH from small cell lung cancer/carcinoid, adrenal adenoma/carcinoma), physiologic stress (illness, surgery, pregnancy — via elevated CBG), depression/severe psychiatric illness (pseudo-Cushing's)
Low cortisolPrimary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's — autoimmune most common in developed countries, TB historically/globally, adrenal hemorrhage), secondary/tertiary adrenal insufficiency (pituitary/hypothalamic disease, or — far more commonly in practice — exogenous steroid-induced HPA suppression, see Volume 3a)
Primary vs. secondary — the discriminator
Pair cortisol with ACTH: high ACTH + low cortisol = primary (adrenal) failure, with the adrenal unable to respond despite maximal pituitary drive (also look for hyperpigmentation — Volume 1). Low/inappropriately-normal ACTH + low cortisol = secondary/tertiary (pituitary/hypothalamic) failure, or exogenous suppression.

Dynamic testing: cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test — peak cortisol ≥18 mcg/dL (500 nmol/L) at 30–60 min indicates an intact axis; a blunted response confirms adrenal insufficiency (primary or long-standing secondary, since chronic ACTH deficiency eventually causes adrenal atrophy too). See Volume 3a for the specific thresholds used when testing recovery from exogenous steroid-induced suppression.

Aldosterone & ReninThe Ratio Is the Test

Neither aldosterone nor renin alone is as useful as the aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) for screening primary hyperaldosteronism.

PatternInterpretation
High aldosterone, low/suppressed renin, elevated ARRPrimary hyperaldosteronism (adrenal adenoma/hyperplasia) — the adrenal is autonomously overproducing, suppressing renin via negative feedback (see Volume 2 RAAS section)
High aldosterone, high reninSecondary hyperaldosteronism — appropriate RAAS activation from renal artery stenosis, diuretic use, heart failure, or true volume depletion
Low aldosterone, low reninSuggests a non-RAAS mineralocorticoid-excess process (see Volume 3a's licorice/11β-HSD2 discussion) or Liddle syndrome (Volume 2) — aldosterone itself is appropriately suppressed because the problem lies downstream
Medication interference
Spironolactone, eplerenone, and high-dose NSAIDs distort the ARR and should be held (where clinically safe) for several weeks before testing; beta-blockers can falsely elevate the ratio by suppressing renin more than aldosterone.

MonitoringPractical Frequency

Adrenal insufficiency on replacement: clinical response is primary; no lab perfectly tracks adequacy of hydrocortisone dosing — avoid over-relying on random cortisol levels while on replacement Post-steroid-taper HPA recovery: morning cortisol, spaced weeks apart — see Volume 3a thresholds Primary hyperaldosteronism on treatment: potassium and blood pressure response guide titration more than repeat ARR

Calcium, PTH & Vitamin D

Reading the hub-and-spoke calcium axis from Volume 1 as a lab panel — the PTH/calcium pairing is one of the highest-yield two-value interpretations in all of endocrinology.

The Core PairingCalcium + PTH Together

CalciumPTHInterpretation
HighHigh or inappropriately normal (typically >65 pg/mL when calcium is elevated)Primary hyperparathyroidism — autonomous PTH secretion; normal physiology would suppress PTH to <20 pg/mL at this calcium level, so anything higher is inappropriate
HighLow/suppressedPTH-independent hypercalcemia — malignancy (PTHrP-mediated or bony metastasis), granulomatous disease (sarcoidosis — extrarenal 1α-hydroxylase activity in macrophages), vitamin D toxicity, milk-alkali syndrome
LowHighSecondary hyperparathyroidism — appropriate compensatory response, most often vitamin D deficiency or CKD (see Volume 1)
LowLow/inappropriately normalHypoparathyroidism — post-thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy (most common), autoimmune, DiGeorge syndrome (Volume 1), hypomagnesemia (magnesium is required for PTH secretion — always check magnesium in unexplained hypoparathyroidism)
NormalPersistently elevated (×2 over ≥6 months)Normocalcemic primary hyperparathyroidism — but first exclude vitamin D deficiency (25-OH-D <30 ng/mL can itself cause a normal-calcium/high-PTH picture that resolves with repletion) before accepting this diagnosis

Vitamin D25-OH-D Thresholds

25-OH-vitamin DStatus
< 20 ng/mLDeficient — commonly associated with secondary hyperparathyroidism
20–29 ng/mLInsufficient
≥ 30 ng/mLSufficient — generally the level needed for maximal PTH suppression and skeletal benefit
Order 25-OH-D, not 1,25-(OH)₂-D, for status
As established in Volume 1: 25-OH-D reflects substrate availability and has a long half-life, making it the correct status marker. 1,25-(OH)₂-D (calcitriol) is tightly regulated and can be normal or paradoxically low even in severe vitamin D deficiency or hypercalcemia of malignancy (renal 1α-hydroxylase gets suppressed) — ordering it to assess "vitamin D status" is a common and misleading error. Reserve 1,25-(OH)₂-D testing for specific scenarios: suspected granulomatous disease (where it's characteristically elevated), CKD-related mineral bone disease, or rare hereditary vitamin D-resistance/1α-hydroxylase disorders.

Correction & ConfoundersAlbumin, Ionized Calcium, Phosphate

Corrected calcium

~40% of serum calcium is albumin-bound — hypoalbuminemia lowers total calcium without changing the physiologically active free fraction. Correct using: corrected Ca = measured Ca + 0.8 × (4.0 − albumin). When in doubt, or in critical illness/acid-base disturbance (which also shifts protein binding), measure ionized calcium directly rather than relying on the correction formula.

Phosphate

Moves opposite to PTH in primary/secondary hyperparathyroidism (PTH is phosphaturic — Volume 1) but tracks with the underlying problem in renal failure (both PTH and phosphate rise as GFR falls). A low phosphate with high FGF23 and normal PTH/calcium points toward the FGF23-driven disorders (XLH, tumor-induced osteomalacia — Volume 1) rather than a PTH-axis problem.

MonitoringPractical Frequency

Vitamin D repletion: recheck 25-OH-D at 3 months, then annually once replete Primary hyperparathyroidism, non-surgical monitoring: calcium + creatinine annually, DEXA every 1–2 years CKD-mineral bone disease: PTH, calcium, phosphate per CKD stage per nephrology protocol — intact PTH assay generation matters more as renal function falls (see below)
Assay generation matters in renal failure
Second-generation ("intact") PTH assays cross-react with inactive C-terminal fragments that accumulate in renal failure, artificially inflating results. Third-generation ("whole PTH" / "bio-intact") assays are more accurate in this setting — worth knowing which generation your lab uses when trending PTH in a dialysis patient.

Glucose & Pancreatic Labs

Glucose, HbA1c, insulin, and C-peptide — reading the beta-cell physiology from Volume 1 as a diagnostic panel.

HbA1cInterpretation & Pitfalls

HbA1cCategory
< 5.7%Normal
5.7–6.4%Prediabetes
≥ 6.5%Diabetes (on two occasions, or one plus symptoms/unequivocal hyperglycemia)
Conditions that make HbA1c unreliable
HbA1c reflects average glucose over the RBC lifespan (~120 days) — anything that shortens or lengthens that lifespan distorts the result independent of true glycemic control. Falsely low: hemolytic anemia, recent significant blood loss/transfusion, pregnancy, CKD with erythropoietin therapy (see Volume 2 EPO section). Falsely high: iron deficiency anemia, splenectomy, older RBC age from any cause. Hemoglobinopathies (sickle cell, thalassemia trait) can interfere directly with certain assay methods — use fructosamine or continuous glucose monitoring data instead when a hemoglobinopathy is known.

Insulin & C-PeptideDistinguishing Endogenous From Exogenous

As established in Volume 1: insulin and C-peptide are cleaved in equimolar amounts from proinsulin, but only C-peptide survives in commercial insulin preparations (which contain no C-peptide).

InsulinC-peptideGlucoseInterpretation
HighSuppressedLowExogenous insulin administration (factitious or accidental)
HighHighLowEndogenous hyperinsulinemia — insulinoma, sulfonylurea use (stimulates the patient's own beta cells, so C-peptide stays elevated), post-bariatric hypoglycemia
LowLowHighInsulin-deficient diabetes (T1DM, or late/severe T2DM with beta-cell exhaustion)
HighHighHighInsulin resistance (early T2DM, metabolic syndrome) — beta cells compensating for peripheral resistance

AutoantibodiesType 1 vs. Type 2 vs. MODY

As covered in Volume 1: anti-GAD65 (most commonly tested), anti-IA-2, and anti-ZnT8 support autoimmune type 1 diabetes. Negative autoantibodies with a strong family history and preserved C-peptide should raise suspicion for MODY (genetic testing warranted, particularly relevant given the sulfonylurea-sensitivity treatment implications discussed in Volume 1).

MonitoringPractical Frequency

HbA1c: every 3 months if not at goal or therapy recently changed; every 6 months if stable at goal Steroid-induced hyperglycemia: see Volume 3a's afternoon/evening glucose-check guidance — morning-only checks miss the pattern Insulinoma workup: 72-hour supervised fast with q6h glucose/insulin/C-peptide is the diagnostic gold standard, not a random level

Catecholamines & Metanephrines

Why metanephrines displaced catecholamines themselves as the preferred pheochromocytoma screening test — a direct application of Volume 2's catecholamine breakdown pathway.

Why Metanephrines, Not Catecholamines

As established in Volume 2: catecholamines are secreted episodically (paroxysmal in pheochromocytoma), so a random plasma catecholamine level drawn between episodes can be falsely normal. Metanephrines (metanephrine, normetanephrine) are produced continuously within tumor cells themselves from ongoing catecholamine synthesis/leakage — independent of whether the tumor is actively "firing" at the moment of the blood draw — making them far more sensitive for screening.

TestSensitivityNotes
Plasma free metanephrinesHighest (~96–100%)Preferred first-line test; best negative predictive value — a normal result effectively rules out pheochromocytoma
24-hour urine fractionated metanephrinesHigh, slightly lower specificity than plasmaUseful alternative or confirmatory test; integrates over a full day, reducing single-draw variability

InterferenceWhat Causes False Positives

Acute physiologic/psychological stress at time of draw Caffeine, nicotine Tricyclic antidepressants, SNRIs (venlafaxine), MAO inhibitors Levodopa, decongestants (pseudoephedrine) Acetaminophen (can interfere with some assay methods) Upright/seated posture during blood draw (draw supine after 20–30 min rest when possible for the most specific result)
Practical approach to a borderline result
Given how many common substances cause false elevation, a mildly elevated plasma metanephrine in a patient on an interfering medication is more often a false positive than a true pheochromocytoma — repeat testing supine, after holding interfering substances where clinically feasible, before proceeding to imaging.

FractionationWhat the Specific Pattern Suggests

As covered in Volume 2: adrenal medullary tumors (with intact local cortisol exposure driving PNMT) tend to produce more metanephrine (epinephrine-derived), while extra-adrenal paragangliomas tend to produce predominantly normetanephrine (norepinephrine-derived) — the fractionated result can hint at tumor location before imaging, and a predominantly normetanephrine pattern should raise suspicion for an extra-adrenal source or an SDHx-associated syndrome (Volume 2).

MonitoringPost-Resection

Metanephrines should normalize within 1–2 weeks post-resection; persistent elevation suggests incomplete resection or metastatic/multifocal disease. Annual biochemical surveillance is reasonable long-term given recurrence risk, especially in hereditary syndromes (Volume 2).

Pituitary Labs — Prolactin & GH/IGF-1

Why IGF-1, not GH itself, is the right screening test for growth hormone excess — directly exploiting the axis kinetics from Volume 2.

ProlactinCauses of Elevation

LevelLikely cause
Mildly elevated (roughly <100 ng/mL)Physiologic (pregnancy, lactation, stress, sleep, nipple stimulation, recent breast exam), medication effect (dopamine antagonists — antipsychotics, metoclopramide; also verapamil, some antidepressants), primary hypothyroidism (TRH cross-stimulation — Volume 1/2), stalk effect from a nearby non-prolactin-secreting mass (see Volume 2)
Markedly elevated (often >200 ng/mL)More specific for a genuine prolactin-secreting macroadenoma
The "hook effect" — a genuine assay pitfall
Extremely high prolactin levels (from a large macroprolactinoma) can occasionally saturate the immunoassay and produce a falsely low or normal reading — a lab artifact called the "hook effect." If a large pituitary mass is seen on imaging with an unexpectedly modest prolactin level, ask the lab to rerun the sample at a 1:100 dilution to rule this out before assuming stalk effect.

GH/IGF-1Why IGF-1 Is the Screening Test

As established in Volume 2: GH is secreted in sharp pulses (largest during slow-wave sleep) with a short half-life — a random GH level is essentially uninterpretable, since it could be drawn during a trough or a pulse. IGF-1 has a stable, long half-life and integrates GH exposure over the preceding days, making it the correct first-line screening test for both acromegaly/gigantism (elevated IGF-1) and adult GH deficiency (low IGF-1, though this needs correlation with clinical context since IGF-1 also falls with malnutrition, hypothyroidism, and liver disease independent of true GH deficiency).

PatternInterpretation
High IGF-1Confirm with oral glucose tolerance test — failure of GH to suppress below ~0.4 ng/mL after glucose load confirms acromegaly (normal physiology suppresses GH with hyperglycemia; autonomous somatotroph tumors don't)
Low IGF-1 + clinical suspicion of GH deficiencyGH stimulation testing (insulin tolerance test, glucagon stimulation, or GHRH-arginine) needed for confirmation — IGF-1 alone is a screen, not a diagnostic test, in this direction

MonitoringPractical Frequency

Prolactinoma on dopamine agonist therapy: recheck prolactin ~monthly until normalized, then periodically; repeat MRI at 12 months or with new symptoms Acromegaly post-treatment: IGF-1 at 12 weeks post-surgery, then periodically; OGTT-GH suppression to confirm biochemical remission GH replacement therapy: IGF-1 is the primary dose-titration marker, checked ~4–8 weeks after dose changes

General Pitfalls & Monitoring Principles

The cross-cutting patterns that repeat across every axis in this atlas — worth internalizing once rather than re-deriving for each hormone.

Pattern 1Binding Proteins Distort Total, Not Free, Hormone

This shows up for cortisol (CBG), thyroid hormone (TBG), and testosterone/estradiol (SHBG) — all covered individually across Volumes 1–3b. The general rule: anything that changes binding-protein production (pregnancy, oral estrogen, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, critical illness, thyroid status itself for SHBG) moves the total hormone level without necessarily reflecting a real change in the free, biologically active fraction. When in doubt about whether an abnormal total hormone level is real, ask what the relevant binding protein is doing.

Pattern 2Pulsatile/Circadian Hormones Need the Right Draw Time

Cortisol — morning draw (peak ~6–8am) Testosterone — morning draw before 10am GH — random level uninterpretable; use IGF-1 instead LH/FSH — less time-critical, but GnRH pulsatility still underlies short-term variability

Any hormone secreted in pulses or with a strong circadian rhythm (see the secretory-pattern discussion in Volume 1 Foundational Principles) will give a misleading result if drawn at the wrong time of day — this is one of the most common preventable sources of diagnostic confusion in this entire atlas.

Pattern 3Primary vs. Secondary — Pair the Gland Hormone With Its Trophic Hormone

The single most reusable diagnostic framework in this atlas: pair the peripheral gland's hormone with the pituitary/hypothalamic hormone driving it.

AxisPeripheral hormone low, trophic hormone highPeripheral hormone low, trophic hormone low/normal
HPTPrimary hypothyroidismSecondary (central) hypothyroidism
HPAPrimary adrenal insufficiencySecondary/tertiary adrenal insufficiency
HPG (male)Primary hypogonadism (testicular failure)Secondary hypogonadism (hypothalamic-pituitary)
HPG (female)Primary ovarian insufficiencySecondary (hypothalamic amenorrhea, pituitary disease)

This "high trophic hormone = the gland itself is failing" vs. "low/normal trophic hormone despite low peripheral hormone = the problem is upstream" logic is the same pattern in every single axis in this atlas.

Pattern 4Acute Illness Distorts Almost Everything

Non-thyroidal illness syndrome (Volume 1), stress-related cortisol/CBG changes (this file), and blunted or exaggerated glucose/insulin dynamics in critical illness all mean that routine endocrine labs drawn during acute hospitalization are frequently misleading. Where possible, defer non-urgent endocrine workup to the outpatient setting once the patient has recovered from the acute illness — a general principle that applies across nearly every hormone axis in this atlas, not just the ones with a dedicated illness-related syndrome name.

Pattern 5Medication Effects Are Everywhere — A Consolidated List

MedicationLab effect
Oral estrogen (OCPs, HRT)↑ TBG, CBG, SHBG → ↑ total T4, total cortisol, total testosterone/estradiol without a true change in free hormone
Biotin (common supplement)Can interfere with many immunoassay platforms (streptavidin-biotin based) — falsely low TSH, falsely high T4/T3 and other hormone results; ask about biotin use and hold ~48 hours before testing if a result seems inconsistent with the clinical picture
GlucocorticoidsSuppress ACTH/cortisol (Volume 3a), suppress TSH somewhat, raise glucose, can suppress LH/FSH at high doses
Dopamine antagonistsRaise prolactin (this file)

Spironolactone/eplerenone, NSAIDs, beta-blockersDistort aldosterone-renin ratio (this file)
Biotin interference deserves special mention
Because biotin supplementation is extremely common (marketed for hair/skin/nails) and often not volunteered by patients unless specifically asked, it's an underappreciated cause of lab results that don't match the clinical picture across multiple hormone assays simultaneously — a genuinely easy thing to miss unless it's specifically on the differential for an internally inconsistent panel.
Verify before relying
Reference ranges, cutoffs, and assay-specific caveats in this file reflect general clinical laboratory medicine standards at the time of writing — always confirm against your own institution's specific assay platform and reference ranges, which can vary meaningfully between labs.

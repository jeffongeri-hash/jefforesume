Guideline Changes Atlas Immunology · Vol. 19 — Anaphylaxis Criteria
What Changed · 2023–2025

Anaphylaxis finally has one diagnostic standard, not two competing ones

For years, NIAID/FAAN and WAO anaphylaxis criteria disagreed at the margins — enough to matter in real cases. A 46-member international GA2LEN panel spent 2023–2024 reconciling them into a single consensus definition and clinical support tool, published in JACI in January 2025, alongside a 2023 AAAAI/ACAAI practice parameter update with real bedside implications.

02 · Deep Dive

One diagnostic standard, finally

GA2LEN anaphylaxis clinical support tool New consensus, 2024/2025

Prior to this consensus, NIAID/FAAN and WAO anaphylaxis criteria didn't fully agree — a gap that could genuinely change whether a given presentation got called anaphylaxis. The GA2LEN panel's new clinical support tool is fundamentally aligned with the WAO 2020 criteria, explicitly recognizing isolated respiratory or isolated cardiovascular findings after likely allergen exposure as sufficient for a diagnosis — patients don't need multi-system involvement to qualify.

The tool also formalizes age-based epinephrine autoinjector dosing more explicitly than prior guidance:

Age/weightEpinephrine dose
<12 months0.1 mg
<6 years0.15 mg
≥6 years0.3 mg
Adolescents/adults0.5 mg

Note: some organizations (e.g., ASCIA) recommend transitioning children to the 0.3 mg autoinjector once they exceed 20 kg, regardless of age — check which convention your local protocols follow, as autoinjector dosing recommendations may not fully align with manufacturer labeling.

03 · Deep Dive · Myth-Busting

Not every epinephrine dose requires an ambulance

The 2023 AAAAI/ACAAI practice parameter update softens a long-standing reflexive instruction: patients and caregivers can be counseled that immediate EMS activation may not be required if the patient has a prompt, complete, and durable response to epinephrine treatment — provided additional epinephrine is available if needed. This is a conditional, not a blanket, change: it depends on response quality and backup availability, not a general "skip the ER" message.

⚡ Preemptive dosing discouraged

The same update advises against giving epinephrine preemptively to an asymptomatic patient — treat at the first sign of suspected anaphylaxis, not before symptoms appear, even in a patient with known severe allergy history.

04 · Deep Dive

Tryptase helps diagnose — epinephrine response doesn't

Baseline serum tryptase (bST) is now recommended for patients presenting with anaphylaxis — both to support the diagnosis and to screen for underlying mast cell disorders, which meaningfully change biphasic-reaction risk and long-term management.

⚠ Don't use epinephrine response as a diagnostic proxy

The guideline explicitly cautions against using either the clinical decision to give epinephrine, or the clinical response to it, as a surrogate marker to establish an anaphylaxis diagnosis. A patient who improves after epinephrine wasn't necessarily having anaphylaxis, and a patient who doesn't improve isn't necessarily not having it — diagnosis should rest on the clinical criteria, not retrospectively inferred from treatment response.

05 · Deep Dive

A needle-free option is now in the mix

Intranasal epinephrine (Neffy) is now available as an option for patients weighing 30 kg or more — the first FDA-approved needle-free epinephrine delivery route for anaphylaxis. This matters for needle-averse patients and caregivers, and for scenarios where injection technique or hesitation has historically delayed treatment.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Isolated respiratory or isolated cardiovascular findings after likely allergen exposure now meet diagnostic criteria on their own — you don't need multi-organ involvement to call it anaphylaxis.
  • A patient with a prompt, complete, durable response to epinephrine and backup epinephrine available may not need an automatic EMS/ED escalation — but this is conditional, not a default.
  • Don't use "responded to epinephrine" or "we gave epinephrine" as retrospective proof of the diagnosis — document against the actual clinical criteria.
  • Consider baseline serum tryptase in anaphylaxis presentations, particularly with recurrent or severe episodes, to screen for mast cell disorders.
  • Intranasal epinephrine (Neffy) is a real option now for patients ≥30 kg who are needle-averse.
  • Don't counsel preemptive epinephrine dosing before symptoms appear, even in high-risk patients.

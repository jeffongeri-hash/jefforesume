Cardiac Auscultation & Palpation

The physical exam counterpart to the cardiac action potential diagram in the Pharmacology Atlas — timing tells you nearly as much as the sound itself.

Murmur timing (systolic vs. diastolic) is the single fastest way to narrow the differential before you've even characterized the murmur's quality — see Vol. 1b for the full pathophysiology of each lesion.

Dynamic maneuvers change murmur intensity in diagnostic ways

Valsalva (strain phase) decreases preload — most murmurs soften, but hypertrophic cardiomyopathy's murmur gets LOUDER (less blood volume to counteract the dynamic outflow obstruction). Passive leg raise/squatting increases preload and afterload — mitral regurgitation and aortic regurgitation murmurs increase, while HCM's murmur decreases. This maneuver-based logic (see Vol. 1b for the full HCM discussion) is one of the highest-yield bedside diagnostic tools in cardiology.

Precordial Palpation

Point of maximal impulse (PMI)

Normally at the 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line, roughly the size of a quarter. Displaced laterally/inferiorly suggests LV dilation; a sustained, forceful PMI suggests LV hypertrophy/pressure overload.

Heaves and thrills

A parasternal heave (sustained lift along the left sternal border) suggests RV hypertrophy/pressure overload (e.g., pulmonary hypertension — see Vol. 1b). A palpable thrill corresponds to a Grade IV+ murmur and localizes to the same area as the murmur's maximal intensity.

When Exam Findings Warrant Escalation
Urgent evaluation
Any new murmur with hemodynamic instability, a new diastolic murmur (always pathologic, unlike some soft systolic murmurs), or a loud new murmur after MI (suspect papillary muscle rupture or VSD — see Vol. 5).
Who to call
Cardiology for any new or changed murmur warranting echocardiographic characterization beyond the bedside exam.

JVP, Volume Status & Edema

The JVP is a direct window into right atrial pressure — a skill that takes practice to trust, but pays off constantly in undifferentiated dyspnea and volume status assessment.

Technique

Position the patient reclined at 30-45 degrees, head turned slightly away from the side being examined. Identify the internal jugular vein's pulsation (not the carotid — the JVP has a biphasic, undulating quality and is not palpable, while the carotid has a single, palpable outward thrust). Measure the vertical height from the sternal angle to the top of the visible pulsation, then add 5cm (the estimated distance from the sternal angle to the right atrium) to estimate central venous pressure. Normal JVP is roughly 6-8cm of water (or often simplified to <3-4cm above the sternal angle).

Recognizing individual waveform components takes practice, but two are especially high-yield: the a wave disappears in atrial fibrillation (no organized atrial contraction), and a giant/prominent v wave suggests significant tricuspid regurgitation.
Hepatojugular reflux — an active provocation, not just observation

Apply firm, sustained pressure over the right upper quadrant (liver) for 10-15 seconds while watching the JVP. A sustained rise in JVP (rather than a transient bump that falls back down) during compression indicates the right heart cannot accommodate the increased venous return — a positive test supports a volume-overloaded or non-compliant right heart, useful when the resting JVP is ambiguous.

Peripheral Edema Grading

GradePit DepthRecovery Time
1+~2mmRapid (immediate)
2+~4mm10-15 seconds

Symmetric vs. asymmetric edema — the fastest triage question

Bilateral, symmetric edema points toward a systemic cause (heart failure, renal disease, hepatic disease, venous insufficiency if chronic) — see the Skin & Extremity Findings section. Unilateral or asymmetric edema shifts the differential sharply toward a local process: DVT (see the Risk Stratification section's Wells score), lymphedema, or localized venous obstruction. This single observation should shape your workup before you've done anything else.

When Volume Status Findings Warrant Escalation
Urgent evaluation
Markedly elevated JVP with respiratory distress (consider acute decompensated HF, tamponade — see Vol. 1b) or any asymmetric leg swelling with concern for DVT.
Who to call
Cardiology for volume status assessment in complex or ambiguous presentations; consider bedside ultrasound (IVC assessment) as an objective adjunct when the JVP exam is equivocal.

Vascular Exam

The bedside counterpart to Vascular Vol. 1 and 2 — pulse grading, bruit auscultation, and the ABI technique that turns a subjective exam into an objective number.

Pulse Grading Scale

GradeDescription
0Absent — no palpable pulse
1+Diminished, weak, difficult to palpate
2+Normal
3+Increased/bounding (consider aortic regurgitation, hyperdynamic states, or an aneurysmal segment at the palpation site)

Standard Pulse Locations

Carotid, brachial, radial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis — document bilaterally and compare symmetry. Asymmetric pulses (especially between right and left, or upper vs. lower extremity) point toward focal arterial disease, dissection (see Vascular Vol. 1), or coarctation in a younger patient.

Bruit Auscultation

Carotid bruit

Auscultate each carotid separately with the bell, patient holding breath briefly to eliminate breath sounds — a bruit suggests turbulent flow from stenosis, though the correlation between bruit presence/loudness and stenosis severity is imperfect (a very tight stenosis can be silent if flow becomes too slow to be turbulent).

Abdominal/renal/femoral bruits

An abdominal bruit (especially with lateralization) raises suspicion for renal artery stenosis (see Vascular Vol. 1) — auscultate just lateral to the midline, above and below the umbilicus. A femoral bruit suggests iliofemoral PAD.

Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI) — Technique

With the patient supine and rested for several minutes, measure systolic BP at the brachial artery (both arms, use the higher value) and at the dorsalis pedis/posterior tibial artery of each ankle using a Doppler probe. ABI = highest ankle systolic pressure for that leg ÷ higher of the two brachial systolic pressures. See Vascular Vol. 1 for the full interpretation table (normal, borderline, mild-moderate, severe PAD, and the noncompressible-vessel caveat in diabetes/ESRD).

Capillary Refill

Press the nail bed or fingertip/toe pad until it blanches, release, and count seconds until color returns — normal is under 2 seconds. Delayed refill suggests poor peripheral perfusion (shock, severe PAD, acute limb ischemia — see Vascular Vol. 1's 6 Ps) but is affected by ambient temperature and patient age, so interpret alongside other perfusion signs rather than in isolation.

Acute limb ischemia — a time-critical exam

Absent pulses combined with pain, pallor, poikilothermia, and especially new paresthesia or paralysis is a surgical emergency (see Vascular Vol. 1's Acute Limb Ischemia section) — this exam should be fast, systematic, and immediately escalated if positive, not deferred pending further workup.

When Vascular Exam Findings Warrant Escalation
Emergent evaluation
Any exam consistent with acute limb ischemia (see above) — this does not wait for imaging.
Urgent evaluation
New asymmetric pulses, a new bruit with associated symptoms, or an abnormal ABI in a patient with claudication symptoms.
Who to call
Vascular surgery for any exam finding suggesting acute or critical limb ischemia; vascular medicine for further workup of an abnormal but non-emergent vascular exam.

Skin & Extremity Findings by Disease

The single fastest way to narrow a differential before any test results come back — arterial disease, venous disease, heart failure, and endocarditis each leave a distinctive signature on the skin.

Arterial (PAD) and venous (CVI/PVD) disease produce nearly opposite skin findings — cool/pale/hairless vs. warm/pigmented/edematous — which is exactly why getting this distinction right changes management (compression therapy helps venous disease and can harm arterial disease — see Vascular Vol. 2).

Quick Differentiation: Arterial vs. Venous Ulcer

Mnemonic — "Painless Janeway, painful Osler"

Janeway = painleSS (both have double letters — a small memory hook), flat, on the palms/soles. Osler nodes = painful, raised, on the pulps. Getting this pairing backwards is a common exam error worth deliberately avoiding.

Vasculitis Skin Findings

Palpable purpura (raised, non-blanching, classically on dependent areas like the lower legs) is the hallmark of small-vessel vasculitis — IgA vasculitis, ANCA-associated vasculitis, cryoglobulinemic vasculitis (see Vascular Vol. 1). Livedo reticularis (a net-like, mottled purple-red discoloration) suggests medium/large-vessel involvement or a vasospastic/microthrombotic process — seen in polyarteritis nodosa, antiphospholipid syndrome, and cholesterol embolization syndrome among others.

Cholesterol embolization — an easy-to-miss cause of livedo reticularis

New livedo reticularis, especially involving the toes ("blue toe syndrome") with palpable pulses, after a recent cardiac catheterization or vascular procedure should raise suspicion for cholesterol embolization syndrome — atheromatous debris showering distally from a disrupted plaque. This is easy to misattribute to a primary vasculitis if the procedural history isn't specifically elicited.

When Skin Findings Warrant Escalation
Urgent evaluation
New endocarditis stigmata in a febrile or bacteremic patient — pursue the 2023 Duke-ISCVID workup (see Vol. 1b); new palpable purpura or livedo reticularis with systemic symptoms.
Who to call
Cardiology/infectious disease for suspected endocarditis; rheumatology for suspected vasculitis; vascular surgery/medicine for arterial vs. venous ulcer management once differentiated.

Documentation & Medicolegal Pearls

Closing this atlas — and the entire series — with how to write down everything the prior five sections taught you to find, in a way that protects both the patient and you.

Structured Cardiac Exam Documentation

A complete, reproducible cardiac exam note follows a consistent structure — inspection, palpation, auscultation, and a synthesis statement — rather than a vague "heart: RRR, no m/r/g" that provides little defensible detail if the chart is ever reviewed.

A more defensible template

"Inspection: no visible heaves or lifts. Palpation: PMI non-displaced, 5th ICS MCL, no thrill. Auscultation: S1/S2 normal, regular rate and rhythm, no S3/S4, no murmurs/rubs/gallops appreciated at aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid, and mitral positions. JVP estimated at [X]cm, no HJR. Extremities: no edema, pulses 2+ and symmetric bilaterally (radial, femoral, DP, PT)." This takes seconds longer to write than "RRR, no m/r/g" but documents that a systematic exam was actually performed at each landmark — valuable both for patient care continuity and medicolegal defensibility.

Murmur Documentation — Use the Full Notation

A complete murmur description includes: timing (systolic/diastolic), grade (I-VI, see the Cardiac Auscultation section), location/point of maximal intensity, radiation, quality (harsh, blowing, rumbling), and pitch — e.g., "Grade III/VI harsh systolic murmur, loudest at the right 2nd intercostal space, radiating to the carotids." A murmur documented only as "systolic murmur" gives the next clinician (or a chart reviewer) almost nothing to work with.

Risk Score Documentation — Show Your Work

Document the score AND the individual components

Writing "HEART score 3, low risk, discharged" is weaker than writing "HEART score 3 (History: 1 - moderately suspicious; ECG: 0 - normal; Age: 0; Risk factors: 1 - one risk factor, hypertension; Troponin: 1 - mildly elevated but non-dynamic), low risk category, discussed with patient, shared decision-making regarding discharge with close follow-up." The itemized breakdown demonstrates the clinical reasoning behind the number, which matters enormously if that chart is ever reviewed months or years later — a bare number invites the question "how did you get there?" with no answer in the record.

Documenting a Negative/Reassuring Exam Has Real Value

Explicitly charting pertinent negatives — "no JVD, no lower extremity edema, no hepatojugular reflux, ABI within normal limits bilaterally, no carotid bruits" — is not filler. It demonstrates that specific, relevant findings were actively sought and not simply omitted, which is a meaningfully different (and more defensible) record than a chart that's simply silent on the topic.

The "I Considered and Ruled Out" Note

Naming the dangerous diagnoses you excluded, and why

For high-risk chief complaints (chest pain, syncope, dyspnea), explicitly documenting the specific dangerous diagnoses considered and the reasoning for excluding each — "Aortic dissection considered given the atypical pain description; however, pain was reproducible on palpation, no pulse deficit, no widened mediastinum on CXR, low clinical suspicion" — creates a far stronger record than a diagnosis and disposition alone. This directly mirrors the documentation philosophy from your ED Charting/Bulletproof Note atlas, applied specifically to cardiovascular chief complaints.

Cross-Referencing This Atlas Series in Your Documentation

When your clinical reasoning draws on a specific guideline-based framework — the 2025 ACC/AHA hypertension thresholds, the SCAI shock staging system, the Duke-ISCVID endocarditis criteria — naming the framework explicitly in your note ("SCAI Stage C cardiogenic shock, escalating to mechanical circulatory support per shock team discussion") demonstrates guideline-concordant reasoning far more clearly than a narrative summary alone, and creates an easier record to defend or explain later.

Discharge documentation for "ruled out" risk-score-negative patients

A patient discharged after a reassuring HEART score, negative PERC, or low Wells score with a negative D-dimer still needs explicit return-precaution documentation — what specific symptoms should prompt return, and confirmation the patient verbalized understanding. A risk score result is a probability statement, not a guarantee, and the chart should reflect that the patient was given clear guidance for the residual (even if small) risk.

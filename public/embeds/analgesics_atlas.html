Analgesics Atlas

Master-level ED pharmacology reference: opioid receptor pharmacology & classification, route kinetics (IV/IM/PO), NSAIDs & acetaminophen, neuropathic agents, migraine dopamine-antagonist cocktails, and the GI cocktail — with an MME converter, equianalgesic reference, and cocktail builder.

Opioids act at G-protein-coupled opioid receptors (mu, kappa, delta) whose activation inhibits adenylyl cyclase, opens K+ channels (hyperpolarizing the neuron), and closes voltage-gated Ca2+ channels — the net effect is reduced neuronal excitability and blunted ascending nociceptive transmission plus enhanced descending inhibition. Analgesia is overwhelmingly a mu-opioid receptor (MOR) effect; so are the respiratory depression, euphoria, constipation, and physical dependence that make the class dangerous.

Receptor Pharmacology — the Three Classical Receptors

Mu (MOR, OPRM1): the principal analgesic receptor. Supraspinal + spinal analgesia, respiratory depression, miosis, euphoria, reduced GI motility, physical dependence. Nearly every clinically important opioid effect (good and bad) is MOR-mediated. Endogenous ligands: beta-endorphin, endomorphins.

Kappa (KOR): spinal analgesia, dysphoria (rather than euphoria), miosis, and diuresis, with LESS respiratory depression than mu. This is why kappa-leaning agonist-antagonists (nalbuphine, butorphanol) have a ceiling on respiratory depression but can precipitate dysphoria. Endogenous ligand: dynorphin.

Delta (DOR): modulates analgesia and mood; less central to acute clinical opioid use but an active drug-development target for analgesics with lower abuse potential. Endogenous ligands: enkephalins.

Signal transduction pearl: MOR analgesia is largely G-protein (Gi/o) mediated, whereas much of the respiratory depression and tolerance has been attributed to beta-arrestin-2 recruitment — the biased-agonism hypothesis behind oliceridine, though the real-world separation of benefit from harm has been more modest than initially hoped.

Structural Classification — Natural vs. Semi-Synthetic vs. Synthetic

NATURAL (opiates, directly from opium poppy Papaver somniferum): morphine and codeine are the two clinically used naturally occurring alkaloids. Thebaine and papaverine are natural precursors but not used as analgesics directly.

SEMI-SYNTHETIC (chemically modified from a natural alkaloid): hydromorphone, hydrocodone, oxycodone, oxymorphone (all derived from morphine or thebaine), plus heroin (diacetylmorphine) and buprenorphine (from thebaine).

SYNTHETIC (fully lab-synthesized, no botanical backbone): fentanyl and its analogs (sufentanil, alfentanil, remifentanil, carfentanil), methadone, meperidine, tramadol, and tapentadol. Synthetics tend to have distinct receptor kinetics and often additional non-opioid mechanisms (e.g., tramadol/tapentadol's monoamine reuptake inhibition, methadone's NMDA antagonism).

Why the classification matters clinically: it predicts cross-reactivity in true allergy (a genuine morphine anaphylaxis does not preclude using a structurally unrelated synthetic like fentanyl), and the synthetic agents' secondary mechanisms drive both their unique benefits and their unique toxicities (serotonin syndrome, QT prolongation, seizures).

The same opioid behaves very differently depending on route — driven by bioavailability, first-pass metabolism, and the rate a drug reaches the effect site. Matching route to clinical urgency and reliability is a core skill.

Why Route Changes Everything

Onset is governed by how fast drug reaches the CNS effect site. IV bypasses absorption entirely (fastest and most titratable). IM and SC depend on tissue perfusion — unreliable in shock, and painful. Oral is slowest and subject to first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Bioavailability (F) is the fraction reaching systemic circulation. Oral morphine's F is only ~30% because of extensive first-pass glucuronidation — this is exactly why oral morphine doses are roughly 3x the IV dose (10 mg IV ≈ 30 mg PO).

Titratability favors IV: small increments, rapid feedback. A committed IM or oral dose cannot be taken back once it is absorbing — an underappreciated safety difference that parallels the IV-vs-subcutaneous insulin logic.

Clinical Pearls
  • The classic parenteral equianalgesic anchor: morphine 10 mg IV ≈ hydromorphone 1.5 mg IV ≈ fentanyl 100 mcg IV. Memorize this triad — it underlies most acute-pain rotations.
  • Oral:IV ratio for morphine is ~3:1; for hydromorphone ~5:1; for oxycodone (oral) there is no US parenteral comparator. These ratios exist because of route-specific first-pass metabolism, not different receptor activity.
  • Fentanyl's brief single-dose duration is redistribution-based, not clearance-based — so repeated dosing or infusion progressively lengthens its effective duration (rising context-sensitive half-time).

For a large fraction of acute pain seen in the ED, non-opioid analgesics are as effective as opioids — and safer. Understanding the COX pathway, the analgesic CEILING effect, and organ-specific toxicity is what lets you use them to their full potential and opioid-spare deliberately.

NSAID Mechanism — the COX Pathway

NSAIDs inhibit cyclooxygenase (COX), blocking conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins. Prostaglandins sensitize peripheral nociceptors and mediate inflammation, fever, and pain — so blocking them provides analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic effects.

COX-1 is constitutive: it protects gastric mucosa, supports platelet thromboxane (aggregation), and maintains renal perfusion. COX-2 is largely inducible at sites of inflammation. Non-selective NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, ketorolac) hit both; the GI and antiplatelet effects come mostly from COX-1 inhibition.

COX-2-selective agents (celecoxib) spare gastric COX-1 → less GI bleeding, but shift the prostacyclin/thromboxane balance toward thrombosis → increased cardiovascular risk. This is the core NSAID trade-off: GI safety vs. CV risk.

The Analgesic Ceiling — a High-Yield Concept

Unlike opioids (no true analgesic ceiling — more drug = more analgesia until toxicity), NSAIDs have an ANALGESIC CEILING. Above a certain dose, additional drug adds toxicity but NOT additional analgesia.

Ketorolac is the classic teaching example: 10 mg IV provides essentially the same analgesia as 30 mg IV for most pain, while the higher dose adds GI/renal risk. This 'analgesic ceiling of ketorolac' has driven a shift toward lower-dose protocols (10–15 mg).

Practical consequence: if a ceiling-dose NSAID hasn't worked, escalating the NSAID dose is futile — switch mechanism (add acetaminophen, a different modality, or an opioid) rather than piling on more NSAID.

Opioid vs. Non-Opioid — What the Comparison Trials Show

A landmark 2017 JAMA RCT (Chang et al.) in ED patients with acute extremity pain found that ibuprofen 400 mg + acetaminophen 1000 mg provided pain relief statistically indistinguishable from three different opioid/acetaminophen combinations at 2 hours — a foundational opioid-sparing result.

For renal colic, IV/IM NSAIDs (e.g., ketorolac) frequently outperform opioids with less vomiting. For acute low back pain, adding an opioid or muscle relaxant to an NSAID has repeatedly failed to improve functional outcomes.

The recurring peer-reviewed theme: for many common acute pains, multimodal non-opioid regimens match opioids on analgesia while avoiding respiratory depression, dependence, and nausea — the evidentiary backbone of opioid-sparing ED practice.

Neuropathic pain (diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, radiculopathy, central pain) responds poorly to opioids and NSAIDs. First-line therapy is a different pharmacology entirely: gabapentinoids, TCAs, and SNRIs — agents that modulate abnormal neuronal excitability and descending inhibition rather than blocking nociceptor signaling.

Gabapentinoid Mechanism — the α2δ Target

Despite being designed as GABA analogs, gabapentin and pregabalin have essentially NO action at GABA receptors and do not change GABA levels. Their target is the α2δ-1 auxiliary subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels on presynaptic neurons in the dorsal root ganglion and spinal dorsal horn.

By binding α2δ-1, they reduce trafficking/function of the calcium channel, decreasing presynaptic Ca2+ influx and thereby dampening release of excitatory neurotransmitters (glutamate, substance P, CGRP) from sensitized neurons.

The effect is state-dependent: they preferentially quiet pathologically sensitized (upregulated α2δ-1) pathways while having little effect on normal acute nociception — which is why they treat neuropathic pain but do essentially nothing for acute somatic pain.

Pharmacokinetic distinction: gabapentin has saturable, dose-dependent (non-linear) absorption — bioavailability falls as dose rises, requiring TID dosing and slow titration. Pregabalin has linear, near-complete absorption → more predictable, twice-daily dosing.

First-Line Framework & Numbers Needed to Treat

The major neuropathic-pain guidelines (e.g., NeuPSIG) place three classes as first-line: gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin), TCAs (amitriptyline, nortriptyline), and SNRIs (duloxetine, venlafaxine).

NNT (number needed to treat for 50% pain relief) is sobering across the class — roughly in the 4–8 range for most agents, with TCAs often among the lowest NNTs but limited by anticholinergic side effects. No single agent is dramatically superior; choice is driven by comorbidities and side-effect fit.

Combination therapy (e.g., a gabapentinoid + an SNRI or TCA) often outperforms monotherapy — a recognized and evidence-supported strategy when a single agent partially works.

ED migraine management is built on DOPAMINE ANTAGONISTS (the antiemetic/antipsychotic class), not opioids — which are explicitly second-line at best and worsen recurrence and medication-overuse headache. The 'migraine cocktail' delivers these agents plus adjuncts up front, together, for maximal benefit.

Why Dopamine Antagonists Work in Migraine

Prochlorperazine, metoclopramide, droperidol, chlorpromazine, and haloperidol are dopamine (D2) antagonists. Their antimigraine effect is only partly explained by treating nausea — they have an independent, incompletely understood central antimigraine action (dopaminergic pathways are implicated in migraine pathophysiology).

Metoclopramide adds a 5-HT3 antagonist effect and improves gastric motility (helpful because migraine causes gastroparesis that impairs oral drug absorption).

The main shared adverse effects are extrapyramidal: AKATHISIA (motor restlessness, very common and distressing) and acute dystonic reactions. Droperidol and haloperidol additionally prolong QT (droperidol carries a boxed warning, though real-world ED doses appear low-risk).

The Evidence — Head-to-Head & Cocktail Studies

Prochlorperazine 10 mg IV outperforms metoclopramide 10 mg IV in head-to-head migraine trials and beats ketorolac, magnesium, valproate, and even subcutaneous sumatriptan in various studies — making it arguably the single best first-line ED agent.

Metoclopramide 10 mg IV is guideline-supported and the go-to backup during prochlorperazine shortages; a dose-finding trial showed 20 mg and 40 mg give NO extra benefit over 10 mg but more akathisia.

Diphenhydramine does NOT itself relieve migraine and does NOT reliably prevent akathisia at standard (10 mg) metoclopramide doses — routine addition is discouraged by newer evidence. It DOES meaningfully reduce prochlorperazine-induced akathisia (one trial: ~61% relative risk reduction), so its value is agent- and dose-specific.

Front-loading matters: administering the NSAID + dopamine antagonist + dexamethasone + fluids together up front outperforms a stepwise 'try one, then add another' approach. A single dose of dexamethasone reduces 24–72h migraine RECURRENCE (it does not improve acute relief).

Opioids: explicitly second-line or worse — associated with worse outcomes, higher recurrence, return visits, and medication-overuse headache. Reserve for true refractory/contraindication scenarios.

The 'GI cocktail' is an ED symptomatic treatment for dyspepsia — classically antacid ± viscous lidocaine ± an anticholinergic (Donnatal). It's worth knowing precisely because the evidence is more negative than its popularity suggests, and because it is NOT a valid diagnostic test to rule out cardiac chest pain.

Components & Rationale

Antacid (e.g., Maalox/Mylanta, 30 mL): neutralizes gastric acid — the component with the most consistent symptomatic benefit.

Viscous lidocaine 2% (~10–15 mL): topical anesthetic intended to numb esophageal/gastric mucosa.

Anticholinergic/antispasmodic (Donnatal = phenobarbital + hyoscyamine + atropine + scopolamine): intended to reduce spasm/motility — but slowed motility can itself worsen dyspepsia, and the additive risk/interaction profile is unfavorable.

What the Evidence Actually Shows

The original 1990 study (antacid vs. antacid + viscous lidocaine) suggested added benefit from lidocaine. However, subsequent better-controlled RCTs did not replicate this.

The frequently-cited 2003 Berman double-blind RCT compared antacid alone vs. antacid + Donnatal vs. antacid + Donnatal + lidocaine and found NO difference between groups — all improved similarly. Conclusion: the 'cocktail' additions may be unnecessary.

Later work found antacid monotherapy was equally effective and MORE palatable than lidocaine-containing mixtures, with viscous lidocaine adding oral numbness and a theoretical aspiration risk (it blunts airway protective reflexes). The pragmatic bottom line for many EDs: antacid alone is a reasonable first step.

CRITICAL caution: response to a GI cocktail does NOT rule out cardiac ischemia. 'The pain got better with the cocktail, so it's not the heart' is a documented, dangerous diagnostic fallacy — relief is nonspecific and cardiac and GI pain frequently overlap.

Clinical Pearls
  • Use the GI cocktail (or just antacid) as symptomatic relief for a clinical dyspepsia picture — never as a rule-out for ACS.
  • If you use viscous lidocaine, weigh the aspiration/airway-reflex-blunting risk, especially in elderly or dysphagic patients; antacid alone avoids this.
  • Donnatal adds anticholinergic burden and drug interactions without clear benefit — most current teaching favors dropping it.

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Endocrinopathies

The highest-yield blind spot in modern practice — a cancer patient on immunotherapy who "just has cancer" on the problem list can be in an unrecognized adrenal crisis.

MechanismWhy Checkpoint Inhibitors Cause This

CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors work by releasing the brakes on T-cell activation against tumor antigens — the same mechanism that fights cancer also unleashes autoimmune attack on healthy endocrine tissue via increased T-cell activity against self-antigens, unmasking of pre-existing autoantibodies, and elevated inflammatory cytokines. CTLA-4 is preferentially expressed on pituitary thyrotroph and gonadotroph cells, which is the proposed mechanism behind ipilimumab's outsized hypophysitis risk compared to PD-1/PD-L1 agents.

HypophysitisCTLA-4 vs. PD-1/PD-L1 — Genuinely Different Diseases

CTLA-4 inhibitors (ipilimumab)PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors
Incidence10–15% — the most common endocrinopathy with this drug class; ~8% with nivolumab-ipilimumab combination vs. ~4% ipilimumab aloneSubstantially lower
OnsetEarly — typically within 2–12 weeks (mean ~3 cycles)Later and more variable — median ~28 weeks (range 10–46 weeks)
PatternGland enlargement on MRI, combined multi-axis hypopituitarism more commonIsolated ACTH deficiency (secondary adrenal insufficiency) without distinct MRI abnormalities more common
ReversibilityACTH deficiency is frequently permanent even after gland enlargement resolvesSimilarly often permanent once established
This mimics far more common, more benign diagnoses
ICI-hypophysitis-driven adrenal insufficiency presents with hypotension, dehydration, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia — a picture that can be mistaken for sepsis, brain metastasis progression, or simple cancer-related cachexia in a patient already expected to look unwell. A low threshold for checking morning cortisol/ACTH in any ICI patient with new fatigue, hypotension, or unexplained electrolyte changes is the single most important practical takeaway in this section.

Thyroid Dysfunction

Occurs in roughly 6%+ of ICI-treated patients — most commonly a destructive thyroiditis pattern (transient thyrotoxicosis followed by hypothyroidism, analogous to postpartum/subacute thyroiditis in Volume 1), though ICI-induced Graves' disease and isolated hypothyroidism also occur. Unlike hypophysitis, thyroid irAEs occur with both CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1 agents at broadly comparable rates.

Primary Adrenal Insufficiency (Adrenalitis)

Less common than hypophysitis-driven secondary adrenal insufficiency but important to distinguish: primary ICI-adrenalitis causes hyperpigmentation and mineralocorticoid deficiency (hyponatremia and hyperkalemia — see Volume 1) since the entire adrenal cortex is affected, whereas hypophysitis-driven secondary insufficiency spares the RAAS-dependent zona glomerulosa (Volume 1/3c) and typically does not cause hyperkalemia.

Fulminant Type 1 Diabetes

A distinct and dangerous ICI complication — rapid beta-cell destruction presenting as new-onset diabetes, frequently in diabetic ketoacidosis (see Volume 4) at first presentation, sometimes with minimal preceding polyuria/polydipsia to serve as warning. C-peptide is typically low/undetectable at diagnosis (rapid, near-complete beta-cell loss rather than the more gradual autoimmune process of classic T1DM) and insulin therapy is permanent.

Management Principles

Steroids are routinely used for hypophysitis and primary adrenal insufficiency Steroids are NOT routinely used for most other ICI endocrinopathies (thyroiditis, diabetes) — unlike other organ-system irAEs ICI therapy can typically continue through mild-moderate (CTCAE grade 1–2) endocrine irAEs once hormone replacement is started Most ICI endocrinopathies are permanent — this is a long-term hormone replacement issue, not a self-resolving toxicity
Multi-gland involvement is real and often sequential
Patients can develop multiple ICI endocrinopathies over time (e.g., thyroiditis followed months later by adrenal insufficiency, followed by diabetes) — a single negative endocrine workup at ICI initiation doesn't rule out a later irAE, and ongoing vigilance across the treatment course is warranted rather than a one-time baseline check.

Macroprolactinemia

A biologically inert assay artifact that causes a meaningful fraction of "unexplained" hyperprolactinemia — and a genuinely easy way to avoid an unnecessary pituitary MRI and dopamine agonist prescription.

What It Is

Circulating prolactin exists in three forms: monomeric (23 kDa — the biologically active form responsible for classic hyperprolactinemia symptoms), big prolactin (45–60 kDa), and big-big prolactin/macroprolactin (150–170 kDa) — typically a prolactin-IgG antibody complex. Macroprolactin is biologically inactive (too large to cross capillary membranes and reach receptors effectively) but standard immunoassays cannot distinguish it from monomeric prolactin, so it's counted in the total reported result — producing an elevated prolactin level in a patient with no real hyperprolactinemic physiology.

How Common — and Why It Matters

Estimated prevalence among patients being tested for hyperprolactinemia ranges roughly 10–20% depending on population and cutoff used — common enough that it should be routinely considered, not treated as a curiosity. Failure to identify it leads directly to unnecessary pituitary MRI, unnecessary dopamine agonist therapy (with its own side effects), and — in a subset of published cases — women mistakenly treated for years for "idiopathic hyperprolactinemia" that was never biologically active in the first place.

TestingPEG Precipitation

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is added to the serum sample, precipitating out the large macroprolactin complexes; the supernatant is then re-assayed for "post-PEG" monomeric prolactin.

Post-PEG recoveryInterpretation
< 40%Macroprolactin is the dominant circulating form — macroprolactinemia, not true hyperprolactinemia
40–60%Indeterminate zone — method-dependent, interpret alongside clinical picture
> 60%True (monomeric) hyperprolactinemia — proceed with standard workup (Volume 2/3c)
Macroprolactinemia doesn't exclude a real problem
Macroprolactin can coexist with a genuine prolactinoma or true hyperprolactinemia from another cause — a low post-PEG recovery lowers suspicion but doesn't substitute for clinical correlation if the presentation (galactorrhea, hypogonadal symptoms) is otherwise compelling. Gel filtration chromatography remains the gold standard if a PEG result is ambiguous, though it's rarely needed in routine practice.

Management

Confirmed macroprolactinemia with a normal post-PEG monomeric prolactin generally requires no treatment, no dopamine agonist, and no routine pituitary MRI — long-term follow-up studies show no symptomatic progression in patients with macroprolactinemia and a normal monomeric fraction. This is one of the cleanest "stop the workup here" moments in endocrine diagnostics once confirmed.

Perioperative & Periprocedural Endocrine Management

What to do with steroids, diabetes medications, and thyroid hormone around surgery and procedures — the practical intersection of everything covered so far.

Steroid Stress DosingMatched to Surgical Risk

Building directly on Volume 3a's adrenal suppression thresholds: patients on chronic glucocorticoids (or recently tapered off within the suppression-risk window) need supplemental coverage proportional to surgical stress, not a flat dose for every procedure.

Procedure categoryTypical stress doseExamples
MinorUsual daily dose only — no supplementation neededMinor dental work, skin biopsy, minor procedures under local anesthesia
ModerateUsual dose + hydrocortisone ~50mg IV at induction, tapering over 24hCholecystectomy, joint replacement, hemicolectomy
Major/critical illnessHydrocortisone 100mg IV then 50mg IV q8h (or continuous infusion), tapering over 48–72h as stress resolvesCardiac surgery, major GI resection, prolonged ICU stay, sepsis

This directly mirrors the physiologic surge the intact HPA axis would normally mount (Volume 1) — the goal is replacing what the patient's own suppressed axis cannot provide during the stress window, then tapering back to baseline as that stress resolves.

Diabetes MedicationsWhat to Hold and When

Medication classPerioperative guidance
SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin)Hold 3 days before scheduled surgery; 4 days for ertugliflozin — the outlier given euglycemic DKA risk (Volume 4) even with a normal-appearing glucose, which makes it easy to miss if not specifically on the differential postoperatively
MetforminGenerally held day of surgery/procedure; specifically held before iodinated contrast administration given lactic acidosis risk if acute kidney injury develops post-contrast
GLP-1 receptor agonistsWeekly formulations: consider holding one week prior for procedures under deep sedation/general anesthesia, given delayed gastric emptying (Volume 2) and aspiration risk; daily oral formulation held day of surgery
SulfonylureasHold day of surgery given hypoglycemia risk with fasting (Volume 1 K-ATP mechanism)
InsulinBasal insulin typically continued at reduced dose (~50–80%) even while fasting; prandial/correction insulin adjusted to NPO status and glucose monitoring
Euglycemic DKA is the single highest-yield perioperative diabetes pitfall
A postoperative patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor with nausea, tachypnea, or unexplained malaise and a normal-looking glucose can still be in DKA — glucose alone will not flag it (Volume 4). Check a basic metabolic panel (anion gap) and beta-hydroxybutyrate/ketones directly if there's any clinical suspicion, regardless of glucose.

Thyroid Medications

Levothyroxine's long half-life (Volume 1) means a missed dose or two perioperatively rarely causes clinically significant swings — it can typically be resumed once oral intake returns without special bridging. Patients with poorly controlled hyperthyroidism should have elective surgery deferred given thyroid storm risk (Volume 4); antithyroid drugs are generally continued through the perioperative period where surgery cannot be deferred.

Pheochromocytoma

Any patient with a known or newly suspected pheochromocytoma requires the full alpha-then-beta blockade sequence (Volume 4) completed before any surgery — including unrelated surgery — given the risk of an intraoperative catecholamine crisis from anesthesia induction or tumor-adjacent manipulation.

Heterophile Antibody Interference (HAMA)

The general assay-interference category that biotin (Volume 3c) and the prolactin "hook effect" (Volume 2) both live inside — worth knowing on its own terms.

MechanismWhy Sandwich Immunoassays Are Vulnerable

Most hormone immunoassays are "sandwich" assays — a capture antibody and a detection antibody, both typically raised in animals (often mice), bind the target hormone from opposite sides. Heterophile antibodies — naturally occurring, weakly poly-specific human antibodies that react against animal immunoglobulins — can bridge the capture and detection antibodies directly, without any real hormone present, producing a falsely elevated result. A related, historically named subset — human anti-mouse antibodies (HAMA) — arise specifically after exposure to murine monoclonal antibodies (diagnostic imaging agents, some cancer immunotherapies), though the terms are often used interchangeably in practice. Rheumatoid factor (reacting against the Fc region of human/animal immunoglobulins) causes a closely related interference pattern.

Recognizing ItThe Telltale Pattern

Result markedly inconsistent with the clinical picture Multiple, seemingly unrelated hormones all elevated simultaneously Result changes substantially when re-run on a different assay platform Nonlinearity on serial dilution (a true elevated hormone level dilutes proportionally; an antibody-bridging artifact often does not)
A real, published example worth internalizing
A documented case (Gulbahar et al., JCEM 2015) presented with markedly elevated TSH, ACTH, FSH, PTH, IGF-1, prolactin, hCG, and calcitonin simultaneously — with no clinical picture to match any of it. Every value normalized after heterophile-blocking tube treatment and PEG precipitation. This is the pattern to remember: when a panel of otherwise-unrelated hormones is all abnormal in a patient who looks clinically fine, assay interference — not simultaneous multi-gland disease — is the more likely explanation.

Confirming It

MethodHow it works
Heterophile-blocking tubesCommercial reagent (e.g., Scantibodies HBR) binds and neutralizes the interfering antibody before re-assay — result normalizes if interference was the cause
Serial dilutionA true elevated hormone dilutes linearly; nonlinear dilution suggests an antibody-mediated artifact
PEG precipitationSame principle as the macroprolactinemia workup earlier in this file — precipitates out large antibody complexes
Re-assay on a different platformDifferent manufacturers use different antibody sources — a result that's discordant across platforms points to interference rather than a real analyte level

Practical Takeaway

Whenever a hormone level is markedly inconsistent with the clinical picture — especially alongside other unrelated hormone abnormalities on the same panel — call the lab before pursuing further imaging or treatment. This single phone call, requesting dilution linearity testing or a heterophile-blocking repeat, is often the fastest way to avoid an unnecessary and potentially invasive workup chasing an artifact.

Amiodarone-Induced Thyrotoxicosis (AIT)

Occurs in ~3% of amiodarone-treated patients in North America — two mechanistically opposite subtypes that need opposite treatments, frequently in a cardiology patient who can't easily stop the causative drug.

Why Amiodarone Does This

Amiodarone is ~37% iodine by weight — a single 200mg tablet delivers roughly 100x the daily recommended iodine intake — and is highly lipophilic with a months-long half-life, so thyroid effects can emerge (or persist) long after the drug is stopped. It also directly inhibits deiodinase activity (Volume 1), and its benzofuran structure bears structural similarity to thyroid hormone itself. Two distinct mechanisms produce thyrotoxicosis from this one drug.

Type 1 vs. Type 2Opposite Mechanisms, Opposite Treatments

Type 1 AITType 2 AIT
MechanismJod-Basedow phenomenon — iodine-induced excess hormone synthesis in a thyroid with pre-existing subclinical autonomy (nodular goiter, latent Graves')Destructive thyroiditis — direct amiodarone toxicity to follicular cells releases preformed hormone from an otherwise normal gland
Underlying thyroidAbnormal (nodular goiter or latent Graves') — often previously undiagnosedNormal
Radioactive iodine uptakeNormal or increased (though can be paradoxically low from the competing iodine load itself)Low/suppressed
Color Doppler ultrasoundIncreased vascularityAbsent/reduced vascularity — reflects the destructive rather than hyperfunctioning process
TreatmentThionamides (methimazole/PTU) — blocks the excess synthesis directlyGlucocorticoids — suppresses the destructive/inflammatory thyroiditis process
Thyroid function tests don't distinguish the two types
Standard labs (TSH, free T4/T3) look similar in both — the differentiation genuinely requires the additional imaging (radioactive iodine uptake, color Doppler ultrasound) above, plus a careful history for pre-existing thyroid nodularity. Mixed forms are common in practice, and when the mechanism is genuinely unclear, a combined trial of thionamide and glucocorticoid is a reasonable pragmatic approach rather than waiting for imaging to force a clean categorization.

Practical Management Complications

Stopping amiodarone doesn't immediately help — the iodine load persists for months given the drug's long half-life Cardiology may need amiodarone continued for the underlying arrhythmia it's treating — a genuine risk/benefit tension, not a simple "just stop the drug" fix Severe or refractory cases occasionally require thyroidectomy for definitive source control Can precipitate thyroid storm (Volume 4) — amiodarone is explicitly listed among storm precipitants

Amiodarone-Induced HypothyroidismThe More Common, Less Dramatic Counterpart

Far more common than AIT overall — amiodarone's iodine load can also trigger a failure to escape the Wolff-Chaikoff effect (Volume 1) in a susceptible thyroid (especially with underlying Hashimoto's), causing straightforward hypothyroidism rather than thyrotoxicosis. Managed with standard levothyroxine replacement without necessarily stopping amiodarone.

01 / Cardiac Surgery & Devices

CABG

The 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI guideline consolidated a decade of separate PCI and CABG guidance into one anatomy-driven, patient-centered framework — built around the SYNTAX score and a mandatory Heart Team conversation.

Anatomic Complexity Drives the Decision

Top: how anatomic complexity (SYNTAX score, left main involvement, proximal LAD status) shifts the CABG-vs-PCI balance. Bottom: conduit choice and why it matters for decades, not just the index hospitalization.
A genuinely surprising downgrade

Isolated proximal LAD stenosis with preserved LV function was downgraded from a Class 1 recommendation for CABG (2011 guideline) to Class 2b in 2021 — reflecting accumulated evidence that CABG didn't demonstrate a clear survival advantage over PCI or medical therapy alone in this specific, more limited anatomic scenario. This is a good example of guidelines moving in the opposite direction from what older training might suggest.

Why the LIMA Is Worth Protecting

The dramatic difference in 10-year patency between the LIMA (>90%) and saphenous vein grafts (~50-60%) isn't a minor technical detail — it's the single biggest predictor of long-term graft-related outcomes after CABG. This is why the LIMA is preferentially placed to the most important target (almost always the LAD), and why radial artery grafts are increasingly favored over additional vein grafts for second and third targets when feasible.

On-Pump vs. Off-Pump

On-pump (with cardiopulmonary bypass)

The traditional approach — the heart is arrested and the patient supported by CPB while grafts are constructed on a still field. Allows precise anastomoses but carries the systemic inflammatory and embolic risks of bypass itself.

Off-pump ("beating heart")

Grafts constructed on the beating heart using stabilization devices, avoiding CPB entirely. Appeals particularly in patients with heavily calcified/atherosclerotic aortas (avoiding aortic cross-clamping reduces embolic stroke risk) — though outcomes data comparing the two approaches remain a genuinely debated area, with surgeon/center experience mattering considerably.

The Heart Team isn't just a formality

The 2021 guideline's emphasis on multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion (interventional cardiology + cardiac surgery, ideally with the patient's input on values and preferences) reflects genuine equipoise in many intermediate-complexity cases — this is one of the clearer examples in cardiology of a guideline explicitly building shared decision-making into the recommendation structure rather than dictating a single "correct" answer.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology jointly (Heart Team) for any multivessel disease where the revascularization strategy isn't already obvious from anatomy alone.
Urgent surgical evaluation
Left main disease, especially with hemodynamic compromise or ongoing ischemia — this often needs expedited surgical timing rather than routine scheduling.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
Any patient where CABG is the preferred or only reasonable revascularization option and local surgical capability doesn't exist.
Transport considerations
Standard ACS/ischemia transport precautions apply while awaiting definitive revascularization — see Vol. 4 (ACS Deep Dive) for the acute management framework that applies until the surgical/interventional decision is made.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery and interventional cardiology together for genuine Heart Team discussion — avoid a single-specialty referral pattern for complex multivessel disease where the guideline explicitly calls for joint input.

PCI & Stent Placement

The percutaneous counterpart to CABG — same underlying goal of restoring coronary flow, completely different mechanism, complication profile, and long-term surveillance needs.

Stent Evolution & Thrombosis Timing

Top: why drug-eluting stents became the default choice for most PCI. Bottom: the ARC (Academic Research Consortium) timing classification for stent thrombosis — timing directly points toward the likely mechanism.
Stent thrombosis is not the same thing as in-stent restenosis

These are frequently confused but represent opposite-tempo problems. Stent thrombosis is abrupt, complete vessel occlusion — it presents as a STEMI, develops over minutes, and carries 20-45% mortality. In-stent restenosis is gradual neointimal overgrowth narrowing the stented segment over months — it presents as recurrent, often exertional angina, developing slowly enough to be caught electively in most cases. Don't let the similar-sounding names blur two completely different acuity levels.

Indications — When PCI Is Chosen Over CABG

See the CABG section for the full 2021 ACC/AHA/SCAI anatomic-complexity framework — the short version: PCI is generally preferred for lower-complexity disease (1-2 vessel, no proximal LAD involvement), is a reasonable alternative in intermediate-complexity disease pending Heart Team discussion, and is generally favored over CABG in the acute STEMI/culprit-vessel setting given the time-sensitivity of reperfusion (see Vol. 4's Reperfusion Strategy section).

Procedural Complications Worth Knowing

Coronary dissection/perforation

Guidewire or balloon-related vessel injury during the procedure itself — small dissections are often successfully sealed with additional stenting, but perforation (especially with pericardial extension) can produce acute tamponade requiring emergent pericardiocentesis and sometimes surgical repair.

No-reflow phenomenon

Restoration of flow in the epicardial artery without corresponding improvement in myocardial perfusion — from distal microembolization and microvascular dysfunction, most common with large thrombus burden. Intracoronary vasodilators (adenosine, nitroprusside, verapamil) are used to try to restore microvascular flow.

Access site complications

Radial access has become the preferred default over femoral in most contemporary practice specifically because it substantially reduces access-site bleeding and vascular complications — though radial artery occlusion (usually asymptomatic given dual hand blood supply, but not universally so) is its own recognized complication. Femoral access carries the more classically taught complications: retroperitoneal hemorrhage, pseudoaneurysm, and arteriovenous fistula formation (see Vascular Vol. 2's Vascular Trauma section for the general hard-sign/soft-sign framework that applies to any access-site vascular injury).

DAPT — Cross-Reference, Don't Duplicate

The full antiplatelet selection and duration framework (ticagrelor/prasugrel vs. clopidogrel, the 2025 guideline's specific recommendations, CABG hold times) lives in Vol. 4's Antiplatelet Therapy section — it applies identically whether the stent was placed electively for stable disease or emergently for ACS. The one stent-specific addition worth emphasizing here: DAPT discontinuation is one of the strongest modifiable risk factors for late and very late stent thrombosis — any elective procedure or medication change being considered for a recent stent recipient needs a specific conversation about whether and how DAPT can be safely interrupted, rather than a default assumption that it's fine to hold for a few days.

Surveillance is symptom-driven, not routine-imaging-driven

Unlike some surgical valve/graft scenarios with defined surveillance imaging intervals, stented coronary segments are generally not re-imaged on a fixed schedule — recurrent angina or a positive stress test prompts repeat angiography, rather than routine periodic angiography in an asymptomatic patient. This makes patient education about recognizing recurrent anginal symptoms genuinely important, not just a formality.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Interventional cardiology for any suspected stent thrombosis (treat as STEMI — see Vol. 4) or in-stent restenosis (recurrent angina in a prior PCI patient).
Emergent evaluation
Any suspected coronary perforation with hemodynamic compromise — this needs immediate pericardiocentesis capability and cardiac surgery backup on standby.
Urgent evaluation
New access-site complications (expanding hematoma, new bruit/thrill suggesting pseudoaneurysm or AV fistula, or limb ischemia from radial occlusion) — see Vascular Vol. 2 for the general vascular access complication framework.
Transfer if no interventional cardiology on-site
Suspected stent thrombosis (emergent, same urgency as any STEMI) or in-stent restenosis requiring repeat angiography/intervention.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology directly for any stent-related complication; cardiac surgery on standby for suspected perforation with tamponade physiology.

Valve Surgery

Durability without anticoagulation, or anticoagulation without reoperation — the mechanical-vs-bioprosthetic decision is one of the clearest "there is no free lunch" conversations in all of cardiac surgery.

Mechanical vs. Bioprosthetic

The core tradeoff: mechanical valves last essentially forever but demand lifelong anticoagulation; bioprosthetic valves need no long-term anticoagulation but will eventually degenerate and may require reoperation (or valve-in-valve TAVR).
DOACs are contraindicated with mechanical valves — full stop

This is one of the few absolute DOAC contraindications in cardiology. The RE-ALIGN trial specifically tested dabigatran against warfarin in mechanical valve patients and stopped early due to excess thromboembolic and bleeding events in the dabigatran arm. Warfarin remains mandatory for mechanical valves regardless of how appealing a DOAC's ease-of-use profile is elsewhere in a patient's regimen.

Valve-in-Valve TAVR — Changing the Calculus

The growing availability of transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures for degenerated bioprosthetic valves has meaningfully shifted this conversation over the past decade — a bioprosthetic valve no longer necessarily means an open reoperation when it eventually degenerates. This has pushed some centers toward a lower age threshold for bioprosthetic valve selection than was traditional, since the "reoperation" penalty of a bioprosthetic choice is now often a catheter-based procedure rather than repeat open-heart surgery.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery and cardiology jointly for any valve replacement decision — this conversation benefits from both perspectives, plus the patient's own values around anticoagulation and reoperation risk.
Urgent evaluation
Suspected prosthetic valve dysfunction (new murmur change, new heart failure symptoms, or hemolysis suggesting paravalvular leak) in any valve replacement patient — this needs prompt echocardiographic evaluation.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery/structural heart program on-site
Suspected prosthetic valve thrombosis or endocarditis, or any valve-in-valve candidacy evaluation for degenerated bioprosthetic valves.
Transport considerations
For suspected prosthetic valve thrombosis with hemodynamic compromise, treat with the same urgency as any other acute valvular emergency (see Vol. 1b) — this can deteriorate rapidly.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery for valve-related surgical questions; cardiology/structural heart specifically for valve-in-valve TAVR candidacy.

Aortic Surgery

Where the pathology lives on the aorta determines the entire surgical approach — building directly on the pathophysiology from Vascular Vol. 1's aortic aneurysm and dissection sections.

Open Repair vs. TEVAR

Ascending/arch disease still generally requires open surgical repair; descending disease is increasingly managed endovascularly. Arch involvement adds the unique physiologic challenge of circulatory arrest.
Endoleak — the price of avoiding open surgery

TEVAR trades the morbidity of open surgery for a different long-term surveillance burden: endoleak, where blood continues to flow into the aneurysm sac around or through the stent-graft despite exclusion. Type I (attachment site) and Type III (graft component) endoleaks generally require reintervention given ongoing aneurysm pressurization risk; Type II (retrograde flow from branch vessels) is often observed unless the sac is enlarging. Lifelong surveillance imaging is a real commitment for any TEVAR patient, not a one-time procedure with no further follow-up.

Circulatory Arrest — What to Watch For Post-Op

Any patient who underwent deep hypothermic circulatory arrest for arch repair carries meaningfully elevated risk of neurologic injury (stroke, delirium, or more subtle cognitive changes) proportional to the arrest duration — cerebral perfusion adjuncts (antegrade or retrograde) extend the safe window but don't eliminate the risk entirely. A new or worsening neurologic exam finding in this population deserves urgent neurology involvement and imaging; don't attribute it reflexively to routine post-operative sedation or delirium without a more complete evaluation.

Bentall procedure — when root and valve both need replacement

When aortic root dilation coexists with aortic valve disease (common in Marfan syndrome and other aortopathies — see Vascular Vol. 1), a Bentall procedure replaces the aortic root, valve, and reimplants the coronary ostia into the new graft in a single composite operation — a more complex undertaking than isolated valve or isolated root replacement, reflecting the combined pathology.

Post-op aortic surgery patients presenting with new pain

New or recurrent chest/back pain in a patient with a history of aortic surgery (open or endovascular) should prompt urgent imaging to evaluate for graft-related complications — anastomotic pseudoaneurysm, graft infection, or a new dissection at a different aortic segment. Don't assume "they already had their aorta fixed" means the aorta is no longer a consideration in the differential.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiothoracic surgery for any ascending/arch pathology requiring repair; vascular surgery or cardiothoracic surgery (per local practice) for descending pathology being considered for TEVAR.
Emergent evaluation
Any new pain in a patient with prior aortic surgery, or any suspected graft infection (fever, positive blood cultures, imaging findings of perigraft fluid/air) — graft infection is a genuine surgical emergency with high mortality if untreated.
Transfer if no cardiothoracic/vascular surgery on-site
Any acute aortic pathology requiring surgical or endovascular intervention, following the same urgency framework as native aortic dissection/aneurysm (see Vascular Vol. 1).
Transport considerations
Blood pressure control during transport for any suspected acute graft complication, similar to native aortic emergencies; avoid unnecessary Valsalva or straining.
Who to call
Cardiothoracic surgery for ascending/arch disease; vascular surgery or cardiothoracic surgery for descending disease depending on local program structure; infectious disease co-management for any suspected graft infection.

LVAD & Mechanical Circulatory Support

These patients present to your ED whether or not you've cared for one before — a continuous-flow LVAD changes nearly every reflexive assumption in a standard cardiovascular assessment.

The Continuous Flow Circuit (animated)

Blood is continuously drawn from the LV apex, driven through a magnetically levitated centrifugal pump (HeartMate 3 is the current dominant device), and returned to the ascending aorta — with a percutaneous driveline as the connection to the external controller and batteries.
No pulse does not mean no perfusion

Continuous-flow physiology means many LVAD patients have a weak or entirely absent palpable pulse and an unreadable blood pressure by standard automated cuff — this is expected, not a sign of cardiac arrest. Use a Doppler probe over the brachial artery with a manual blood pressure cuff to obtain a mean arterial pressure (the "Doppler opening pressure" approximates MAP in continuous-flow physiology) rather than assuming hemodynamic collapse from an unreadable standard vital sign.

Interactive: Working Through an LVAD Alarm

Every LVAD program has its own specific protocol and 24/7 coordinator line — call it early. This is a general framework for thinking through the most common alarm scenario, not a substitute for that call.

Step 1 — Is this a low-flow alarm?

Low flow can result from hypovolemia/dehydration (the most common benign cause — the pump can't draw adequate volume from an underfilled LV), a suction event (the inflow cannula abutting the septum or LV wall, often positional and resolves with fluid or repositioning), pump thrombosis, or true cardiogenic causes (tamponade, worsening RV failure). Check the pump parameters (flow, power, pulsatility index) alongside the clinical picture rather than treating the alarm in isolation.

Step 2 — Check power consumption alongside flow

Rising power with rising or unstable flow patterns raises concern for pump thrombosis — the rotor is working harder against an obstruction. Falling flow with normal or low power more often reflects a volume/preload problem (hypovolemia, suction event, tamponade) than a mechanical pump problem. This power-flow relationship is the single most useful piece of data for narrowing the differential before the LVAD coordinator arrives.

Step 3 — Suspected pump thrombosis: what changes management

Rising LDH and plasma-free hemoglobin support the diagnosis alongside the power/flow pattern above. Pump exchange is the definitive treatment for confirmed thrombosis — this is a conversation for the LVAD/cardiac surgery team, not an ED-level intervention, but recognizing the pattern early and calling appropriately matters enormously for outcome.

Step 4 — Suspected hypovolemia/suction event

Cautious fluid administration is often the correct initial response — but remember these patients frequently have concurrent heart failure physiology and impaired RV function, so this isn't unlimited volume resuscitation. Reassess flow parameters after a modest fluid challenge rather than repeatedly bolusing without reassessment.

GI Bleeding — The Complication You'll See Most Often

Acquired von Willebrand syndrome

The shear stress of continuous-flow pumps mechanically cleaves high-molecular-weight von Willebrand factor multimers — virtually all LVAD patients develop this acquired coagulopathy, and it combines with mandatory anticoagulation, reduced arterial pulsatility (promoting intestinal angiodysplasia/AVM formation), and antiplatelet therapy to produce GI bleeding rates as high as 24.5% at five years in contemporary HeartMate 3 data (MOMENTUM 3 trial). This is the most common reason for LVAD-related hospital readmission — expect to see it.

Management follows standard GI bleeding principles (endoscopic evaluation, transfusion as needed) with the added wrinkle of balancing anticoagulation reduction against pump thrombosis risk — this balance is worked out jointly with the LVAD team and hematology, not unilaterally in the ED. Octreotide has some evidence for reducing rebleeding in refractory LVAD-associated GI bleeding from angiodysplasia specifically.

Driveline Infection

The percutaneous driveline exit site is a permanent breach in the skin barrier and the most common site of LVAD-related infection — redness, drainage, or pain at the exit site warrants cultures and often prolonged antibiotic therapy, since driveline infections can be difficult to fully eradicate without removing the device (not generally an option) and can seed the pump itself if untreated.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Call the LVAD coordinator immediately
For any LVAD alarm, suspected malfunction, or significant clinical change — every program has a 24/7 line, and this call should happen in parallel with, not after, initial ED evaluation.
Consult now
Cardiac surgery/advanced heart failure for suspected pump thrombosis, significant GI bleeding requiring anticoagulation adjustment decisions, or any driveline infection.
ICU admission
Any hemodynamic instability, suspected pump thrombosis, or significant GI bleeding in an LVAD patient — these patients decompensate along a different physiologic pathway than standard cardiac patients and need LVAD-experienced critical care.
Transfer if no LVAD program on-site
Essentially any significant LVAD complication should prompt early discussion with the implanting center, even if initial stabilization happens locally — LVAD management genuinely benefits from program-specific familiarity with the exact device and the patient's baseline parameters.
Transport considerations
Ensure backup batteries and controller accompany the patient; a transport team with LVAD-specific training is strongly preferred; never disconnect or silence an alarming device without understanding the cause — troubleshoot per protocol or with the coordinator on the line.
Who to call
The patient's LVAD coordinator/implanting center first and always — they know the specific device, baseline parameters, and recent trends that make remote troubleshooting far more effective than starting from scratch.

Heart Transplant

A denervated heart breaks nearly every reflexive teaching point about cardiac symptoms and pharmacology — silent ischemia is the rule, and atropine simply doesn't work.

Rejection: Three Types by Timing and Mechanism

Hyperacute rejection is now rare given modern crossmatch testing; acute cellular rejection is screened for via surveillance biopsy in the first year; chronic rejection (cardiac allograft vasculopathy) is the long-term threat that silently limits graft survival.
The denervated heart has no angina warning system

Afferent sympathetic fibers are severed during transplantation and typically don't meaningfully reinnervate — a transplant recipient having a significant coronary event from cardiac allograft vasculopathy usually feels nothing resembling classic angina. This is why surveillance coronary angiography (often annual) substitutes for symptom-triggered workup in this population, and why any transplant recipient with dyspnea, fatigue, or new heart failure symptoms deserves a lower threshold for cardiac workup than the absence of chest pain might otherwise suggest.

Pharmacology Changes Too

Atropine doesn't work

Atropine acts by blocking vagal (parasympathetic) tone on the SA node — but the denervated transplanted heart has no vagal innervation to block. Symptomatic bradycardia in a transplant recipient needs direct-acting agents (isoproterenol, epinephrine) or pacing, not atropine, which will simply fail.

Blunted exercise response

Heart rate rises during exercise in a transplanted heart via circulating catecholamines rather than direct sympathetic neural input — the response is slower to onset and slower to recover than in an innervated heart, which is a normal finding in this population rather than a sign of chronotropic incompetence requiring intervention.

Immunosuppression & Infection Risk

Standard maintenance immunosuppression combines a calcineurin inhibitor (tacrolimus or cyclosporine), an antiproliferative agent (mycophenolate mofetil), with or without corticosteroids — each with its own toxicity profile (nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity with calcineurin inhibitors, GI and hematologic effects with mycophenolate). Any transplant recipient presenting with fever needs a broadened infectious differential reflecting chronic immunosuppression: CMV reactivation, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, invasive fungal infection, and atypical presentations of otherwise-common infections given blunted immune response.

Drug interactions are a constant background consideration

Calcineurin inhibitors are metabolized through CYP3A4 — a huge range of commonly prescribed medications (many antibiotics, antifungals, and even some over-the-counter supplements) can meaningfully raise or lower tacrolimus/cyclosporine levels. Any new medication being added to a transplant recipient's regimen deserves a quick interaction check, and involving the transplant program before making changes is generally the safer default.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
The transplant cardiology program for essentially any acute presentation in a heart transplant recipient — they maintain detailed knowledge of the patient's rejection history, current immunosuppression regimen, and recent biopsy/surveillance results that meaningfully change management.
Urgent evaluation
New heart failure symptoms, new arrhythmia, or unexplained fatigue/malaise in a transplant recipient — remember these often represent silent rejection or silent ischemia given denervation, not the more obvious presentations expected in a native heart.
ICU admission
Any hemodynamic instability, suspected acute rejection with graft dysfunction, or significant opportunistic infection in a transplant recipient.
Transfer if no transplant cardiology program on-site
Essentially any significant acute presentation — transplant recipients benefit enormously from care coordinated with their specific transplant program given the complexity of immunosuppression management and rejection surveillance history.
Who to call
The patient's transplant cardiology program directly and early — most programs have a dedicated coordinator line specifically for this purpose, similar to LVAD program structure.

Post-Cardiac Surgery Complications

Closing this volume with a timing framework — much like Vol. 5's post-MI complications timeline, knowing what postoperative day it is dramatically narrows the differential for a new symptom.

The Postoperative Timeline

Post-operative atrial fibrillation affects roughly 30% of cardiac surgery patients and peaks days 2-4 — it's common enough that it should be anticipated and monitored for, not treated as a surprising complication.

Expandable: New Symptom by Postoperative Day

Hours to Day 1 — hemodynamic instability or high chest tube output

Bleeding is the dominant early concern — track chest tube output closely (a sudden decrease in a previously draining tube can indicate clot obstruction and tamponade physiology developing, not necessarily reassurance that bleeding has stopped). Sudden hemodynamic deterioration with low or absent tube output should raise immediate suspicion for tamponade — urgent bedside echo and surgical involvement, don't wait.

Days 2-4 — new arrhythmia (usually AFib)

Post-operative AFib is common enough (~30% incidence) that most programs have a standard protocol (typically beta-blockade continuation/initiation, with rate or rhythm control individualized). It's generally self-limited and resolves as post-operative inflammation settles, but still warrants standard AFib stroke-risk and anticoagulation consideration if it persists beyond the immediate post-operative window (see Vol. 3's Atrial Fibrillation section for the full framework).

Week 1-2 — fever, wound changes, or sternal instability

Superficial sternal wound infections are more common and less concerning than deep sternal wound infection/mediastinitis — but any sternal click, instability, or deep/purulent drainage with fever needs urgent surgical evaluation. Mediastinitis is a serious, potentially life-threatening infection requiring aggressive surgical debridement and prolonged antibiotics, not outpatient oral antibiotic management.

Weeks to months — pericardial symptoms or graft concerns

Post-pericardiotomy syndrome (an autoimmune pericarditis analogous to Dressler's syndrome after MI — see Vol. 5) can present with pleuritic chest pain, fever, and a new or recurrent pericardial effusion weeks after surgery. Late graft occlusion (saphenous vein grafts most vulnerable, given their lower long-term patency compared to arterial conduits) can present as recurrent angina or a new ACS — the timeline and graft type both inform how strongly to suspect this versus native disease progression.

Post-pericardiotomy syndrome can progress to tamponade

Though generally self-limited and managed like other pericarditis (NSAIDs, colchicine — see Vol. 1b), a significant pericardial effusion from post-pericardiotomy syndrome can occasionally progress to tamponade physiology, particularly if missed or undertreated. Any recent cardiac surgery patient with recurrent chest pain, new dyspnea, or hemodynamic changes deserves an echo to assess effusion size, not just symptomatic anti-inflammatory treatment.

Delirium is common and usually multifactorial

Post-cardiac surgery delirium, particularly in the first 1-2 weeks, is common and typically reflects a combination of factors — sleep disruption, medication effects, underlying cognitive vulnerability, and the physiologic stress of major surgery (amplified further by cardiopulmonary bypass or circulatory arrest when used). Standard delirium management principles apply, but a new or worsening neurologic exam finding should still prompt consideration of a focal neurologic event rather than being reflexively attributed to "ICU delirium" without at least a basic evaluation.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery for any suspected surgical complication (bleeding, tamponade, wound concerns, sternal instability) — these patients should generally have a low threshold for direct surgical team involvement given the recency of their operation.
Emergent evaluation
Suspected tamponade at any postoperative timepoint, or any hemodynamic instability with unclear cause in a recent cardiac surgery patient.
ICU admission
Any hemodynamic instability, significant arrhythmia requiring active management, or suspected mediastinitis/deep sternal wound infection.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
Any suspected surgical complication in a patient no longer at the original surgical center — coordinate with the original surgical team when possible given their direct knowledge of the operative details, even if immediate stabilization happens locally.
Who to call
The original cardiac surgery team when feasible, given their specific knowledge of the operative course; local cardiac surgery/cardiology for immediate stabilization needs when the original team isn't readily reachable.

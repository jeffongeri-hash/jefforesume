Vol. 3 · Region 1 · Esophagus

Esophageal Motility Disorders — Chicago Classification v4.0

High-resolution manometry turned esophageal dysmotility from a barium-swallow impression into a precise, pressure-topography diagnosis — and achalasia's three subtypes carry genuinely different prognoses.

The Diagnostic FrameworkIntegrated Relaxation Pressure First

High-resolution manometry (HRM) measures pressure continuously along the esophagus during 10 wet swallows, generating a color pressure-topography plot. The first branch point in Chicago Classification v4.0 is the integrated relaxation pressure (IRP) at the esophagogastric junction — normal is <15mmHg. An elevated IRP means the EGJ isn't relaxing properly on swallowing, splitting the differential into achalasia (if peristalsis is also absent) versus EGJ outflow obstruction (if peristalsis is preserved).

AchalasiaThree Subtypes, Three Prognoses

Building directly on GI Atlas Vol. 1's introduction of achalasia as selective loss of inhibitory (NO/VIP) myenteric neurons — HRM subtyping refines that single diagnosis into three patterns with meaningfully different treatment responses.

SubtypeManometric patternClinical relevance
Type IElevated IRP, 100% failed peristalsis, no panesophageal pressurizationThe "classic" pattern — a flaccid, dilated esophageal body with no contractile activity at all
Type IIElevated IRP, failed peristalsis, panesophageal pressurization in ≥20% of swallows (the entire esophageal column pressurizes as one unit, since it can't peristalse but retains enough wall tension to trap and compress swallowed content)Best prognosis and best response to treatment of the three subtypes — this compressive pressurization pattern appears to predict a more treatment-responsive disease course
Type IIIElevated IRP, premature/spastic contractions, no appreciable peristalsisWorst prognosis — the spastic component reflects a more advanced or distinct pathophysiology, generally responding less completely to standard myotomy/dilation than Type II
Why subtype changes the treatment conversation
Because Type II carries the best prognosis and Type III the worst, subtyping isn't just descriptive — it directly informs counseling about expected treatment response before choosing between pneumatic dilation, Heller myotomy, and POEM (peroral endoscopic myotomy), and helps set expectations for patients with Type III disease who may need more aggressive or combined approaches.

EGJ Outflow ObstructionElevated Pressure, Preserved Peristalsis

Elevated IRP with preserved peristalsis — Chicago Classification v4.0 now requires supportive findings (symptoms, elevated intrabolus pressure, or an abnormal timed barium esophagogram/FLIP result) before treating this as clinically meaningful, since an isolated manometric IRP elevation without corroborating evidence is common and often not clinically significant. Four subgroups exist based on the peristalsis pattern (spastic, hypercontractile, ineffective, or normal peristalsis) — a genuinely heterogeneous category whose natural history is incompletely understood, which is why the working group specifically cautions against irreversible treatments (myotomy, dilation) without more corroborating evidence, given that spontaneous symptom resolution is common.

Secondary EGJOO — Don't Skip the Structural Workup

EGJOO can be mimicked by mechanical causes — hiatal hernia, EGJ tumor/infiltration, or prior fundoplication — that produce the identical manometric pressure pattern without any underlying neuromuscular disorder. Cross-sectional imaging or endoscopy to exclude a structural/mechanical cause is a necessary step before labeling a case idiopathic.

Scleroderma EsophagusA Distinct, Non-Neurogenic Mechanism

Unlike achalasia (neuronal loss) or jackhammer esophagus (excessive excitatory drive), scleroderma's esophageal involvement reflects smooth muscle atrophy and fibrosis directly — the muscle itself, not its innervation, is replaced by fibrous tissue. This produces absent peristalsis in the smooth-muscle-dependent distal two-thirds of the esophagus (the proximal striated-muscle segment is typically spared) combined with a markedly hypotensive LES — the opposite EGJ problem from achalasia's hypertensive, non-relaxing sphincter. This combination (weak body + weak LES) is why scleroderma patients suffer severe reflux disease rather than the obstructive dysphagia pattern of achalasia, despite both being called "esophageal motility disorders."

Vol. 3 · Region 2 · Stomach

Gastroparesis

Delayed gastric emptying without mechanical obstruction — and the introduction of the interstitial cells of Cajal, the GI tract's own pacemaker cells, whose loss underlies much of this section and the small bowel dysmotility section that follows.

Interstitial Cells of CajalThe GI Tract's Pacemaker Cells

ICCs form a specialized network within the muscularis, generating spontaneous, rhythmic slow waves of electrical depolarization that set the basic electrical rhythm the smooth muscle contracts around — gastric slow waves cycle at ~3/minute, distinct from the frequency in other gut regions. ICCs also relay neurotransmitter signals (including the NO/VIP inhibitory and ACh excitatory signals introduced throughout GI Atlas Vol. 1) from enteric neurons to smooth muscle, functioning as an intermediary rather than just a standalone pacemaker. Loss or dysfunction of ICCs is now understood as a central mechanism in both gastroparesis and the small bowel/colonic dysmotility syndromes covered later in this volume.

Etiologies

CauseMechanism
Diabetic (most common identifiable cause)Chronic hyperglycemia damages both vagal nerve fibers and ICCs directly — a visceral analog of diabetic peripheral neuropathy affecting the gut's own wiring and pacemaker network together
IdiopathicThe single largest category — often post-viral onset, suggesting an infectious/postinfectious autoimmune-adjacent mechanism in at least a subset, though the precise process remains incompletely defined
Post-surgicalVagal nerve injury during upper GI, bariatric, or antireflux surgery
MedicationsOpioids (the same enteric μ-receptor mechanism from GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section, here slowing gastric emptying specifically), anticholinergics, GLP-1 receptor agonists (an expected, dose-related effect given GLP-1's physiologic role in delaying gastric emptying — Vol. 1)

Diagnosis

Requires excluding mechanical obstruction first (upper endoscopy) — gastroparesis is a functional diagnosis, and a mechanical outlet obstruction (e.g., a stricture or mass) can produce an identical clinical picture. The diagnostic gold standard is 4-hour gastric emptying scintigraphy — retention >10% at 4 hours (or >60% at 2 hours) confirms delayed emptying. Wireless motility capsule testing is an alternative where available.

TreatmentEscalating by Severity

  1. Dietary modification — small, frequent, low-fat, low-fiber meals (fat and fiber both physiologically slow gastric emptying even in healthy stomachs — see GI Atlas Vol. 1's CCK discussion — so reducing them removes an additional emptying-delaying signal on top of the underlying disease).
  2. Prokinetics — metoclopramide (D2 antagonist + some 5-HT4 agonist activity, only FDA-approved long-term option in the US but limited by a boxed warning for tardive dyskinesia with prolonged use), erythromycin (motilin receptor agonist, Vol. 1 — effective but subject to rapid tachyphylaxis), domperidone (D2 antagonist, not FDA-approved in the US but widely used elsewhere, requiring cardiac QT monitoring).
  3. Antiemetics for symptom control — 5-HT3 antagonists (Vol. 1) alongside prokinetic therapy.
  4. Gastric electrical stimulation — an implanted device providing high-frequency electrical stimulation; improves nausea/vomiting more reliably than it improves actual emptying times, suggesting a partly separate mechanism from simply correcting the motility defect.
  5. G-POEM (gastric peroral endoscopic myotomy) — an endoscopic pyloromyotomy, cutting the pyloric sphincter to reduce outlet resistance; an increasingly used option for refractory cases, conceptually similar to how POEM addresses the LES in achalasia (this volume's Esophageal Motility Disorders section) but applied to the pylorus instead.
Vol. 3 · Region 3 · Small Intestine

Small Bowel Dysmotility

When the migrating motor complex from GI Atlas Vol. 1 breaks down entirely — from a mechanical-mimicking pseudo-obstruction syndrome to the bacterial overgrowth that follows when the housekeeping wave stops sweeping.

Chronic Intestinal Pseudo-Obstruction (CIPO)

A syndrome of recurrent symptoms and radiographic signs of bowel obstruction — dilated loops, air-fluid levels — without any mechanical blockage. Reflects severe, diffuse dysfunction of the smooth muscle (myopathic), enteric neurons (neuropathic), or interstitial cells of Cajal (this volume's Gastroparesis section) — often overlapping categories rather than one clean mechanism.

CategoryCause
MyopathicVisceral myopathies (familial or sporadic), scleroderma (the same smooth muscle fibrosis mechanism from this volume's Esophageal Motility Disorders section, extending distally), amyloidosis, mitochondrial disorders
NeuropathicParaneoplastic (anti-Hu antibodies, classically associated with small cell lung cancer — autoimmune destruction of enteric neurons), Parkinson's disease, Chagas disease (Vol. 1), diabetic autonomic neuropathy
MesenchymopathicICC network loss/dysfunction, mechanistically parallel to gastroparesis but affecting the small bowel's pacemaker network instead

Management is largely supportive — nutritional support (often requiring parenteral nutrition in severe cases, intersecting with GI Atlas Vol. 1's short bowel syndrome discussion around GLP-2/teduglutide, though CIPO's problem is motility rather than absorptive surface area), prokinetics with variable efficacy, and treating superimposed bacterial overgrowth (below) — surgery is generally avoided given the diffuse nature of the underlying dysfunction, unlike a focal mechanical obstruction.

Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth (SIBO)The Direct Consequence of a Broken Housekeeping Wave

As established in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Foundational Principles section, the fasting-state migrating motor complex — driven by motilin — exists specifically to sweep bacteria and debris distally between meals. Any process that disrupts this wave (CIPO above, scleroderma, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, or a structural problem like a blind loop or stricture that physically traps bacteria out of the sweep's reach) allows colonic-type bacterial concentrations to establish in the small bowel, where they don't belong.

ConsequenceMechanism
Bloating, gasBacterial fermentation of luminal carbohydrate before the host can absorb it
Fat malabsorptionBacterial deconjugation of bile acids (interfering with the micelle-formation step from GI Atlas Vol. 2's Fat Absorption section) — deconjugated bile acids are prematurely absorbed and less effective at solubilizing lipid
B12 deficiencyBacteria directly consume luminal B12 before the host's intrinsic-factor-mediated uptake (Vol. 2) can occur
Elevated, not decreased, folateSome bacterial species synthesize folate, distinguishing SIBO from most other malabsorptive processes on a basic vitamin panel — a genuinely useful, easily overlooked lab clue

Diagnosed via hydrogen/methane breath testing (lactulose or glucose substrate — an early or exaggerated rise reflects bacterial fermentation occurring too proximally/too soon) or, less commonly now, direct jejunal aspirate culture. Treated with targeted antibiotics (rifaximin, given its minimal systemic absorption and gut-restricted action) alongside addressing the underlying motility disorder driving recurrence — treating the overgrowth without addressing the motility cause typically leads to relapse.

Vol. 3 · Region 4 · Colon

Colonic Motility Disorders

IBS's current Rome IV framework, slow-transit constipation as a distinct entity from pelvic floor dysfunction (covered next section), and diverticular disease reframed as fundamentally a motility problem.

IBSRome IV Criteria

Recurrent abdominal pain, on average ≥1 day/week in the last 3 months (with symptom onset ≥6 months prior), associated with ≥2 of: related to defecation, associated with a change in stool frequency, or associated with a change in stool form/appearance. Subtyped by predominant stool pattern:

IBS-C (constipation-predominant) IBS-D (diarrhea-predominant) IBS-M (mixed) IBS-U (unclassified)

Building on GI Atlas Vol. 1's introduction of serotonin signaling dysregulation as one implicated IBS mechanism: altered motility (both accelerated transit in IBS-D and slowed transit in IBS-C), visceral hypersensitivity (an exaggerated pain response to normal degrees of luminal distension — not necessarily more distension itself, but a lower threshold for perceiving it as painful), and gut microbiome alterations are all considered contributing, overlapping mechanisms rather than a single unifying cause. Treatment is matched to the predominant pattern: 5-HT3 antagonists or bile acid sequestrants for IBS-D (also worth excluding bile acid malabsorption directly — GI Atlas Vol. 2's Biliary Secretion section — as a mimicking diagnosis), 5-HT4 agonists/secretagogues (linaclotide, plecanatide) for IBS-C, and antispasmodics or neuromodulators (low-dose tricyclics) across subtypes for pain-predominant presentations.

Slow-Transit ConstipationA Distinct Motility Entity

Distinguished from IBS-C (where pain is a defining feature) and from pelvic floor/dyssynergic defecation (next section, where the problem is evacuation mechanics rather than transit speed itself) by colonic transit studies (radiopaque marker study or wireless motility capsule) showing genuinely delayed movement through the colon, generally without a proportionate pain component. Reflects either reduced high-amplitude propagating contractions (the colon's main propulsive motor pattern) or an underlying neuromuscular/ICC-network problem analogous to the mechanisms covered in the Gastroparesis and Small Bowel Dysmotility sections, just localized to the colon instead. Distinguishing slow-transit constipation from dyssynergic defecation matters clinically because they're treated completely differently — prokinetic/secretagogue therapy for slow transit versus biofeedback/pelvic floor retraining for dyssynergia (next section) — and a patient can have either, or both simultaneously.

Diverticular DiseaseReframed as a Motility Problem

Colonic diverticula form at points of relative structural weakness — where the vasa recta penetrate the muscularis — but elevated intraluminal pressure from segmental colonic motor activity is the mechanical driver pushing mucosa/submucosa outward through those weak points. Low dietary fiber intake is the classic implicated risk factor, thought to act by increasing colonic muscular segmentation (higher-pressure, shorter-interval contractions against harder, lower-volume stool) rather than through any direct structural effect — directly tying diverticular disease's development back to the same colonic motor physiology covered throughout this section rather than treating it as a purely structural/anatomic diagnosis.

Vol. 3 · Region 5 · Anorectum

Anorectal Disorders

Defecation is the one GI motor act under substantial voluntary control — which means its disorders are as often about learned muscle coordination as about the smooth muscle physiology covered everywhere else in this atlas.

Normal DefecationA Coordinated, Partly Voluntary Reflex

Rectal distension triggers the rectoanal inhibitory reflex — the internal anal sphincter (smooth muscle, involuntary) relaxes automatically, allowing rectal contents to contact sensory epithelium that distinguishes gas from liquid from solid stool ("sampling"). Voluntary continence is then maintained by external anal sphincter and puborectalis contraction (both skeletal muscle, under voluntary/somatic control) until defecation is socially convenient, at which point these skeletal muscles voluntarily relax and are joined by increased intra-abdominal (Valsalva) pressure to complete evacuation. This dual smooth-muscle/skeletal-muscle, involuntary/voluntary architecture is unique in the GI tract — every other sphincter covered in this atlas (LES, pyloric sphincter, Sphincter of Oddi) is smooth muscle and involuntary throughout.

Dyssynergic DefecationA Learned Coordination Failure

Rather than relaxing the puborectalis and external anal sphincter during attempted defecation, patients with dyssynergic defecation paradoxically contract them — functionally fighting their own evacuation attempt. This is fundamentally a coordination/learned-behavior problem (sometimes originating from childhood stool-withholding behavior, or developing after a painful defecation episode that reinforces sphincter guarding) rather than a structural or smooth-muscle neuromuscular disease — which is exactly why it's diagnosed by anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion testing (demonstrating the paradoxical contraction pattern directly) and treated with biofeedback therapy retraining the correct relaxation sequence, rather than with the prokinetic/secretagogue medications used for slow-transit constipation (previous section). Distinguishing the two matters directly: treating dyssynergia with laxatives alone, without addressing the coordination problem, tends to fail.

Fecal IncontinenceMultiple Possible Failure Points

CauseMechanism
Obstetric/surgical sphincter injuryDirect mechanical disruption of the external anal sphincter's structural integrity — the most common identifiable cause in women
Pudendal neuropathyChronic straining (itself often from the dyssynergic or slow-transit constipation above) can stretch and damage the pudendal nerve over time, weakening voluntary sphincter control — a case of one motility disorder mechanically causing a second, distinct one
Diminished rectal sensationDiabetic autonomic neuropathy or spinal cord disease impairing the sensory "sampling" reflex described above — stool arrives without adequate warning
Overflow incontinenceSevere fecal impaction (often from unrecognized slow-transit constipation or dyssynergia) with liquid stool leaking around the retained mass — a presentation that can be mistaken for primary diarrhea if the underlying impaction isn't examined for directly

Why This Section Closes the Colorectal Story

Constipation presenting at the bedside can reflect slow-transit disease (previous section — a smooth muscle/ICC problem), dyssynergic defecation (a learned skeletal muscle coordination problem), or both simultaneously — and fecal incontinence can result from a structural failure, a neuropathic failure, or as a downstream consequence of chronic untreated constipation from either of the first two categories. Sorting a single presenting symptom into the correct one (or combination) of these mechanistically distinct categories — rather than treating "constipation" or "incontinence" as a single diagnosis — is the practical takeaway tying together this section and the two colonic/small bowel sections before it.

Vol. 3 · Cross-Cutting · Postoperative

Postoperative Ileus

The closing section pulls together nearly every inhibitory mechanism covered in this atlas — neurogenic, inflammatory, and pharmacologic brakes on motility, all firing at once after abdominal surgery.

Three Overlapping Mechanisms

MechanismDetail
NeurogenicSurgical handling activates inhibitory sympathetic reflexes (spinal and adrenergic) that suppress motility — an exaggerated, temporary version of the normal sympathetic inhibitory tone present throughout the gut
InflammatoryBowel manipulation triggers local release of inflammatory mediators and activates resident intestinal macrophages, which directly suppress smooth muscle contractility in the manipulated segment — a self-limited inflammatory brake proportional to the degree of surgical handling
PharmacologicPerioperative and postoperative opioids act on the same enteric μ-opioid receptors introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Small Intestine — Neurotransmitters section (the enkephalin system) — a major, modifiable contributor layered on top of the surgery-intrinsic neurogenic and inflammatory components

These three mechanisms don't resolve at the same pace: gastric and small bowel motility typically recover within 24–48 hours, while colonic motility lags, often taking 48–72+ hours to return — which is why clinical "ileus resolution" is generally paced by return of flatus/bowel movements (a colonic event) rather than by earlier signs like bowel sounds.

Prevention & ManagementERAS Protocols

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols bundle multiple interventions that each address one of the three mechanisms above:

Minimally invasive/laparoscopic technique — reduces bowel manipulation, directly limiting the inflammatory component Opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia (regional blocks, NSAIDs, acetaminophen) — directly reduces the pharmacologic component Early postoperative feeding — provides a physiologic motility stimulus rather than "resting" the gut, a reversal of older surgical dogma Early mobilization Peripherally-acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (alvimopan) — directly targets the pharmacologic mechanism, FDA-approved specifically to accelerate GI recovery after bowel resection, exploiting the identical peripheral-receptor-selectivity principle as the opioid-induced constipation reversal agents introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 1

Prolonged/Pathologic IleusWhen to Stop Calling It "Expected"

Ileus persisting beyond the typical recovery window (generally >3–5 days, depending on surgery type) should prompt reconsideration — excluding a mechanical small bowel obstruction (a true structural blockage, not a motility failure, requiring an entirely different management approach), electrolyte derangements (hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia both independently impair smooth muscle contractility, worth correcting directly rather than simply waiting out), and ongoing opioid exposure as a still-modifiable contributor even several days out from surgery.

Closing the Motility Volume

Postoperative ileus is, in miniature, a review of this entire volume: an ICC/smooth muscle component (Gastroparesis, Small Bowel Dysmotility sections), a neurogenic component (the sympathetic reflex, paralleling the enteric neuron mechanisms threaded throughout GI Atlas Vol. 1), and a pharmacologic opioid-receptor component (this volume's recurring callback to Vol. 1's enkephalin physiology) — all resolving in the region-specific sequence (gastric/small bowel first, colon last) that mirrors exactly how this volume itself was organized, region by region, from esophagus through anorectum.

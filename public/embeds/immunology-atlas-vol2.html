01 · Foundations

What It Truly Means to Be Immunocompromised

Not a checkbox on the chart — a graded, often multifactorial state that changes almost every downstream decision.

“The chart says ‘history of immunosuppression’ — does that mean I treat this fever completely differently, or is that label doing more work than it should?”

“Immunocompromised” gets used as a binary label — present or absent — when the reality is a continuous spectrum of degree, breadth, and duration. A patient on low-dose methotrexate for mild rheumatoid arthritis and a patient one week post-allogeneic stem cell transplant are both technically “immunocompromised,” but they carry almost nothing in common in terms of actual infection risk. The clinically useful question isn't whether a patient is immunocompromised, but which specific arm of host defense is impaired, how severely, and for how long — because that combination is what actually predicts which pathogens become dangerous.

Immunocompromise is often cumulative, not categorical

A patient doesn't need a single named diagnosis to be functionally immunocompromised. Advanced age, poorly controlled diabetes, malnutrition, chronic kidney disease/uremia, cirrhosis, and alcohol use disorder each blunt host defense modestly on their own — but stack several together (which is common) and the cumulative effect can rival a single “textbook” immunodeficiency. This is the patient most likely to be under-recognized as high-risk, because no single line on the problem list says “immunocompromised.”

Why this changes the encounter, not just the antibiotic choice

Atypical presentation is the norm here, not the exception. A neutropenic patient has no neutrophils to form pus — an intra-abdominal or soft tissue infection that would be obviously fluctuant and erythematous in an immunocompetent patient can look almost unremarkable on exam in a neutropenic one. A patient on chronic corticosteroids can have a blunted or absent febrile response and minimal peritoneal signs despite a perforated viscus. The absence of textbook findings is not reassuring in this population — it's expected.

02 · Primary Immunodeficiency

Congenital Immunodeficiencies

Rare individually, but each maps to a distinctive infection pattern that's often the clue that gets the diagnosis made.

Primary immunodeficiencies are organized most usefully by which arm of host defense is missing — that arm predicts both the infection pattern and the typical age of presentation. Most present in infancy/early childhood, but several (CVID in particular) are frequently not diagnosed until adulthood.

B-cell defect
X-linked Agammaglobulinemia (Bruton's)

BTK gene mutation halts B-cell maturation — essentially absent B cells and immunoglobulins. Recurrent sinopulmonary and GI infections beginning after maternal antibody wanes, around 6–12 months of age.

Encapsulated bacteria, enteroviruses · X-linked (boys)
B-cell defect
Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID)

Impaired B-cell differentiation into plasma cells despite normal B-cell numbers — low IgG, IgA, and/or IgM. The most common symptomatic primary immunodeficiency; frequently diagnosed in adulthood after years of recurrent infections.

Recurrent sinopulmonary infection, bronchiectasis, autoimmune cytopenias
B-cell defect
Selective IgA Deficiency

Isolated low/absent IgA with normal IgG/IgM. Most common primary immunodeficiency overall; many patients are asymptomatic.

Mucosal (sinopulmonary, GI) infections · anaphylaxis risk with blood products containing IgA
T-cell defect
DiGeorge Syndrome

22q11.2 deletion causes thymic hypoplasia/aplasia — variable T-cell deficiency. Part of a broader syndrome (cardiac defects, hypocalcemia, characteristic facies).

Viral, fungal, opportunistic infections · presents in infancy
Combined B+T defect
Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID)

Multiple genetic causes converge on absent/non-functional T cells (and often B/NK cells) — the “bubble boy” disease. A true pediatric emergency: presents in the first months of life with failure to thrive and overwhelming, often fatal, infection if unrecognized.

Opportunistic infections of every category · live vaccines are fatal
Phagocyte defect
Chronic Granulomatous Disease (CGD)

NADPH oxidase defect — neutrophils can engulf but can't generate the respiratory burst needed to kill catalase-positive organisms. Recurrent abscesses and granuloma formation.

Staph aureus, Serratia, Burkholderia, Nocardia, Aspergillus
Phagocyte defect
Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency

Defective neutrophil adhesion/migration to sites of infection. Classic clue: delayed umbilical cord separation and infections without pus formation despite marked leukocytosis.

Recurrent bacterial infection, poor wound healing
Complement defect
Terminal Complement Deficiency (C5–C9)

Inability to form the membrane attack complex. Covered in depth in Volume I's complement cascade section — included here for completeness of the pathogen-mapping picture.

Recurrent Neisseria (meningococcemia, disseminated gonococcal infection)
Clinical clue worth remembering: an adult presenting with a first episode of recurrent sinopulmonary infections, or a first case of invasive pneumococcal disease without an obvious reason, is worth a basic humoral workup (quantitative immunoglobulins) — CVID is under-recognized precisely because it isn't a pediatric diagnosis.
03 · Secondary Immunodeficiency

Acquired & Iatrogenic Immunodeficiency

Far more common in the ED than any primary immunodeficiency — and the specific cause determines the specific risk.

HIV/AIDS — CD4-stratified opportunistic infection risk

CD4 count is one of the few places in medicine where a single lab value maps this cleanly onto a specific differential diagnosis. Always pair it with the clinical picture and, when known, whether the patient is on antiretroviral therapy — a suppressed viral load with an low historical CD4 nadir carries different risk than an active, untreated low CD4 count.

Asplenia & functional asplenia

The spleen is the primary filter for encapsulated organisms and intraerythrocytic parasites; surgical splenectomy and functional asplenia (sickle cell disease, from repeated splenic auto-infarction) carry essentially the same risk profile. The feared complication is overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) — fulminant sepsis and DIC that can progress from feeling well to critically ill within hours, with reported mortality as high as 50–70% once fulminant. Any fever in an asplenic patient warrants immediate empiric antibiotics (typically a third-generation cephalosporin) while the workup proceeds, not a “wait and see.”

Chemotherapy-induced neutropenia & febrile neutropenia

Figure 2. Typical ANC nadir timing after cytotoxic chemotherapy — infection risk peaks roughly 7–14 days after administration.

Febrile neutropenia is defined as a single temperature ≥38.3°C (101°F), or ≥38.0°C (100.4°F) sustained over an hour, in a patient with an ANC <500/μL (or <1000 with an expected decline below 500 within 48 hours). This is treated with the same urgency as sepsis — IDSA-anchored practice calls for empiric antipseudomonal beta-lactam monotherapy (cefepime, meropenem/imipenem, or piperacillin-tazobactam) ideally within an hour of triage; vancomycin is not routine and is reserved for specific indications (suspected line infection, skin/soft-tissue infection, pneumonia, hemodynamic instability, known MRSA colonization). The MASCC risk index helps identify low-risk patients (score ≥21) who may be candidates for outpatient oral therapy after initial evaluation.

Practice trend worth knowingMore recent stewardship-focused data (2023–2025 cohorts) support discontinuing empiric antibiotics after roughly 72 hours of defervescence and clinical recovery, regardless of whether the ANC has recovered — a shift from older practice of continuing antibiotics until neutrophil recovery, aimed at reducing C. difficile and other antibiotic-associated harm without increasing bacteremia relapse.
Verify before use: specific empiric regimen choice, MASCC score application, and discontinuation timing should follow your institution's current oncology/antimicrobial stewardship protocol.

Chronic corticosteroid use

Beyond the general threshold discussed above, corticosteroids deserve specific mention because their effect is broad (impairing neutrophil migration, cell-mediated immunity, and wound healing simultaneously) and because they actively mask the usual signs of infection — blunted fever, blunted leukocytosis, blunted peritoneal signs — making the patient look reassuring right up until they aren't. PJP prophylaxis is a relevant consideration at sustained higher doses, particularly when combined with other immunosuppressants.

Biologics & targeted immunosuppressants

TNF inhibitors carry a specific, well-documented risk of reactivating latent tuberculosis and hepatitis B — screening before initiation is standard, but the ED-relevant point is that a patient on a TNF inhibitor with a new pulmonary or hepatic presentation deserves that reactivation risk on the differential. JAK inhibitors carry an elevated herpes zoster risk. Rituximab (anti-CD20, B-cell depleting) carries hepatitis B reactivation and, rarely, progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) risk. Full agent-by-agent mechanism, dosing, and adverse effect detail is covered in the upcoming dedicated Pharmacology Atlas — this entry exists here only to keep the infection-risk framework complete.

“Soft” cumulative immunocompromise

Revisit the Volume I foundational point here: advanced age, poorly controlled diabetes, malnutrition, uremia, cirrhosis, and alcohol use disorder don't earn a line on the problem list the way a named immunodeficiency does, but they stack, and a patient with several of these together can carry infection risk comparable to a defined immunodeficiency — worth actively considering rather than waiting for a diagnosis label to prompt it.

04 · Practical Synthesis

The ED Approach to the Febrile Immunocompromised Patient

The same fever means something different depending on what's missing — the workup and disposition should reflect that.

Practical synthesis

Identify which defect (or combination) is present — not just that the patient is “immunocompromised” — since that determines which organisms belong in the empiric coverage. Set a lower threshold for treating before confirmatory workup returns, given how often exam and inflammatory markers are blunted. In specific high-acuity syndromes — febrile neutropenia, suspected OPSI in an asplenic patient — treat the door-to-antibiotic timeline with the same urgency as sepsis. Keep the threshold for imaging lower than usual: a reassuring exam is far less reassuring in this population. Document the specific immunocompromising condition and its severity explicitly in the chart — “immunocompromised” alone doesn't communicate which risk profile you're managing.

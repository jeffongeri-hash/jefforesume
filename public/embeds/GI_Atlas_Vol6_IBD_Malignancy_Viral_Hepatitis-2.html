IBD Clinical Management

GI Atlas Vol. 4 covered the drugs — this section covers the disease itself: how severity is actually scored, the extraintestinal disease that can be the presenting feature, and when surveillance colonoscopy starts.

Severity Scoring

ScoreDiseaseKey components
Truelove-Witts criteriaUlcerative colitisStool frequency, blood in stool, fever, tachycardia, anemia, ESR — classifies mild/moderate/severe; "severe" (≥6 bloody stools/day plus systemic toxicity) approaches the toxic megacolon risk threshold covered in the GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas
Mayo scoreUlcerative colitisStool frequency, rectal bleeding, endoscopic findings, physician global assessment — widely used in clinical trials and increasingly in practice to standardize response/remission definitions
Harvey-Bradshaw IndexCrohn's diseaseGeneral well-being, abdominal pain, stool frequency, abdominal mass, complications — a simplified bedside alternative to the more research-oriented Crohn's Disease Activity Index

Colorectal Cancer Surveillance in IBDA Different Schedule From Average-Risk Screening

Surveillance starts earlier and runs more frequently than average-risk screening
Chronic colitis (ulcerative colitis, or Crohn's colitis involving a substantial portion of the colon) raises colorectal cancer risk independent of the average-risk factors covered in this volume's Colorectal Cancer Screening section — surveillance colonoscopy typically begins 8–10 years after diagnosis of colitis (not from age 45 the way average-risk screening does), with repeat surveillance every 1–3 years depending on risk factors (extent of colitis, concurrent PSC, family history, prior dysplasia). Concurrent PSC specifically shortens this interval further given the compounded risk.

Flare Management Principles

Always exclude infection first — C. difficile testing in particular, given IBD patients' elevated baseline risk and the identical clinical presentation (GI Atlas Vol. 4/5b) Fecal calprotectin helps distinguish a true inflammatory flare from a functional (IBS-like) symptom overlay in a patient with known IBD — a genuinely common diagnostic dilemma in practice Severe flares require inpatient management with the toxic megacolon vigilance covered in the GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas Corticosteroids remain the mainstay of acute flare induction even though they're not appropriate for maintenance therapy (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a's dosing/tapering principles apply directly)

Colorectal Cancer Screening

The age threshold dropped from 50 to 45 in 2021 — worth confirming this is reflected in any older personal framework, since it changes who's actually "average risk and under-screened."

Current USPSTF Guidance

Average-risk screening now starts at 45, not 50
The USPSTF updated its recommendation in 2021, extending screening to ages 45–75 (previously 50–75) given rising CRC incidence in younger adults — roughly 13% of new colorectal cancer cases now occur in people under 50. This is a genuinely important update if working from older training, and it doesn't change the equally important point that concerning symptoms (unexplained bleeding, anemia, change in bowel habit) in any age, including under 45, warrant prompt evaluation rather than being dismissed as too young for CRC.

Screening Modalities & Intervals

TestIntervalNote
ColonoscopyEvery 10 yearsOnly modality that is both diagnostic and therapeutic (polypectomy) in the same procedure — the reference standard others are validated against
FIT (fecal immunochemical test)AnnualDetects human hemoglobin specifically — more sensitive and specific than older guaiac-based testing, with less dietary interference
FIT-DNA (stool DNA test)Every 1–3 yearsCombines FIT with DNA markers for colorectal neoplasia-associated mutations — higher sensitivity than FIT alone but a higher false-positive rate
CT colonographyEvery 5 yearsNon-invasive direct visualization; a positive finding still requires follow-up colonoscopy
Flexible sigmoidoscopyEvery 5 years (or every 10 years combined with annual FIT)Visualizes only the distal colon/rectum — misses proximal lesions, a genuine limitation relative to full colonoscopy

Any positive non-colonoscopy screening result requires follow-up colonoscopy to complete the screening episode — a FIT-positive patient who never gets the follow-up colonoscopy is not meaningfully screened, a distinction that matters for both clinical practice and quality metrics.

Elevated-Risk ScreeningStarts Earlier, Runs More Often

Risk factorRecommendation
First-degree relative with CRC or advanced polyp before age 60, or ≥2 first-degree relatives at any ageColonoscopy starting at age 40, or 10 years before the youngest affected relative's diagnosis (whichever is earlier) — repeated every 5 years
Lynch syndrome (hereditary non-polyposis colorectal cancer)Colonoscopy starting in the early 20s, repeated every 1–2 years given markedly accelerated adenoma-to-carcinoma progression
Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)Annual flexible sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy starting in the early teens — untreated FAP carries near-100% lifetime CRC risk, and prophylactic colectomy is often planned once polyp burden becomes unmanageable
IBD/chronic colitisA distinct surveillance schedule (8–10 years after diagnosis, then every 1–3 years) — covered in this volume's IBD Clinical Management section rather than repeated here, since it isn't age-triggered the way average-risk and most hereditary-syndrome screening is

Why the Modality Choice Matters Less Than Actually Completing Screening

Current guidance deliberately avoids designating one single "best" test — the strongest predictor of population-level benefit is completion of whichever appropriately-sensitive test a patient will actually do, not universal adoption of the single most sensitive option. This is why FIT mailer outreach programs and shared decision-making conversations about modality preference are now considered legitimate, evidence-supported parts of a screening strategy rather than a compromise on rigor.

GI Tumor Markers & Cancer Staging Basics

The staging framework behind the cancer surgeries already covered across this atlas series — Whipple, esophagectomy, gastrectomy, colectomy — assembled here as the diagnostic/oncologic layer underneath them.

Tumor MarkersMonitoring Tools, Not Screening Tests

MarkerAssociated cancerPrimary use
CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen)Colorectal cancerNot sensitive/specific enough for screening (this volume's Colorectal Cancer Screening section covers the actual screening tools) — its real value is as a baseline and surveillance marker after diagnosis/resection, where a rising trend can signal recurrence before it's otherwise clinically apparent
CA 19-9Pancreatic cancer (also biliary tract cancers)Similarly not a screening test — used for prognosis and monitoring treatment response/recurrence after diagnosis (relevant directly to the Whipple procedure population, GI Atlas Vol. 5). Falsely elevated in any cause of biliary obstruction (GI Atlas Vol. 5/6), so an elevated level in a jaundiced patient doesn't necessarily mean malignancy until obstruction is relieved and the level is rechecked. Also undetectable in the ~5–10% of the population who are Lewis antigen-negative and structurally unable to produce it — a genuine false-negative trap in exactly the patients it would otherwise be most useful for
AFP (alpha-fetoprotein)Hepatocellular carcinomaUsed alongside imaging for HCC surveillance in cirrhotic patients (GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas's Cirrhosis section) — sensitivity is imperfect enough that imaging surveillance (ultrasound ± AFP, per society guidance) remains necessary rather than relying on the marker alone
CA 125Not GI-specific (ovarian cancer), but can be elevated in any peritoneal carcinomatosisOccasionally relevant in a GI oncology workup when peritoneal spread is suspected, despite its primary association lying outside the GI tract
The universal caveat for every marker on this list
None of these markers are validated or recommended as standalone screening tests in an asymptomatic average-risk population — each is used for prognosis, treatment response monitoring, or recurrence surveillance after a diagnosis is already established by tissue/imaging. Ordering one to "screen" for cancer in an asymptomatic patient is a common and avoidable misapplication.

Staging Basics — TNM Framework

Every GI cancer in this atlas series is staged using the same underlying TNM logic (Tumor depth/extent, Node involvement, Metastasis) — the specific depth/nodal thresholds differ by organ, but the framework is shared.

CancerT-stage logicRelevant surgery (this atlas series)
EsophagealDepth of invasion through mucosa/submucosa/muscularis propria/adventitia — depth correlates strongly with nodal metastasis risk, part of why even small, superficial lesions get careful depth assessment (endoscopic ultrasound) before deciding between endoscopic and surgical therapyEsophagectomy (GI Atlas Vol. 5)
GastricSimilar depth-based logic through the gastric wall layersGastrectomy (GI Atlas Vol. 5)
PancreaticPrimarily driven by size and, critically, vascular involvement (superior mesenteric artery/vein, portal vein) — this is what determines resectability more than size alone, directly tied to the preoperative imaging assessment in GI Atlas Vol. 5's Whipple sectionWhipple procedure (GI Atlas Vol. 5)
ColorectalDepth through the bowel wall layers (mucosa → submucosa → muscularis propria → serosa/pericolic tissue) — T-stage here directly predicts nodal involvement risk, part of why a T1 lesion confined to the submucosa may be managed endoscopically while deeper invasion triggers formal oncologic resectionColectomy (GI Atlas Vol. 5)

Why Staging Determines the Surgery, Not the Reverse

Every resection covered in this atlas series' surgical volumes is planned around a staging assessment completed beforehand — the Whipple procedure's preoperative vascular imaging (GI Atlas Vol. 5) is a direct staging exercise, determining resectability before the operating room is ever booked. Neoadjuvant chemoradiation for esophageal/gastric/pancreatic cancer, when used, is itself a stage-dependent decision — locally advanced disease often gets treated before surgery specifically to downstage the tumor and improve resection margins, while early-stage disease may proceed directly to surgery. Staging, in this sense, is the diagnostic layer that determines which of this atlas series' surgical chapters actually applies to a given patient.

Viral Hepatitis

Five distinct viruses, one shared clinical picture — the serology panel is what actually tells them apart, and Hepatitis B's panel is the one genuinely worth memorizing cold.

The Five Viruses, Quickly

VirusTransmissionChronicity
Hepatitis AFecal-oral (contaminated food/water)Never chronic — self-limited, though can rarely cause fulminant hepatitis
Hepatitis BBlood, sexual contact, perinatalChronic in a minority of adult infections (much higher rate if acquired perinatally/in early childhood, given immature immune clearance at that age)
Hepatitis CBlood (injection drug use predominant route currently), less commonly sexualChronic in the majority of infections (~75–85%) — the reverse pattern from Hepatitis A
Hepatitis DBlood — but only occurs with concurrent Hepatitis B, since it's a defective virus requiring HBsAg for its own viral envelopeCoinfection (simultaneous with acute HBV) tends toward spontaneous clearance alongside HBV; superinfection (HDV acquired on top of established chronic HBV) tends toward more severe, more often chronic disease
Hepatitis EFecal-oral (contaminated water, undercooked pork/game in some regions)Typically self-limited like Hepatitis A, but carries a distinctly elevated fulminant hepatitis/mortality risk specifically in pregnant women — a genuinely important exception to the "enteric hepatitis viruses are generally mild" pattern

Hepatitis B SerologyThe Panel Worth Knowing Cold

PatternHBsAgAnti-HBsAnti-HBc IgMAnti-HBc IgGHBeAgInterpretation
Acute infection+−+−/+Often +Active acute infection — IgM anti-HBc is the marker specifically distinguishing acute from chronic
Window period−−+−/+−HBsAg has cleared but anti-HBs hasn't yet risen — IgM anti-HBc is the only positive marker and is diagnostic during this gap; missing this pattern is how an acute infection gets mislabeled as "no evidence of hepatitis B"
Chronic infection+−−+VariableHBsAg persisting >6 months defines chronicity; HBeAg positivity within this pattern indicates high viral replication/infectivity
Resolved infection (natural immunity)−+−+−Past infection, now cleared — total anti-HBc (IgG) is the marker distinguishing this from vaccination, since vaccination never generates a core antibody response
Vaccination−+−−−Anti-HBs alone, with a completely negative anti-HBc — the vaccine contains only surface antigen, never core antigen, which is exactly why this pattern is the one clean way to confirm vaccine-induced rather than infection-induced immunity
The single highest-yield distinction on this whole panel
Anti-HBc (core antibody) is never produced by the vaccine — only real infection generates it. This is the one fact that resolves almost every confusing HBV serology pattern: if anti-HBc is present in any form, the patient has been infected at some point, regardless of what the surface antigen/antibody results show.

Hepatitis A & C Serology, Briefly

VirusAcute markerPast exposure/immunity marker
Hepatitis AAnti-HAV IgMAnti-HAV IgG (from past infection or vaccination — unlike HBV, there's no separate core-antibody-equivalent distinction needed since HAV never becomes chronic)
Hepatitis CAnti-HCV screens for exposure; HCV RNA confirms active/current infectionAnti-HCV alone, with a negative HCV RNA, indicates past infection that has been spontaneously or treatment-cleared — a genuinely important distinction now that direct-acting antivirals cure the large majority of treated HCV, making "anti-HCV positive" no longer synonymous with "currently infected" the way it once reliably was

Clinical Pattern — Acute Viral Hepatitis, Any Cause

Regardless of which virus, acute hepatitis shares a common biochemical signature: markedly elevated transaminases (often >1000 in acute viral hepatitis, typically ALT ≥ AST — the reverse of the AST-predominant pattern seen in alcoholic hepatitis), with bilirubin and INR tracking disease severity. A rising INR with worsening mental status in this setting raises concern for acute liver failure, a distinct emergency from the chronic decompensation processes covered in the GI Emergencies & Hepatology Atlas's cirrhosis sections, given the previously healthy liver's different failure trajectory and transplant-listing urgency.

Pediatric GI Emergencies

Three age-specific presentations where the vomiting pattern (bilious vs. non-bilious, timing, and the specific exam/imaging finding) does almost all of the diagnostic work.

A note on scope
These three entities may already be covered in more depth in a dedicated pediatric emergency medicine atlas — they're included here briefly for completeness within the GI series, cross-referenced to that resource for the fuller pediatric-specific workup and management detail where one exists.

Pyloric Stenosis

FeatureDetail
Typical age2–8 weeks — presentation outside this window should prompt reconsidering the diagnosis
PresentationNon-bilious, projectile vomiting, classically immediately after feeding, with a hungry infant eager to re-feed afterward — the non-bilious character reflects the obstruction being proximal to the ampulla of Vater (GI Atlas Vol. 5's Whipple section covers this same anatomic landmark from the adult surgical side)
Classic exam findingA palpable "olive"-shaped mass in the right upper quadrant/epigastrium, representing the hypertrophied pylorus — best felt after the stomach has been emptied by vomiting or a test feed
Metabolic patternHypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis from persistent vomiting of gastric (acidic, chloride-rich) contents — the same proximal-obstruction electrolyte pattern covered for adult bowel obstruction in GI Atlas Vol. 5b, here in its classic pediatric form
DiagnosisAbdominal ultrasound — measuring pyloric muscle thickness and canal length against established cutoffs
TreatmentFluid/electrolyte correction first (correcting the alkalosis before anesthesia reduces perioperative apnea risk), then pyloromyotomy — surgically splitting the hypertrophied muscle without entering the mucosa, conceptually similar in spirit to the myotomy principle used for achalasia in GI Atlas Vol. 1/3, though a distinct muscle and mechanism

Intussusception

FeatureDetail
Typical age3 months to 3 years, peak incidence 6–18 months
MechanismA proximal bowel segment telescopes into a distal segment — most commonly ileocolic — often triggered by a lymphoid tissue lead point (e.g., post-viral Peyer's patch hypertrophy) in this age group; an identifiable pathologic lead point (Meckel's diverticulum, polyp) becomes proportionally more likely outside the classic age range
PresentationIntermittent, severe colicky abdominal pain with the classic (but not universal) "currant jelly" stool — blood and mucus reflecting mucosal ischemia at the telescoped segment; a palpable sausage-shaped abdominal mass is sometimes appreciated on exam
DiagnosisUltrasound — the "target" or "doughnut" sign on cross-section is the classic finding
TreatmentAir or contrast enema is both diagnostic and therapeutic in the large majority of cases — reducing the intussusception non-operatively; surgery is reserved for enema reduction failure, perforation, or peritonitis

Malrotation with Midgut Volvulus

FeatureDetail
Typical ageMost present within the first month of life, though later presentation is possible
MechanismIncomplete embryologic intestinal rotation leaves the small bowel mesentery on a narrow pedicle rather than its normal broad attachment — predisposing to the entire midgut twisting around the superior mesenteric artery, a true vascular emergency threatening the whole small bowel's blood supply at once
PresentationBilious vomiting in a neonate is malrotation/volvulus until proven otherwise — the single most important teaching point in this section, given how rapidly this can progress to catastrophic bowel loss
DiagnosisUpper GI contrast study is the gold standard, looking for an abnormally positioned duodenojejunal junction (normally should cross the midline at the ligament of Treitz); a "whirlpool sign" on ultrasound/Doppler (the mesenteric vessels twisting around each other) is a supportive finding where available. The classic neonatal "double bubble" sign (stomach + proximal duodenum distension, no distal gas) more specifically indicates duodenal atresia/obstruction rather than volvulus itself, though both can coexist and both are frequently discussed together
TreatmentEmergent surgery (Ladd's procedure) — untwisting the volvulus, dividing the abnormal peritoneal bands (Ladd's bands) causing duodenal compression, and widening the mesenteric base — true minutes-matter urgency, on par with the acute mesenteric ischemia emergency covered in GI Atlas Vol. 5b, since delay directly costs bowel length in an already anatomically vulnerable, narrow-pedicled mesentery

The One-Line Summary of All Three

Non-bilious projectile vomiting, 2–8 weeks, hungry after → pyloric stenosis Colicky pain + currant jelly stool, 6–18 months → intussusception Bilious vomiting in a neonate → malrotation/volvulus until proven otherwise, emergent workup

Guideline Changes Atlas Dermatology · Vol. 15 — Epidermal Necrolysis
What Changed · 2024

SJS and TEN now formally treated as one disease

The first S3-level (highest German evidence grade) guideline for these conditions reframes Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis as points on a single severity spectrum — "epidermal necrolysis" — and brings a concrete wound-care correction that runs against burn-unit instinct: leave the detached skin in place.

02 · Deep Dive

One disease, a severity spectrum

Epidermal necrolysis (EN) Unified terminology

SJS and TEN are now formally described as variants of a single disease entity — epidermal necrolysis — distinguished by degree of body surface area involvement rather than treated as categorically separate diagnoses. This isn't just semantic: it reflects a shared pathophysiology (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-mediated keratinocyte death, commonly drug-induced) and supports a unified diagnostic and management framework across the severity range rather than SJS-specific vs. TEN-specific protocols.

03 · Deep Dive · Myth-Busting

Don't debride like it's a burn

Detached epidermis management Practice correction

Prophylactic debridement of devitalized epidermis has not been shown to improve outcomes compared to leaving detached skin in place. Unlike thermal burns, epidermal necrolysis can leave cells that are detached but not entirely necrosed or damaged — so there may be real benefit to leaving that layer in situ (after blister fluid is evacuated) rather than stripping it as burn-unit habit might suggest.

⚡ Why this matters at first contact

EN patients are frequently transferred through EDs before reaching a burn or dermatology unit. If early wound management follows a burn-debridement reflex by default, this guideline's evidence argues against it — evacuate blister fluid, but don't strip viable-but-detached epidermis prophylactically.

04 · Evidence Watch

Systemic immunomodulating therapy: genuinely unsettled

⚡ Flagging real uncertainty, not a settled recommendation

The guideline reviews etanercept, cyclosporine, IVIG, and corticosteroids without a clean consensus favorite. A 96-patient RCT found etanercept reduced SCORTEN-predicted mortality to a statistically significant degree while corticosteroids in the same trial did not — though the corticosteroid arm was treated for a disproportionately long duration, which may have delayed wound healing and confounded the comparison. Separately, multiple systematic reviews and network meta-analyses of the broader literature have reached different conclusions about which systemic therapy (if any) meaningfully improves mortality beyond supportive care alone — some favor cyclosporine, some favor etanercept, some find no treatment clearly superior to supportive care. This is genuinely unsettled evidence; treat any specific systemic agent as an individualized, specialist-guided decision rather than a guideline-mandated default.

05 · Deep Dive

SCORTEN timing, reaffirmed

The SCORTEN severity-of-illness score remains the guideline-endorsed tool for mortality risk assessment in epidermal necrolysis. It should be calculated within the first 24 hours after admission and again on day 3 — the second calculation matters because early trajectory changes the risk estimate meaningfully as the clinical picture evolves.

06 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Think of SJS and TEN as one spectrum disease (epidermal necrolysis) rather than two separate diagnoses — severity is the axis, not category.
  • Evacuate blister fluid, but don't prophylactically debride detached epidermis — the evidence doesn't support the burn-unit reflex here.
  • Calculate SCORTEN at admission (within 24h) and again on day 3 — don't rely on a single early score.
  • Don't assume there's a single guideline-mandated systemic drug of choice — etanercept, cyclosporine, and IVIG+corticosteroids all have some supportive evidence and none has a clean consensus win. This is a specialist discussion, not an ED protocol decision.
  • Identify and stop the culprit drug as early as possible regardless of which systemic therapy is chosen — this remains the one universally agreed intervention.

Perforated Peptic Ulcer Repair

A true surgical emergency — and the direct surgical consequence of the mucus-bicarbonate barrier failure introduced back in GI Atlas Vol. 2.

Indications

Free perforation of a gastric or duodenal ulcer with pneumoperitoneum and peritonitis — an absolute surgical emergency given ongoing gastric/duodenal content spillage into the peritoneal cavity. Duodenal perforations are more common than gastric; both are downstream of the same NSAID/H. pylori-driven barrier failure covered in GI Atlas Vol. 2 and Vol. 4.

Risk Factors

NSAID use (GI Atlas Vol. 2's prostaglandin barrier mechanism) H. pylori infection (GI Atlas Vol. 1/4) Critical illness/severe physiologic stress (stress ulceration) Smoking, corticosteroid use (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a) Advanced age — associated with delayed presentation and higher mortality

Preoperative Preparation

This is a resuscitate-and-go scenario — aggressive IV fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics started immediately (not simply "prophylactic," given established peritoneal contamination), NG decompression, and rapid transition to the OR. Upright chest X-ray or CT showing free air under the diaphragm supports the diagnosis, but imaging should never substantially delay operative intervention in a patient with peritonitis and a compelling clinical picture.

Antibiotics

Broad-spectrum coverage for mixed gastrointestinal flora (typically a combination covering gram-negative and anaerobic organisms — e.g., a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor, or ceftriaxone plus metronidazole) started empirically at diagnosis, not merely dosed once at incision the way clean-case prophylaxis works — treatment continues postoperatively for a duration guided by the degree of contamination found intraoperatively.

ProcedureThe Graham Patch

Rather than attempting to close the friable, inflamed ulcer edges directly (which tend to tear sutures), a tongue of omentum is mobilized and sutured directly over the perforation — the Graham (omental) patch — sealing the defect using the omentum's own blood supply to promote healing, rather than relying on the ulcer-affected tissue itself to hold a repair. Peritoneal lavage to clear contamination is performed concurrently. Underlying ulcer disease (H. pylori, NSAID use) still requires separate medical treatment postoperatively — the patch seals the acute perforation but doesn't address the ulcer's cause.

Approach

Laparoscopic repair is increasingly standard in hemodynamically stable patients at centers with appropriate expertise, showing comparable outcomes to open with the usual advantages (less pain, faster recovery). Open repair (midline laparotomy) remains preferred for hemodynamically unstable patients, large/difficult-to-localize perforations, or when laparoscopic expertise/equipment isn't readily available — this is a time-critical emergency where the fastest safe approach wins over the least invasive one.

Optimizing Outcomes

Time from perforation to operation is the single dominant outcome predictor — mortality rises substantially with delay beyond 24 hours from symptom onset, mirroring the same time-sensitivity theme from this atlas's Appendectomy section but with even higher stakes given the more immediately life-threatening peritoneal contamination. The Boey score (preoperative shock, major medical comorbidity, and delay >24 hours) helps risk-stratify expected mortality and complication rates.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Prolonged inpatient stay proportional to degree of peritoneal contamination found intraoperatively Definitive H. pylori testing and eradication (GI Atlas Vol. 4) once stable — untreated infection risks ulcer/perforation recurrence Permanent avoidance or careful risk-benefit reconsideration of NSAID use Repeat endoscopy generally recommended for gastric (not duodenal) ulcers to exclude malignancy once healed

Postoperative Lab Changes

Marked leukocytosis and lactate elevation are expected initially given established peritonitis — trending both is more useful than a single value, since a genuinely reassuring postoperative course should show both improving within 24–48 hours. Persistently rising inflammatory markers or lactate should prompt evaluation for an intra-abdominal abscess, patch leak, or ongoing undrained contamination.

Bowel Obstruction Surgery & Exploratory Laparotomy

Not every obstruction needs surgery — but recognizing which ones do, and how quickly, is the central clinical skill this section builds toward.

IndicationsSigns That Surgery Can't Wait

Signs of strangulation/ischemia — fever, tachycardia, peritonitis, lactic acidosis, or CT findings of bowel wall pneumatosis/lack of enhancement Closed-loop obstruction (a segment obstructed at two points, unable to decompress in either direction — high strangulation risk even without current ischemic signs) Failure of nonoperative trial (NG decompression, bowel rest) after an appropriate observation period — typically 24–48 hours for adhesive small bowel obstruction without concerning features Complete (rather than partial) obstruction, particularly in a virgin (no prior surgery) abdomen, which raises concern for a mass lesion rather than simple adhesions
Most adhesive small bowel obstructions resolve without surgery
The majority of adhesive small bowel obstructions — the most common cause of small bowel obstruction overall, from prior abdominal surgery — resolve with NG decompression, bowel rest, and IV fluids alone. Surgery is reserved for the specific red flags above rather than being the default response to every obstruction, which is exactly why serial abdominal exams and a defined trial period matter clinically.

Risk Factors

Prior abdominal/pelvic surgery (adhesions — the leading cause overall) Hernia (incarcerated, GI Atlas Vol. 5's Hernia Repair section) Malignancy (intraluminal, extrinsic compression, or peritoneal carcinomatosis) Inflammatory strictures (Crohn's disease, GI Atlas Vol. 4) Volvulus (sigmoid most common in older/institutionalized patients; cecal volvulus less common but similar mechanism)

Preoperative Preparation

NG decompression (reducing aspiration risk at induction and improving surgical working space), aggressive fluid resuscitation (bowel obstruction causes substantial third-spacing and vomiting-related losses), and correction of the electrolyte derangements that follow (see this section's lab discussion below). Foley catheter for close urine output monitoring given the fluid shifts involved. CT imaging localizes the transition point and screens for the ischemic/closed-loop features above before the patient goes to the OR.

Antibiotics

Broad-spectrum coverage (similar to the perforated viscus regimen in this volume's previous section) is used whenever bowel ischemia, perforation, or strangulation is suspected or confirmed intraoperatively — for a straightforward adhesiolysis without any bowel compromise found, standard cefazolin-based prophylaxis (this atlas's Cross-Cutting Principles section, GI Atlas Vol. 5) is generally sufficient, with the operative findings themselves determining which regimen actually applies once the abdomen is open.

Procedure

Exploratory laparotomy (or diagnostic/therapeutic laparoscopy in appropriate candidates) identifies the transition point and the underlying cause — adhesiolysis (lysing the causative adhesive band) for adhesive obstruction, hernia reduction and repair for an incarcerated hernia, or bowel resection if a segment is found non-viable (dusky, non-peristalsing, without Doppler or visible pulsatile flow after a period of observation and warming). Sigmoid volvulus is often initially managed with endoscopic detorsion, reserving surgery for recurrent cases or failed/unsafe endoscopic reduction.

Approach

Open laparotomy remains extremely common for bowel obstruction surgery specifically, given dense adhesions from prior surgery often make laparoscopic visualization and safe adhesiolysis difficult, and given the higher acuity/instability of many of these presentations. Laparoscopic adhesiolysis is reasonable in selected stable patients with a single, identifiable adhesive band and no prior extensive abdominal surgery — a narrower selection window than for most other procedures in this atlas.

Optimizing Outcomes

Timely recognition of strangulation risk — rather than an extended trial of nonoperative management in a patient with concerning features — is the dominant modifiable factor, mirroring the time-sensitivity theme running through this entire volume. Adequate preoperative fluid/electrolyte correction reduces perioperative cardiovascular and renal complications in a population that's often significantly volume-depleted before ever reaching the OR.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Postoperative ileus (GI Atlas Vol. 3) is essentially universal and often more pronounced than after elective bowel surgery, given the bowel's preexisting distension and inflammation Recurrent adhesive obstruction risk persists lifelong after any abdominal surgery — patient education on this possibility is worthwhile at discharge Diet advanced gradually as return of bowel function is confirmed (flatus, then bowel movement)

Splenectomy

One of the few procedures in this atlas where the most important postoperative consideration (lifelong infection risk) has nothing to do with the GI tract itself.

Indications

Traumatic splenic injury (hemodynamically unstable, or high-grade injury failing nonoperative management) Hematologic disease — ITP refractory to medical therapy, hereditary spherocytosis, some hemolytic anemias Splenic tumors (primary or, rarely, metastatic) Incidental splenic injury during other abdominal surgery Splenic abscess or symptomatic splenic cyst (less common)

Trauma: Nonoperative Management First, Where Possible

The modern default has shifted away from automatic splenectomy
Given the lifelong infection risk splenectomy carries (below), nonoperative management — observation, serial hemoglobin checks, and angioembolization for active extravasation — is now preferred for hemodynamically stable trauma patients, even with higher-grade splenic injuries, reserving splenectomy for hemodynamic instability or nonoperative management failure. This represents a genuine, relatively modern shift in practice from older, more automatic "spleen trauma means splenectomy" teaching.

Preoperative Preparation

Vaccination is the single most important preoperative consideration whenever splenectomy is elective/planned (hematologic indications, elective oncologic cases) — pneumococcal, meningococcal, and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccines should be given at least 2 weeks before a planned splenectomy for the best immune response; when surgery is emergent (trauma), vaccination is instead given postoperatively, typically 2 weeks after surgery once the acute stress response has settled, since immune response to vaccination is blunted in the immediate perioperative/critical illness window.

Antibiotics

Standard cefazolin prophylaxis for the operation itself — distinct from and in addition to the vaccination strategy above, which addresses long-term infection risk rather than perioperative surgical site infection.

Approach & Procedure

Laparoscopic splenectomy is standard for elective indications (hematologic disease, elective oncologic cases) — the splenic artery and vein are individually ligated at the hilum, and the spleen is removed in a specimen bag (often after morcellation for larger organs, to allow extraction through a small port site). Open splenectomy remains preferred for trauma (rapid access and control of a potentially massively hemorrhaging organ takes priority over minimally invasive technique in this setting), markedly enlarged spleens (technically difficult to manipulate laparoscopically), or when concurrent injuries require open exploration anyway.

Optimizing Outcomes

Preoperative vaccination timing (above) is the dominant modifiable long-term outcome factor. For trauma specifically, maximizing the trial of nonoperative management in appropriate candidates avoids splenectomy's lifelong infection risk entirely — the best outcome in trauma is often not needing this operation at all.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection (OPSI) — a lifelong risk
Loss of the spleen's filtering function and its role in antibody production against encapsulated organisms (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae) creates a lifelong, low-probability but high-mortality risk of fulminant sepsis from these specific pathogens — OPSI carries a mortality rate reported as high as 50% even with treatment. Patients need lifelong education to seek immediate medical attention for any fever, and some clinicians provide standby antibiotics for patients to self-start while seeking care in remote/delayed-access settings. Booster vaccination schedules (pneumococcal particularly) continue for life, not just once postoperatively.
Medical alert bracelet/card recommended given OPSI's rapid, catastrophic potential course Malaria prophylaxis is especially critical for asplenic patients traveling to endemic areas, given impaired clearance of intraerythrocytic parasites

Hepatectomy / Liver Resection

The liver's remarkable regenerative capacity is what makes major resection possible at all — and what makes preoperative future liver remnant calculation the central planning step.

Indications

Hepatocellular carcinoma Colorectal liver metastases (resection is potentially curative here, unlike most metastatic disease) Cholangiocarcinoma (intrahepatic) Benign lesions with symptoms or malignant potential (large adenomas, symptomatic hemangiomas) Living donor liver transplantation (partial hepatectomy from a healthy donor)

Preoperative PreparationFuture Liver Remnant Calculation

The defining preoperative planning step unique to this procedure: volumetric CT/MRI estimates the future liver remnant (FLR) — the volume of liver that will remain after resection — since inadequate remnant volume risks post-hepatectomy liver failure. A normal liver generally tolerates resection down to ~20–30% FLR; a cirrhotic or otherwise chronically diseased liver needs a substantially larger remnant (often ≥40%) given reduced baseline regenerative and functional reserve. When the calculated FLR is inadequate, portal vein embolization of the side to be resected can be performed weeks beforehand — redirecting portal flow to the future remnant, inducing it to hypertrophy before surgery, directly exploiting the liver's regenerative capacity as a planned preoperative step rather than leaving it to happen only after resection.

Risk Factors for Complications

Underlying cirrhosis/chronic liver disease — reduced regenerative capacity and baseline synthetic function Inadequate future liver remnant (above) Major intraoperative blood loss Prior chemotherapy (particularly oxaliplatin-associated sinusoidal injury, or steatohepatitis from certain regimens) — can impair regeneration independent of baseline liver health

Antibiotics

Cefazolin is standard; broadened coverage is reasonable when a biliary-enteric anastomosis is anticipated as part of the resection (some extended resections require this) given the enteric flora exposure that introduces, mirroring the same logic applied to the Whipple procedure's hepaticojejunostomy component (GI Atlas Vol. 5).

Approach & Procedure

Resections are described by the anatomic (Couinaud) segments removed — a single segment, a formal hemihepatectomy (right or left), or an extended hemihepatectomy. Minimally invasive (laparoscopic/robotic) hepatectomy is increasingly used for smaller, peripherally located resections and is associated with less blood loss and faster recovery than open surgery when anatomically feasible. Open hepatectomy remains standard for major/extended resections, central tumors near major vascular structures, or cirrhotic livers where precise hemostatic control is paramount — inflow occlusion techniques (temporarily clamping the hepatic artery/portal vein, the Pringle maneuver) are used intraoperatively to limit blood loss during transection regardless of approach.

Optimizing Outcomes

Accurate FLR assessment and, where needed, preoperative portal vein embolization are the dominant modifiable factors specific to this procedure. Minimizing intraoperative blood loss (both because transfusion itself carries risk and because it's associated with worse oncologic outcomes in cancer resections) and center/surgeon volume — again, as with the Whipple and esophagectomy — meaningfully affect outcomes.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Close monitoring for post-hepatectomy liver failure in the first several days — the complication FLR planning is specifically designed to prevent Gradual return of liver volume/function over weeks to months as regeneration proceeds — most regrowth occurs within the first few weeks Activity restriction similar to other major abdominal surgery — avoid heavy lifting for several weeks Surveillance imaging on an oncologic schedule for cancer resections

Postoperative Lab Changes

FindingSignificance
Transient transaminitis and hyperbilirubinemiaExpected in the first several days from surgical manipulation and the remnant liver's regenerative stress — should trend down, not up
Rising bilirubin with coagulopathy (elevated INR) beyond the first few daysThe classic biochemical signature of post-hepatectomy liver failure — the remnant is inadequate to meet synthetic/metabolic demand; the "50-50 criteria" (bilirubin >50 µmol/L and INR >1.5 on postoperative day 5) is a validated predictor of major morbidity/mortality in this setting

Perianal & Anorectal Surgery

Smaller in scope than the rest of this atlas's major resections, but among the most common ER-adjacent GI surgical presentations — and the one category where getting the anatomy (sphincter complex) wrong has outsized functional consequences.

Perianal Abscess — Incision & Drainage

Indications: a fluctuant, tender perianal or perirectal mass — drainage should not wait for antibiotics alone to work, since an undrained abscess under pressure will not resolve without source control regardless of antibiotic choice. Procedure: incision over the point of maximal fluctuance, breaking up loculations, with the cavity left open to drain (rather than primary closure, which risks re-abscess formation) — simple perianal abscesses are frequently drained under local anesthesia in the ED/clinic setting itself, while deeper (ischiorectal, supralevator) abscesses generally require the OR for adequate anesthesia and exposure. Antibiotics: not routinely required after adequate drainage of a simple abscess in an immunocompetent patient, though used for surrounding cellulitis, immunosuppression, diabetes, or systemic signs of infection.

Roughly a third to half develop a fistula afterward
A perianal abscess is thought to originate from an infected anal crypt gland, and a meaningful proportion of drained abscesses go on to form a chronic fistula-in-ano connecting the anal canal to the skin — patients should be counseled about this possibility at the time of initial drainage rather than being surprised by it at follow-up.

Fistulotomy / Fistula Surgery

Indications: symptomatic (draining, recurrently infected) fistula-in-ano, generally addressed after the acute abscess has resolved rather than at the time of initial drainage. Procedure: for a simple, low fistula tract not involving a significant portion of the sphincter complex, fistulotomy (laying the tract open along its length to heal by secondary intention) is definitive and curative. For complex or high fistulas crossing significant sphincter muscle, alternative sphincter-sparing techniques (seton placement, LIFT procedure, fibrin glue/plug) are used instead, specifically to avoid the incontinence risk of dividing too much sphincter muscle.

Hemorrhoidectomy

Indications: grade III–IV internal hemorrhoids (prolapsing, not spontaneously or manually reducible) or symptomatic external hemorrhoids refractory to conservative management — most hemorrhoidal disease is managed nonoperatively (fiber, sitz baths, topical agents, rubber band ligation for lower-grade internal disease) with excisional surgery reserved for more advanced or refractory cases. Procedure: excision of the redundant hemorrhoidal tissue/vascular cushions; stapled hemorrhoidopexy is an alternative technique repositioning rather than excising tissue, generally less painful postoperatively but with a higher long-term recurrence rate than excisional hemorrhoidectomy.

Lateral Internal SphincterotomyFor Anal Fissure

Indications: chronic anal fissure refractory to medical therapy (topical nitrates or calcium channel blockers — deliberately mimicking the NO-mediated smooth muscle relaxation mechanism from GI Atlas Vol. 1's Esophagus section, here applied to the internal anal sphincter instead of the LES; botulinum toxin injection is a further medical option before surgery, exploiting the same ACh-blocking mechanism used at the LES in achalasia treatment, GI Atlas Vol. 3). Procedure: a partial, controlled division of the internal anal sphincter reduces the elevated resting sphincter tone that perpetuates fissure ischemia and impaired healing — directly treating the physiologic driver (sphincter hypertonicity) rather than just the fissure itself, conceptually similar to how a Nissen fundoplication (GI Atlas Vol. 5) addresses LES physiology directly rather than just the resulting reflux symptoms.

Common Threads Across This Category

Most of these procedures are outpatient/same-day — a genuine contrast to the major resections elsewhere in this atlas Sitz baths, fiber, and stool softeners are near-universal postoperative recommendations across this entire category, minimizing straining and mechanical trauma to the healing site Sphincter-sparing technique selection (fistula surgery) or controlled/partial sphincter division (fissure surgery) both reflect the same core principle: preserving continence is prioritized over the most complete anatomic cure when the two are in tension Postoperative urinary retention is a disproportionately common complication in this category given the perianal location and associated pain/spasm — a genuinely common reason for an ER visit shortly after these procedures

Postoperative Lab Changes

Routine labs are generally unremarkable for this category given its typically outpatient, localized nature — a notable contrast to nearly every other section in this atlas. New fever, worsening pain out of proportion to the procedure, or systemic signs after any of these procedures should prompt reassessment for necrotizing soft tissue infection (Fournier's gangrene) — rare, but a true surgical emergency in the perianal region given the area's proximity to multiple fascial planes, and one of the few genuinely life-threatening complications hiding within an otherwise low-acuity surgical category.

Small Bowel Resection

A single procedure whose indications range from a true minutes-count vascular emergency to a planned, elective operation — location and cause of the diseased segment determine everything downstream.

Indications

CauseUrgency
Acute mesenteric ischemia (embolic, thrombotic, or non-occlusive)True emergency — pain out of proportion to exam is the classic teaching sign, reflecting bowel ischemia before peritonitis develops; delay directly costs viable bowel length
Strangulated obstruction with non-viable bowel (this volume's Bowel Obstruction section)Emergent, identified intraoperatively
Crohn's disease strictures refractory to medical therapy (GI Atlas Vol. 4)Elective/semi-elective
Small bowel tumors (primary adenocarcinoma, carcinoid — GI Atlas Vol. 1, GIST)Elective, oncologic
Meckel's diverticulum with complications (bleeding, obstruction, diverticulitis)Variable

Acute Mesenteric IschemiaWhy Speed Dominates Everything Else in This Section

The superior mesenteric artery supplies the majority of the small bowel — embolic occlusion (often cardiac source, e.g., atrial fibrillation) classically causes the most sudden, severe presentation, while thrombotic occlusion (on a background of chronic mesenteric atherosclerosis) may have a preceding history of "intestinal angina" (postprandial pain, food fear, weight loss) before acute thrombosis completes the occlusion. Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia reflects severe splanchnic vasoconstriction during shock/low-flow states rather than a fixed arterial lesion — a distinct mechanism requiring hemodynamic optimization rather than embolectomy or bypass. CT angiography is the diagnostic modality of choice; diagnostic delay directly costs bowel length, since irreversibly ischemic bowel only becomes more extensive with time.

Preoperative Preparation

For mesenteric ischemia: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and rapid transition to angiography/OR — this is a "minutes matter" presentation similar in urgency to perforated viscus (this volume's earlier section). For elective indications (Crohn's strictures, tumors): standard bowel prep is generally not required for isolated small bowel surgery the way it is for colorectal resection (GI Atlas Vol. 5), since small bowel content carries a lower bacterial bioburden than colonic content.

Antibiotics

Cefazolin for elective small bowel resection; broad-spectrum coverage (as in the Perforated Viscus and Bowel Obstruction sections) whenever ischemic or perforated bowel is present or suspected — the antibiotic choice again follows the same "clean elective field vs. established contamination" logic that recurs throughout this entire surgical volume.

Procedure

The diseased or non-viable segment is resected with margins into grossly healthy, well-perfused bowel, and continuity restored with a primary stapled or hand-sewn anastomosis in most cases. For acute mesenteric ischemia specifically, a second-look laparotomy 24–48 hours later is often planned deliberately — rather than committing to an anastomosis immediately in bowel of uncertain viability, the abdomen is left temporarily open or the questionable segment left in discontinuity, with a return trip to reassess viability once demarcation is clearer. Vascular intervention (embolectomy, thrombectomy, or bypass) may be performed concurrently or beforehand for embolic/thrombotic mesenteric ischemia, aiming to restore flow to still-viable bowel before committing to resection margins.

Approach

Open laparotomy remains standard for acute mesenteric ischemia and any emergent small bowel resection, given the need for rapid, thorough exploration of the entire bowel length and the frequent need for concurrent vascular intervention. Laparoscopic resection is well-established for elective indications (Crohn's strictures, small tumors) where extensive exploration and speed aren't the dominant priorities.

Optimizing Outcomes

For mesenteric ischemia, time from symptom onset to revascularization/resection is the single dominant survival predictor — mortality rises steeply with delay, and this diagnosis is unforgiving of the "trial of observation" approach reasonable for many other abdominal presentations in this atlas. For elective resections, preserving bowel length wherever oncologically/therapeutically appropriate protects against the short bowel syndrome consequences covered in GI Atlas Vol. 1/2.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Extensive resection risks short bowel syndrome (GI Atlas Vol. 1's GLP-2/teduglutide discussion) — the more bowel removed and the more distal (terminal ileum) the loss, the greater the functional consequence B12, fat-soluble vitamin, and bile acid absorption specifically depend on an intact terminal ileum (GI Atlas Vol. 2) — resections in this region need long-term monitoring regardless of the original indication Second-look laparotomy patients require close inpatient monitoring between the index and return operations

Feeding Tube Care & ER Troubleshooting

Tract maturity is the single fact that governs almost every decision in this section — how urgently to act, whether bedside replacement is safe, and how much risk a "simple" dislodgement actually carries.

Tube Types, Quickly

TubePlacementUse case
NG (nasogastric)Nose to stomach, no tract/stoma involvedShort-term feeding or decompression — no tract-maturity considerations at all, the simplest category in this section
PEG / G-tube (gastrostomy)Percutaneous, stomach to skinLong-term enteral nutrition when gastric feeding is safe (adequate gastric emptying, low aspiration risk)
GJ-tube (gastrojejunostomy)Percutaneous, with a jejunal extension threaded through the gastric portGastric decompression plus jejunal feeding — used when gastric feeding isn't safe (severe reflux, gastroparesis, GI Atlas Vol. 3) but gastric decompression is still needed
J-tube (jejunostomy)Percutaneous or surgical, directly into jejunumLong-term jejunal feeding, bypassing the stomach entirely — no gastric decompression capability, an important distinction from the GJ-tube above

Tract MaturityThe Concept That Governs This Entire Section

A gastrocutaneous (or jejunocutaneous) tract forms as the stomach or jejunum adheres to the abdominal wall around the tube — generally considered mature at 4 weeks, though bedside replacement through an established tract is often considered reasonably safe as early as 7–10 days in practice; maturity is delayed in malnutrition, immunosuppression, ascites, or steroid use (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 3a). Once mature, the tract itself is a stable, self-supporting channel — before maturity, the stomach/jejunum can fall away from the abdominal wall if a tube is removed, meaning a blindly replaced tube can be pushed into the peritoneal cavity rather than back into the lumen.

The single most important triage question for any dislodged tube
"How long ago was this tube originally placed?" — a tube dislodged from a tract less than 4 weeks old should not be blindly replaced at the bedside; this requires endoscopic or fluoroscopic guidance (or surgical evaluation) given the real risk of creating a false tract or peritoneal placement. A tube dislodged from a mature tract (≥4 weeks) can generally be safely replaced at the bedside using the technique below.

Dislodged TubeA Race Against Tract Closure

The tract begins narrowing within 8–24 hours and can close enough to prevent easy replacement within as little as 2–4 hours in some cases — this is genuinely time-sensitive, on par with the fastest-moving problems elsewhere in this atlas.

  1. Mature tract (≥4 weeks old), dislodged <24 hours ago: place a same-size (or one size smaller, never larger) replacement gastrostomy tube or Foley catheter into the tract immediately to keep it patent, even before formal replacement — inflate the balloon with the manufacturer-recommended volume of water (never air, never saline given crystallization risk over time) and confirm position before resuming any feeds.
  2. Confirm placement before use — aspirate gastric contents and check pH, or obtain a contrast study/X-ray if there's any doubt, before resuming feeds through a bedside-replaced tube. Using a tube without confirming intraluminal (not peritoneal) position risks peritonitis from feed/gastric content leakage.
  3. Immature tract (<4 weeks), or any uncertainty: cover the stoma with clean gauze, keep the patient NPO from that tube, and arrange urgent endoscopic or interventional radiology-guided replacement rather than attempting blind bedside replacement.
  4. J-tube dislodgement carries higher stakes than G-tube dislodgement — the jejunal tract is generally less forgiving and more prone to true closure, and jejunal tubes dislodge at a higher overall rate than gastric tubes given less surrounding tissue securing them; a lower threshold for urgent specialist involvement is reasonable.

Clogged Tube

  1. Confirm the tube isn't simply kinked or clamped — check the entire external length first.
  2. Attempt gentle "milking" of the tube between the fingers if the clog is visible externally.
  3. Flush with warm water using a catheter-tip syringe, using a gentle back-and-forth (pull back, then push) motion rather than steady forward pressure alone.
  4. If unsuccessful, instill a pancreatic enzyme + sodium bicarbonate solution (a crushed non-enteric-coated pancreatic enzyme tablet combined with a non-enteric sodium bicarbonate tablet, or plain baking soda, dissolved in warm water) and let it dwell in the clamped tube for ~30 minutes before reattempting flush — this directly exploits the same enzymatic digestion principle from GI Atlas Vol. 2, deliberately breaking down the same proteinaceous/mineral debris that caused the clog in the first place.
  5. If still unsuccessful, mechanical declogging devices or wire-guided clearance (by a clinician familiar with the specific tube) can be attempted before resorting to full tube replacement.
Avoid cola, juice, and meat tenderizer — contrary to older teaching
Carbonated soda, cranberry juice, and meat tenderizer/chymotrypsin solutions were historically recommended for unclogging but are now specifically discouraged — their acidity or protein content can actually worsen a clog or cause new precipitation, particularly when combined with certain medications or formula components. The pancreatic enzyme + bicarbonate approach above has better evidence and is the current preferred second-line technique after simple warm water flushing.

Buried Bumper Syndrome

The internal bumper migrates into or through the gastric wall/tract rather than sitting flush against the mucosa — usually from excessive external tension left on the tube over time. Presents with difficulty flushing, pain with feeding, or a tube that can't be rotated/advanced normally. Recognized on exam (external bumper appears to sit unusually close to skin) and confirmed by endoscopy; requires endoscopic or surgical removal and tract reassessment rather than simple bedside troubleshooting.

Ostomy Bag Care & ER Troubleshooting

Directly continuing GI Atlas Vol. 5's ostomy creation discussion — most of what shows up in the ER traces back to the same ileostomy-vs-colostomy output physiology covered there.

High-Output IleostomyThe Highest-Yield ER Presentation in This Category

Output >1.5–2L/day risks the acute electrolyte derangements introduced in GI Atlas Vol. 5's Colorectal Resection section (hyponatremia, hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, and — with sustained losses — hypomagnesemia) directly reflecting the small bowel's limited water-concentrating capacity relative to the colon (GI Atlas Vol. 2).

ER approachDetail

Peristomal Skin Breakdown

Stomal effluent — particularly ileostomy output, given its higher digestive enzyme content compared to more distal colostomy output — is directly irritating/digestive to skin it contacts, making a properly fitted appliance the primary prevention strategy. ER approach: ensure the appliance opening is sized correctly to the stoma (too large an opening exposes skin to effluent; this is the most common preventable cause), use a barrier paste/powder for already-irritated skin, and involve a wound/ostomy nurse for fitting reassessment rather than simply treating the irritation topically without addressing the fit itself.

Stoma Prolapse

Telescoping of bowel through the stoma, sometimes several inches — more common with loop (rather than end) ostomies, and with colostomies more than ileostomies given the increased mobility of a mobile transverse or sigmoid loop. ER approach: attempt gentle manual reduction with steady pressure (sometimes aided by applying sugar to the prolapsed mucosa first — the resulting osmotic effect draws out edema fluid, making reduction easier, a technique directly analogous in principle to reducing a prolapsed rectum). If the prolapsed segment appears dusky, non-viable, or reduction is unsuccessful, this requires urgent surgical evaluation rather than repeated reduction attempts.

Stoma Retraction

The stoma sinks below skin level, often from inadequate mobilization at the original surgery, weight gain, or scarring — makes appliance sealing difficult and predisposes to leakage and peristomal skin breakdown (above). ER approach: convex appliances and ostomy belts can improve the seal short-term; significant or symptomatic retraction ultimately requires surgical revision, which isn't an ER-manageable fix.

Parastomal Hernia

A hernia forming around the stoma site — common (reported in a substantial minority of ostomies over time) given the deliberate fascial defect the stoma itself requires. ER approach: most are managed conservatively (supportive garments, activity modification) since they're often asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic — the same incarceration/strangulation red flags from GI Atlas Vol. 5's Hernia Repair section apply here too, and a painful, irreducible, or obstructed parastomal hernia warrants the same urgent surgical evaluation as any other incarcerated hernia rather than routine outpatient referral.

Peristomal Varices

In patients with portal hypertension, dilated submucosal veins can form at the mucocutaneous junction of the stoma — a portosystemic shunt point analogous to esophageal varices, but visible externally at the stoma itself. Can bleed briskly. ER approach: direct pressure first; suture ligation or, for refractory bleeding, interventional radiology-guided embolization/TIPS may be needed — this is a genuinely distinct bleeding source from the more common causes of ostomy-adjacent bleeding (minor mucosal trauma from appliance changes) and should be specifically considered in any ostomy patient with known or suspected liver disease presenting with peristomal bleeding.

No Output / Obstruction

Absent ostomy output with abdominal distension/pain raises concern for obstruction at or proximal to the stoma — the same bowel obstruction principles from this volume's Bowel Obstruction section apply, just with a stoma providing an unusually direct window into bowel function. A food bolus obstruction specifically at the stoma itself (undigested high-fiber food impacting at the fascial opening) is a distinct, mechanically simpler cause worth considering first in an ileostomy patient with sudden cessation of output and cramping — sometimes resolved with warm compresses, gentle massage, and hydration alone, without needing full obstruction workup, though persistent symptoms still warrant imaging.

Closing This Volume

Nearly every ostomy complication in this section traces back to physiology established earlier in this atlas — high output to the ileostomy-vs-colostomy absorption difference (GI Atlas Vol. 2), skin breakdown to the same digestive enzyme content responsible for normal small bowel digestion, and prolapse/hernia/retraction to the same fascial and anatomic considerations covered in this volume's surgical sections. Device troubleshooting, like the surgeries themselves, is ultimately applied physiology.

Appendectomy

The most common emergency abdominal surgery — and one of the cleanest examples of laparoscopic approach becoming the default rather than the exception.

Indications

Acute appendicitis — luminal obstruction (fecalith most common, also lymphoid hyperplasia, rarely tumor) causing distension, impaired venous drainage, bacterial overgrowth, and progressive wall ischemia that can perforate if untreated. Suspected appendicitis with equivocal imaging in a clinically compelling presentation is also a reasonable indication, given the cost of delayed treatment for a ruptured appendix.

Risk Factors for Complicated Disease

Delayed presentation (>48–72h of symptoms) Extremes of age (higher perforation rates in young children and elderly, often from atypical/delayed presentation) Immunosuppression Diabetes

Preoperative Preparation

Minimal specific bowel prep needed (unlike colorectal surgery — this volume's Colorectal Resection section) — primarily IV fluid resuscitation, correcting any electrolyte derangement from vomiting/poor oral intake, and NPO status. CT imaging (or ultrasound, particularly in children/pregnancy to avoid radiation) confirms diagnosis and identifies perforation/abscess preoperatively when the presentation is equivocal.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Cefoxitin (or ceftriaxone + metronidazole, or cefazolin + metronidazole as alternatives) — targeting the mixed aerobic/anaerobic colonic flora the inflamed appendix has been exposed to. If the patient already received cefoxitin or ceftriaxone in the emergency department, a repeat preoperative dose is needed if more than one half-life-appropriate interval (roughly 2 hours for cefoxitin, 12 hours for ceftriaxone) has elapsed since that dose — a genuinely easy detail to miss in a fast-moving ED-to-OR handoff. For perforated/complicated appendicitis, antibiotics typically continue postoperatively (duration guided by source control adequacy) rather than being purely prophylactic.

Approach & Procedure

Laparoscopic is now the default approach for the large majority of cases — three small port incisions, the appendix is identified and its mesoappendix (containing the appendiceal artery) is divided, the base is stapled or ligated, and the specimen is removed in a retrieval bag to avoid contaminating the port site. Open appendectomy (a right lower quadrant McBurney or Rocky-Davis incision) is reserved for cases with dense adhesions, hemodynamic instability, pregnancy in some centers, or surgeon/patient-specific factors, and remains the fallback when laparoscopic visualization is inadequate.

Interval appendectomy — a notable exception to immediate surgery
When appendicitis has progressed to a well-formed peri-appendiceal abscess or phlegmon at presentation, initial management is often percutaneous drainage plus IV antibiotics rather than immediate surgery, given the technical difficulty and complication risk of operating on densely inflamed, abscessed tissue — with appendectomy deferred 6–8 weeks later ("interval appendectomy") once inflammation has resolved. This is a genuine exception to the "appendicitis means surgery now" default worth knowing.

Optimizing Outcomes

Time-to-OR remains the dominant modifiable factor — earlier operation before perforation lowers complication rates far more than any other single intervention here. Beyond timing: adequate fluid resuscitation before induction, appropriately timed antibiotic prophylaxis (within 60 minutes of incision), and laparoscopic approach where feasible (lower wound infection rate, faster recovery than open) all contribute.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Uncomplicated laparoscopic: same-day or next-day discharge typical Complicated/perforated: longer inpatient stay, extended antibiotic course Activity: gradual return to normal activity over 1–2 weeks; heavier lifting restricted longer if open approach Return precautions: fever, worsening pain, or wound drainage warrant re-evaluation for intra-abdominal abscess

Postoperative Lab Changes

Mild leukocytosis is expected in the immediate postoperative period from surgical stress alone and doesn't necessarily indicate infection — trend direction matters more than a single elevated value. A leukocytosis that rises rather than trends down beyond 48–72 hours, especially with fever, should prompt imaging for an intra-abdominal abscess, particularly after perforated appendicitis.

Cholecystectomy

Directly downstream of the bile physiology in GI Atlas Vol. 2 — and the classic teaching case for the "critical view of safety" that prevents the procedure's most feared complication.

Indications

Symptomatic cholelithiasis (biliary colic) Acute cholecystitis Gallstone pancreatitis (once pancreatitis resolves, generally same-admission) Choledocholithiasis (often with preoperative or intraoperative ERCP) Gallbladder polyps >1cm or with concerning features

Risk Factors for Difficult Surgery/Complications

Acute cholecystitis with prolonged symptom duration (>72h) — increased inflammation/fibrosis Prior upper abdominal surgery (adhesions) Obesity Cirrhosis/portal hypertension (bleeding risk, altered anatomy) Male sex (associated with more severe/advanced disease at presentation in some series)

Preoperative Preparation

NPO status, correction of any coagulopathy, right upper quadrant ultrasound to confirm diagnosis (looking for wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy sign) and to screen for common bile duct dilation suggesting choledocholithiasis. Liver function tests and lipase help identify concurrent choledocholithiasis or gallstone pancreatitis that may change the operative/procedural plan (e.g., need for preoperative ERCP).

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Routine prophylactic antibiotics are not required for elective, uncomplicated laparoscopic cholecystectomy in low-risk patients per current guidance — a genuinely underappreciated point given how routinely antibiotics are still sometimes given out of habit. For higher-risk biliary cases (acute cholecystitis, obstructive jaundice, common duct stones, or a non-functioning gallbladder), a single dose of cefazolin (or ceftriaxone) is reasonable, targeting the biliary flora relevant to acutely inflamed/obstructed biliary systems.

Approach & Procedure

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the standard of care — four small port incisions, with the gallbladder dissected away from the liver bed and the cystic artery and cystic duct individually identified, clipped, and divided. The critical view of safety — clearing the hepatocystic triangle of fat/fibrous tissue until only two structures (cystic duct, cystic artery) are seen entering the gallbladder, with the lower gallbladder freed from the liver bed — is the deliberate safety step taken specifically to avoid misidentifying and transecting the common bile duct, the procedure's most feared complication.

Conversion to open is a safety decision, not a failure
When anatomy is unclear or bleeding is difficult to control laparoscopically, converting to an open procedure (a right subcostal/Kocher incision) is the appropriate safety-first decision — this should be framed to patients preoperatively as a possibility, not an adverse outcome, given that forcing a laparoscopic completion in unclear anatomy is precisely how bile duct injuries occur.

Optimizing Outcomes

Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (within 72 hours of symptom onset) for acute cholecystitis is associated with lower complication rates and shorter total hospital stay than delayed surgery after a course of antibiotics alone — timing again emerges as one of the most modifiable outcome factors across GI surgery generally. Achieving and documenting the critical view of safety before dividing any structure is the single most impactful technical practice for avoiding bile duct injury.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Uncomplicated laparoscopic: same-day discharge is now common Diet: no long-term dietary restriction required for most patients; some experience transient fat intolerance/loose stools Bile acid malabsorption diarrhea (GI Atlas Vol. 2) can emerge post-cholecystectomy in a subset of patients — loss of the gallbladder's storage/concentration function changes bile delivery timing, occasionally unmasking or worsening bile-acid-mediated colonic irritation Return precautions: fever, jaundice, or persistent pain should prompt evaluation for a retained common duct stone or bile leak

Postoperative Lab Changes

Mild, transient LFT elevation is common in the first 24–48 hours from surgical manipulation alone. Persistently rising or markedly elevated bilirubin/alkaline phosphatase beyond that window should raise concern for a retained common bile duct stone or bile duct injury — direct hyperbilirubinemia specifically (rather than the mixed pattern of hepatocellular stress) points toward an obstructive/leak process requiring imaging (ultrasound, MRCP, or HIDA scan) rather than simple observation.

Colorectal Resection & Ostomy Creation

The one category of GI surgery where bowel prep and antibiotic prophylaxis genuinely diverge from the rest of this volume — because colonic content is the highest-bioburden material any of these procedures encounters.

Preoperative PreparationWhere This Category Diverges

Elective colorectal surgery is the one procedure category in this volume where mechanical bowel preparation combined with oral antibiotics is standard — reducing luminal bioburden before the bowel is opened. Current evidence supports combining both (mechanical prep alone, without oral antibiotics, has not shown the same infection-reduction benefit) — a genuinely important practice-pattern detail, since mechanical prep alone was standard practice for years before this combination approach was better established.

Mechanical bowel prep

Polyethylene glycol-based solution (GI Atlas Vol. 4's Laxatives section) the day before surgery — physically clearing stool from the colon.

Oral antibiotic prep

Neomycin plus erythromycin, or neomycin plus metronidazole, dosed at specific intervals the day before surgery — reduces intraluminal bacterial concentration directly, distinct from and additive to the IV prophylaxis given at incision.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis (IV, at Incision)

Cefazolin plus metronidazole is standard — covering both the aerobic gram-negative and anaerobic flora specific to colonic content, a broader combination than the cefazolin-alone default used for most other procedures in this volume. Cefoxitin or cefotetan monotherapy (second-generation cephalosporins with inherent anaerobic activity) is a reasonable single-agent alternative. Redosing intra-operatively is needed for cefazolin/cefoxitin if the case exceeds roughly two half-lives (about 4 hours for cefazolin) — genuinely relevant given colorectal resections often run longer than simpler procedures.

Approach & Procedure

Laparoscopic (or robotic-assisted laparoscopic) colectomy is now preferred where feasible — comparable oncologic outcomes to open surgery for cancer resection, with faster recovery, less pain, and lower wound infection rates. Open colectomy (midline laparotomy) remains necessary for emergent cases (perforation, obstruction with peritonitis), extensive adhesions, or very large/locally invasive tumors. The resected segment's vascular pedicle is ligated, the segment removed, and bowel continuity restored via a stapled or hand-sewn anastomosis — or, when an anastomosis is deemed too risky (contamination, hemodynamic instability, poor tissue perfusion, emergent setting), a temporary or permanent ostomy is created instead.

Ostomy Creation

IleostomyTerminal ileumHigh-volume, liquid, continuous — directly reflects the small intestine's limited water-absorption role relative to the colon (GI Atlas Vol. 2), meaning ileostomy output carries real dehydration/electrolyte risk that colostomy output generally doesn't

Ostomies may be temporary (protecting a distal anastomosis while it heals, reversed at a later surgery once healing is confirmed — often by contrast enema) or permanent (when the distal rectum/anus has been removed entirely, as in abdominoperineal resection for low rectal cancer, or when reversal risk is judged too high).

Optimizing Outcomes

Full ERAS protocol adherence (GI Atlas Vol. 3's closing section) has outsized benefit specifically in colorectal surgery given its historically higher ileus/complication burden — early feeding, opioid-sparing analgesia, and early mobilization all directly target the postoperative ileus mechanisms already covered in this series. Preoperative nutritional optimization (addressing albumin/prealbumin deficits before elective surgery where time allows) and smoking cessation counseling are additional modifiable factors specific to this category given the generally older, higher-comorbidity population undergoing colorectal cancer surgery.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

New ostomy: dedicated ostomy nurse teaching before discharge is essential — appliance fitting, skin care, output monitoring High-output ileostomy: dehydration is a leading cause of early readmission — patients need explicit output thresholds (typically >1.5–2L/day) that should prompt call-back Diet: low-residue diet initially post-op, particularly with a new ostomy or anastomosis, advancing as tolerated Activity: avoid heavy lifting 4–6 weeks to protect fascial healing, particularly relevant given incisional hernia risk at stoma/extraction sites

Bariatric Surgery — Sleeve Gastrectomy & Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass

Directly built on GI Atlas Vol. 1's ghrelin and PYY physiology — these procedures work because of specific hormonal consequences of the anatomy, not just mechanical restriction.

Indications

BMI ≥40, or ≥35 with an obesity-related comorbidity (diabetes, hypertension, OSA) Failure of adequate nonsurgical weight loss attempts Increasingly, BMI ≥30 with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes per updated society guidance

Sleeve Gastrectomy

Roughly 80% of the stomach — including the entire fundus — is resected along the greater curvature, leaving a narrow gastric "sleeve." Purely restrictive by volume, but the outsized weight-loss and metabolic effect comes from removing the fundus, the primary source of ghrelin (GI Atlas Vol. 1) — circulating ghrelin drops substantially and durably post-op, independently suppressing appetite beyond what reduced stomach volume alone would explain.

Fig. 2 — Sleeve gastrectomy resection line, removing the fundus

Approach: laparoscopic in the overwhelming majority of cases. Antibiotic prophylaxis: cefazolin, often at the higher weight-based dose (this volume's cross-cutting antibiotic section) given the target population's typically elevated body weight.

Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass

A small (~30mL) gastric pouch is created from the proximal stomach and connected directly to a segment of jejunum (the Roux limb), bypassing the rest of the stomach, duodenum, and proximal jejunum entirely. The bypassed segment — carrying bile and pancreatic secretions — is reconnected further downstream at the jejunojejunostomy, where digestive juices finally mix with food for the first time in the common channel.

Fig. 3 — Roux-en-Y gastric bypass reconstruction

Combines restriction (small pouch) with a genuine metabolic/hormonal effect distinct from the sleeve's mechanism: food reaching the distal small bowel faster and less digested than normal triggers an exaggerated PYY and GLP-1 ("ileal brake," GI Atlas Vol. 1) response, contributing independently to satiety and — notably — to gastric bypass's disproportionately strong effect on type 2 diabetes remission, often preceding significant weight loss itself, implicating the incretin/hormonal change as a distinct mechanism from calorie restriction alone.

Dumping syndrome
Rapid delivery of hyperosmolar food directly into the jejunum (bypassing the stomach's normal gradual-emptying function entirely) causes fluid shifts into the bowel lumen — early dumping (within 30 minutes: GI cramping, diarrhea, tachycardia, flushing from the fluid shift itself) and late dumping (1–3 hours later: reactive hypoglycemia from an exaggerated insulin response to the rapid glucose load — directly reflecting the incretin-amplified insulin release above, in this case overshooting). Managed primarily with dietary modification (smaller meals, separating liquids from solids, reducing simple sugars) rather than medication in most cases.

Risk Factors for Complications

Super obesity (BMI ≥50) — technically more difficult surgery, higher leak/complication rates OSA (perioperative respiratory complication risk) Smoking (anastomotic/marginal ulcer risk, particularly gastric bypass) Prior abdominal surgery (adhesions)

Preoperative Preparation

Multidisciplinary preoperative evaluation is standard — nutritional counseling, psychological evaluation, and often a preoperative low-calorie/liquid diet for 1–2 weeks specifically to shrink the liver (reducing hepatic steatosis bulk improves surgical visualization and safety, particularly for the left hepatic lobe overlying the stomach). Preoperative optimization of OSA (CPAP), diabetes control, and smoking cessation directly target the risk factors above.

Optimizing Outcomes

Preoperative liver-shrinking diet adherence, surgeon/center case-volume (bariatric surgery shows a clear volume-outcome relationship), and structured postoperative dietary progression all matter — but long-term success depends heavily on sustained engagement with the multidisciplinary program (nutrition follow-up, support groups) rather than the operation alone.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Staged diet progression: clear liquids → full liquids → pureed → soft → regular over several weeks Lifelong vitamin/mineral supplementation, especially after gastric bypass — B12 (bypassed stomach/duodenum affects intrinsic factor availability and the R-protein handoff, GI Atlas Vol. 2), iron, calcium, and fat-soluble vitamins all require monitoring NSAID avoidance long-term, particularly after gastric bypass — markedly increased marginal ulcer risk at the gastrojejunostomy Pregnancy typically deferred 12–18 months post-op to allow weight stabilization

Postoperative Lab Changes

FindingMechanism
Rapid, early improvement in glucose/HbA1c (gastric bypass especially)Incretin effect changes, often preceding significant weight loss — a genuinely distinct mechanism from simple caloric restriction (this section's diabetes remission discussion)
B12, iron, calcium, vitamin D deficiency (longer-term)Bypassed absorptive segments (GI Atlas Vol. 2) — routine annual monitoring is standard of care, not just for symptomatic patients
Oxalate nephrolithiasis risk (gastric bypass)Fat malabsorption (Vol. 2) leaves free fatty acids to bind luminal calcium, leaving oxalate unbound and available for increased colonic absorption — a genuinely non-obvious downstream consequence of the fat-handling physiology covered earlier in this series

Hernia Repair & Antireflux Surgery

Nissen fundoplication is a direct, deliberate mechanical fix for the LES physiology introduced all the way back in GI Atlas Vol. 1's Esophagus section.

Inguinal & Ventral/Incisional Hernia Repair

Indications: symptomatic hernia, or any hernia with signs of incarceration (irreducible, painful) or strangulation (compromised blood supply — a surgical emergency given bowel ischemia risk). Approach: laparoscopic or robotic-assisted repair (using mesh placed preperitoneally) is now common for both inguinal and ventral hernias, offering less postoperative pain and faster recovery than open repair, though open repair (with mesh, or primary tissue repair for small defects) remains standard in certain anatomic situations or when strangulated bowel requires resection through the same field. Antibiotics: cefazolin is typical when mesh is placed, reflecting the higher infection consequence of a mesh-related surgical site infection compared to a clean tissue repair.

Strangulated hernia is a different operative conversation entirely
A strangulated hernia requires emergent surgery, often converting what would otherwise be an elective mesh repair into a more complex operation given the possible need for bowel resection if the incarcerated segment isn't viable on direct inspection — the preoperative discussion, antibiotic choice (broader coverage anticipating possible bowel resection), and postoperative course all shift accordingly.

Nissen FundoplicationIndications

GERD refractory to maximal medical therapy (GI Atlas Vol. 4's acid suppression section) Large hiatal hernia with reflux symptoms Patient preference to avoid lifelong PPI therapy

Procedure

The gastric fundus is mobilized and wrapped 360° around the distal esophagus, then sutured to itself — mechanically reinforcing LES competence by creating a high-pressure zone that the natural, native sphincter (GI Atlas Vol. 1) can no longer be overcome by reflux, regardless of the underlying LES tone deficit. Any hiatal hernia present is reduced and the diaphragmatic crura are approximated (crural repair) as part of the same operation, since the hernia itself is often what allowed reflux to become severe enough to need surgery in the first place.

Fig. 4 — Nissen fundoplication: 360° fundic wrap around the distal esophagus

Approach: laparoscopic in the large majority of current cases. Antibiotics: cefazolin is standard given the clean-contaminated nature of the field (esophageal/gastric lumen is entered, though not colonic-level bioburden).

Patient Selection Matters — Esophageal Motility Testing First

Why preoperative manometry is essential, not optional
A full 360° (Nissen) wrap in a patient with an underlying esophageal body motility disorder (GI Atlas Vol. 3's Chicago Classification section — particularly absent contractility or a spastic disorder) can produce severe, difficult-to-treat dysphagia postoperatively, since the weakened or absent peristaltic esophageal body may not generate enough propulsive force to push food through the new, tighter wrap. This is exactly why high-resolution manometry is obtained before antireflux surgery — patients with significant body dysmotility are often directed toward a partial wrap (Toupet — 270° posterior, or Dor — anterior) instead, providing reflux control with less outflow resistance than a full Nissen wrap.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Staged post-op diet: liquids → soft foods → regular over several weeks, minimizing strain on the fresh wrap Gas bloat syndrome: inability to belch/vomit normally given the wrap's mechanical effect, causing bloating and early satiety — usually improves over weeks to months as post-surgical edema resolves Dysphagia is common in the first several weeks (surgical edema) and should improve — persistent dysphagia beyond 6–8 weeks warrants evaluation for a too-tight wrap or wrap migration/herniation Avoid carbonated beverages and large meals initially given the reduced ability to vent gas

Postoperative Lab Changes

Routine labs are generally unremarkable after uncomplicated antireflux or hernia surgery — this category is notable specifically for having less expected lab derangement than the resection-heavy procedures elsewhere in this volume, since no bowel segment or absorptive surface is removed. New anemia or unexpected inflammatory markers should prompt evaluation for an unrecognized enterotomy or mesh-related infection rather than being attributed to routine postoperative physiology.

Whipple Procedure (Pancreaticoduodenectomy)

The most anatomically complex operation in this volume — one resection touching four organs, requiring three separate reconstructive anastomoses to restore continuity.

Indications

Pancreatic head adenocarcinoma (most common) Ampullary carcinoma Distal cholangiocarcinoma Duodenal adenocarcinoma Select cases of chronic pancreatitis with an inflammatory head mass causing intractable pain/obstruction

What Gets Removed

Pancreatic head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, gallbladder, and (in the classic Whipple) the distal stomach/pylorus — or, in the increasingly favored pylorus-preserving variant, the pylorus and proximal duodenum are retained, avoiding a gastric resection and its associated dumping syndrome risk (this volume's Bariatric Surgery section covers the same dumping mechanism in a different surgical context).

ReconstructionThree Anastomoses, One Jejunal Limb

Fig. 5 — Whipple reconstruction: pancreaticojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, gastrojejunostomy in sequence

A single loop of jejunum is brought up to sequentially reconnect all three transected structures, restoring the digestive and biliary drainage sequence in the correct order:

  1. Pancreaticojejunostomy — the remaining pancreatic body/tail is anastomosed to the jejunum first (most proximal) — this is the technically most demanding anastomosis and the dominant source of the procedure's most feared complication (below).
  2. Hepaticojejunostomy — the common hepatic duct is anastomosed next, restoring biliary drainage.
  3. Gastrojejunostomy (or duodenojejunostomy in the pylorus-preserving variant) — the stomach/duodenal remnant is anastomosed last, most distally, restoring GI continuity.

This sequence — pancreatic, then biliary, then gastric, all draining into the same jejunal limb in that order — mirrors the normal anatomic sequence these three systems would meet the duodenum in unoperated anatomy, just achieved via three new surgical connections instead of the native ampulla of Vater.

Risk Factors for Complications

Soft, non-fibrotic pancreatic texture (paradoxically higher leak risk than a fibrotic gland — a fibrotic pancreas holds sutures better) Small (<3mm) pancreatic duct diameter — technically harder anastomosis Low surgeon/center case volume — a well-established volume-outcome relationship, arguably stronger for this procedure than almost any other in general surgery Preoperative biliary obstruction/cholangitis

Preoperative Preparation

Cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI) to confirm resectability (assessing key vascular involvement — superior mesenteric artery/vein, portal vein), nutritional optimization given frequent baseline weight loss/malnutrition in pancreatic cancer patients, and preoperative biliary drainage (ERCP with stenting) in patients with significant obstructive jaundice/cholangitis, though routine drainage in the absence of cholangitis is debated given some data suggesting it may increase infectious complications without clear benefit.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Cefazolin is standard for most cases; broader coverage (extending to biliary/enteric organisms, sometimes with an added anaerobic agent) is often used when a preoperative biliary stent is present, given the stent's tendency to colonize the biliary tree with a more complex bacterial flora than an unstented system.

Approach

Traditionally an open procedure given the complexity of the resection and three-way reconstruction — minimally invasive (laparoscopic or robotic) Whipple is increasingly performed at high-volume specialized centers, but open remains the more common and, outside dedicated high-volume programs, generally the safer default approach for this particular operation, unlike most other procedures in this volume where laparoscopic has already become the clear standard.

Optimizing Outcomes

Case volume is the single most consistently demonstrated outcome factor for this specific operation — mortality and major complication rates are meaningfully lower at high-volume pancreatic surgery centers, more so than for almost any other procedure in this atlas. Preoperative nutritional optimization, a dedicated ERAS pathway adapted for pancreatic surgery, and multidisciplinary perioperative management (often including early, protocolized drain fluid amylase checks to catch a pancreatic leak early) all contribute.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Prolonged inpatient stay relative to most procedures in this volume — typically 1–2 weeks even uncomplicated Pancreatic enzyme replacement (GI Atlas Vol. 4) often needed long-term given reduced pancreatic parenchymal mass and altered anatomy New or worsened diabetes is common — reduced islet cell mass from the resection (Endocrine Atlas Vol. 1's pancreatic section) directly reduces insulin reserve Delayed gastric emptying is a common, generally self-limited early complication, particularly with the pylorus-preserving variant

Esophagectomy & Gastrectomy

Upper GI cancer surgery — where the reconstruction has to solve the same basic problem as bariatric surgery (restoring continuity after removing a major organ segment) but for oncologic rather than metabolic reasons.

EsophagectomyIndications

Esophageal adenocarcinoma or squamous cell carcinoma (localized/locally advanced, post-neoadjuvant therapy) High-grade dysplasia in Barrett's esophagus not amenable to endoscopic therapy End-stage achalasia (rare — GI Atlas Vol. 3) refractory to all other interventions

Procedure & Reconstruction

The esophagus (partial or near-total) is resected, and continuity is most commonly restored using the stomach, fashioned into a narrow "gastric conduit" and pulled up into the chest or neck to anastomose with the remaining proximal esophagus — the stomach's robust blood supply (via the right gastroepiploic artery, preserved deliberately during conduit creation) makes it the preferred reconstructive organ over colon or jejunum interposition, which are reserved for cases where the stomach itself isn't usable (prior gastric surgery, disease involving the stomach).

Approach variantAnastomosis locationNote

McKeown (three-field)Cervical (neck)Abdominal + thoracic + neck incisions; an anastomotic leak here is generally more manageable (drains externally through the neck wound) than an intrathoracic leak, a genuine trade-off against the added third incision

Approach: minimally invasive (laparoscopic/thoracoscopic or robotic-assisted) esophagectomy is increasingly preferred at experienced centers, with comparable oncologic outcomes and reduced pulmonary complications compared to open — but open transthoracic or transhiatal approaches remain common given the procedure's complexity and the frequent need to convert.

GastrectomyPartial vs. Total

Indications: gastric adenocarcinoma (location determines extent — distal tumors allow subtotal/partial gastrectomy preserving the proximal stomach; proximal/diffuse tumors, or hereditary diffuse gastric cancer from a CDH1 mutation, require total gastrectomy). Reconstruction after total gastrectomy is most commonly a Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy — directly reusing the same Roux limb concept from this volume's Bariatric Surgery section, here connecting the esophagus (rather than a small gastric pouch) straight to jejunum since no stomach remains at all.

Risk Factors for Complications

Baseline malnutrition/sarcopenia (common in both esophageal and gastric cancer given pre-diagnosis dysphagia/early satiety) Smoking (pulmonary complications, particularly relevant given esophagectomy's thoracic component) Neoadjuvant chemoradiation (tissue effects can complicate anastomotic healing) Low surgeon/center volume — a similar relationship to the Whipple procedure's volume-outcome pattern

Preoperative Preparation

Nutritional optimization is disproportionately important here given how commonly these patients present already malnourished from months of dysphagia or early satiety — sometimes requiring preoperative enteral (feeding tube) or parenteral nutrition support before surgery is even feasible. Cardiopulmonary optimization matters particularly for esophagectomy's thoracic component; multidisciplinary staging (endoscopic ultrasound, PET-CT) confirms resectability and appropriate neoadjuvant therapy sequencing beforehand.

Antibiotic Prophylaxis

Cefazolin is standard for gastrectomy; esophagectomy (given its thoracic component and the mediastinal/pleural spaces entered) often uses a similar cefazolin-based regimen, sometimes broadened if significant gastric acid suppression (raising gastric bacterial colonization, GI Atlas Vol. 1's stomach-as-barrier discussion) or obstruction is present preoperatively — mirroring the "high-risk gastroduodenal" logic used for upper GI procedures generally.

Optimizing Outcomes

As with the Whipple procedure, case volume is a dominant, consistently demonstrated outcome factor for both operations. Preoperative nutritional rehabilitation, minimally invasive approach where feasible, and a dedicated thoracic/upper-GI ERAS pathway (early mobilization is particularly protective against pulmonary complications after a thoracotomy-based approach) round out the modifiable factors.

Postoperative & Outpatient Considerations

Staged diet advancement over weeks, often starting with a contrast swallow study to confirm anastomotic integrity before oral intake begins Small, frequent meals long-term — the gastric conduit or remnant stomach has markedly reduced capacity B12, iron, calcium supplementation after total/partial gastrectomy — loss of intrinsic factor (Vol. 2) and reduced acid-mediated iron/calcium absorption efficiency mirror the same deficiencies seen after bariatric gastric bypass Dumping syndrome (this volume's Bariatric Surgery section) is common after gastrectomy for the same anatomic reason — loss of the stomach's normal gradual-emptying function

Laparoscopic vs. OpenThe Pattern Across This Volume

Laparoscopic (or robotic-assisted) approach has become the default for appendectomy, cholecystectomy, most colorectal resections, all bariatric surgery, and Nissen fundoplication — reflecting decades of accumulated evidence for equivalent oncologic/functional outcomes with less pain, shorter stays, and lower wound infection rates. The Whipple procedure and esophagectomy are the notable exceptions where open remains more common outside high-volume specialized centers, given the complexity of the reconstruction and the steep learning curve for a minimally invasive approach — a genuinely important distinction from the pattern seen everywhere else in this volume.

Closing the Series

This volume closes the GI Atlas by returning, procedure by procedure, to physiology established across Volumes 1–4: ghrelin and PYY explain why bariatric surgery works, bile acid and B12 absorption pathways explain the deficiencies that follow gastric/small bowel surgery, esophageal manometry explains why some antireflux patients need a partial rather than full wrap, and the ERAS/ileus mechanisms from Volume 3 explain why early feeding and opioid-sparing analgesia matter across nearly every operation here. Surgery, in this framing, is often physiology and pathology from the earlier volumes being deliberately exploited, corrected, or bypassed — rather than a separate body of knowledge.

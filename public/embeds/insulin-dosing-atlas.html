Insulin Dosing Atlas

Insulin time-action profiles, TDD & weight-based dosing, renal/CKD adjustment, DKA/HHS infusion protocols, and last-resort field-estimation methods — with linked interactive calculators.

Every insulin dosing decision starts with knowing the shape of the curve — onset, peak, and duration determine timing, hypoglycemia risk windows, and which agent fits which clinical situation.

Rapid-Acting Analogs — Lispro, Aspart, Glulisine

Mealtime / correction
Onset10–15 min
Peak1–1.5 h
Duration3–5 h
Clinical Notes
  • Give within 15 minutes before, or immediately after, a meal — mismatched timing is the most common cause of postprandial hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia with this class.
  • Duration is dose-dependent: a few units may clear in under 4 hours, while 25–30 unit doses can extend to 5–6 hours.
  • Ultra-rapid formulations (faster aspart, lispro-aabc) shave ~10-20 minutes off onset for patients needing tighter postprandial control.

Short-Acting — Regular Human Insulin

IV infusions · older regimens
Onset30–60 min
Peak2–4 h
Duration6–8 h
Clinical Notes
  • The only insulin formulated for IV infusion — its aqueous stability and decades of validated protocols make it the default for DKA/HHS drips.
  • Give ~30 minutes before a meal when used subcutaneously — the slower onset means late timing risks postprandial hyperglycemia.
  • Larger doses paradoxically have a faster onset but a longer peak and duration.

Intermediate-Acting — NPH

Basal · cost-sensitive regimens
Onset1–2 h
Peak4–12 h
Duration16–24 h
Clinical Notes
  • Has a true, pronounced peak — this is both its usefulness (covers a mid-morning or overnight period) and its main hazard (mid-day or nocturnal hypoglycemia if meals are skipped or small).
  • Requires resuspension (gentle rolling, not shaking) before every dose since it's a cloudy suspension, not a clear solution.
  • Cannot be mixed with glargine, detemir, or degludec — only with regular or rapid-acting insulins, and only immediately before injection.

Long-Acting — Glargine & Detemir

Basal · once/twice daily
Onset1–2 h
PeakMinimal/none
DurationGlargine ~24 h · Detemir 12–24 h (dose-dependent)
Clinical Notes
  • The relatively flat, near-peakless profile is the entire point — it suppresses hepatic glucose output steadily rather than acting like a bolus.
  • Never mix with any other insulin in the same syringe — this disrupts the formulation chemistry that gives glargine and detemir their prolonged action.
  • Detemir's duration is genuinely dose-dependent (shorter at low doses, approaching 24h near ≥0.8 units/kg) — some patients need twice-daily detemir dosing to maintain 24h coverage.

Ultra-Long-Acting — Degludec

Basal · flexible timing
Onset~1 h
PeakNone
DurationUp to 42 h
Clinical Notes
  • The ultra-long, essentially flat profile allows dosing time to vary by several hours day-to-day without losing coverage — useful for patients with unpredictable schedules or shift work.
  • Steady-state takes 2-3 days to reach after starting or dose changes — don't judge efficacy from a single day's glucose readings.
  • No renal dose adjustment required — see the Renal Dosing tab.

Concentrated — U-500 Regular

Severe insulin resistance
Onset~30 min
Peak4–8 h
DurationUp to 24 h
Clinical Notes
  • 5x the concentration of standard U-100 insulin (500 units/mL) — reserved for patients requiring very high total daily doses (often >200 units/day) where U-100 volume becomes impractical.
  • Behaves kinetically more like an intermediate-acting insulin at this concentration despite being 'regular' insulin — both a basal and prandial-like effect.
  • High-alert medication: dosing errors between U-500 and U-100 are a leading cause of severe insulin-related harm — always dose in 'units', use a U-500-specific syringe/pen, and never assume a standard U-100 syringe reading applies.

IV vs. Subcutaneous Insulin — Route Comparison

Route pharmacokinetics
IV continuous infusionSubQ rapid-acting bolusSubQ regular bolus
Onset~10–15 min to pharmacologic effect; a rate change reaches new steady-state within ~20–30 min10–15 min30–60 min
Plasma half-life~4–6 minutes~60–80 min (absorption-rate-limited, not clearance-limited)~90 min (absorption-rate-limited)

Reversibility once givenEffectively reversible — turn the infusion down/off, effect gone in ~20–40 minNOT reversible — the depot keeps releasing insulin for hours regardless of glucose responseNOT reversible — same depot-release limitation, over a longer window
IV continuous infusion (illustrative single-rate effect)
SubQ rapid-acting bolus
Clinical Notes
  • The 4-6 minute IV half-life is WHY continuous infusion is used instead of intermittent IV boluses — a single IV bolus would be pharmacologically gone within 20-30 minutes, far too short to manage ongoing hyperglycemia without a running drip.
  • SubQ insulin's apparent 'half-life' in the literature (60-90 min) does not reflect how fast the body clears insulin — it reflects how slowly insulin leaves the injection depot and enters circulation (so-called 'flip-flop' kinetics). This is why subQ dosing cannot be titrated in real time the way an infusion can.
  • 'Insulin stacking' — giving a repeat subQ correction dose before the prior one has finished acting — is a distinct, common, and preventable error specific to the subQ route; it isn't a comparable risk with a continuously-monitored IV infusion.

Premixed Insulins (e.g., 70/30, 75/25, 50/50)

Simplified dosing
Onset10–30 min
PeakDual peak
Duration10–16 h
Clinical Notes
  • A fixed-ratio combination of a rapid/short-acting component with an intermediate (NPH-based) component — the dual-hump curve covers one meal plus background insulin in a single injection.
  • Simplifies regimens (fewer injections) at the cost of dosing flexibility — the ratio between components can't be adjusted independently.
  • Same resuspension requirement as NPH — must be rolled gently before each dose.

Nearly every insulin dosing decision traces back to one number: the Total Daily Dose (TDD). Weight-based formulas estimate it when starting insulin; the 1800/1500/500 rules derive everything else from it once a patient is already on a regimen.

Starting TDD — Insulin-Naive Patients

RegimenStarting doseNotes
Basal-only initiation (ADA)10 units/day OR 0.1–0.2 units/kg/dayMost common starting approach in T2DM added to oral agents
Basal-only initiation (AACE)0.1–0.2 units/kg/day (A1c <8%) · 0.2–0.3 units/kg/day (A1c >8%)Higher starting dose scaled to degree of hyperglycemia
Full basal-bolus replacement — augmentation0.3 units/kg/dayFor patients with some residual endogenous insulin production
Full basal-bolus replacement — full replacement0.6–1.0 units/kg/dayType 1 diabetes or severe insulin deficiency
Inpatient basal-bolus (BG 140–200 mg/dL)0.4 units/kg/dayReduce to 0.3 units/kg/day if age ≥70 or eGFR <60
Inpatient basal-bolus (BG 201–400 mg/dL)0.5 units/kg/daySame renal/age reduction applies
Clinical Pearls
  • Type 1 diabetes overall TDD range: 0.4–1.0 units/kg/day. Type 2 diabetes: 0.3–2.0+ units/kg/day depending on the degree of insulin resistance.
  • When in doubt, start conservative and titrate — the ADA explicitly recommends adjusting by 10–15% or 2–4 units once or twice weekly based on fasting glucose, and reducing by 10–20% after any unexplained hypoglycemic event.

Basal : Bolus Split

PatternSplitWhen to use
Standard50% basal / 50% bolus (divided over 3 meals)Default starting split for full replacement regimens

Bolus-heavy40% basal / 60% bolusPredominantly post-prandial hyperglycemia with reasonable fasting control
Clinical Pearls
  • "Over-basalization" — repeatedly increasing basal insulin without meaningfully lowering fasting glucose while post-prandial control is the actual problem — is a common and correctable prescribing error.

Correction Factor (Insulin Sensitivity Factor) — The 1800/1500 Rules

Insulin classRuleFormula
Rapid-acting analogs (lispro/aspart/glulisine)1800 Rule1800 ÷ TDD = mg/dL drop per 1 unit
Regular insulin1500 Rule1500 ÷ TDD = mg/dL drop per 1 unit
Clinical Pearls
  • Worked example: TDD = 40 units of rapid-acting insulin → correction factor = 1800 ÷ 40 = 45 mg/dL per unit.
  • The individual glucose drop per unit can range widely — roughly 15 to 100+ mg/dL — depending on personal insulin sensitivity, time of day (dawn phenomenon = more resistant), recent exercise (more sensitive for 24–48h afterward), and acute illness (more resistant).
  • Correction dose = (current glucose − target glucose) ÷ correction factor. Always subtract any active insulin-on-board from a recent dose before correcting again, to avoid stacking.
  • A conservative starting correction factor (leaning toward a HIGHER number, meaning less insulin per correction) is safer in frail, elderly, or renally impaired patients until their actual sensitivity is established.

Insulin-to-Carb Ratio — The 500 Rule

FormulaWorked example
500 ÷ TDD = grams of carbohydrate covered by 1 unitTDD = 40 units → 500 ÷ 40 = 12.5 g — roughly 1 unit per 12–13g of carbohydrate
Clinical Pearls
  • Used for flexible mealtime dosing when a patient counts carbohydrates; less relevant for fixed-dose regimens.

Sliding Scale Insulin — Use With Caution

Clinical Pearls
  • Sliding-scale-only regimens (reactive dosing based solely on the current glucose, with no proactive basal coverage) are associated with worse glycemic control and more hyperglycemia/hypoglycemia swings than proactive basal-bolus dosing — most current inpatient guidelines discourage sliding scale as monotherapy.
  • A correction scale layered ON TOP of scheduled basal-bolus dosing (rather than replacing it) is a reasonable and common practice — the distinction is whether it's the ONLY insulin being given.

The kidney both clears insulin from the circulation and contributes ~15-20% of endogenous glucose production via gluconeogenesis. As GFR falls, insulin requirements drop for BOTH reasons — this is one of the most underappreciated, high-stakes dose adjustments in inpatient and outpatient medicine.

Why Insulin Requirements Fall in CKD

Clinical Pearls
  • Decreased renal insulin clearance — the kidney normally degrades a meaningful fraction of circulating insulin; this clearance falls as nephron mass declines.
  • Decreased renal gluconeogenesis — the kidney is a real contributor to endogenous glucose production, and this falls with kidney failure, lowering baseline glucose and insulin need independent of insulin clearance.
  • Reduced appetite/oral intake and dialysis-related glucose shifts (especially with low/normal-glucose dialysate) compound the risk further.
  • Net effect: insulin requirements can fall dramatically and unpredictably — this is a leading, preventable cause of iatrogenic hypoglycemia in CKD/ESRD inpatients.

ESRD / Dialysis-Specific Adjustments

PopulationTypical reduction from pre-renal-failure dose
Type 1 diabetes, Stage 5 CKD/ESRD28–60% dose reduction
Type 2 diabetes, Stage 5 CKD/ESRD~50% dose reduction — and roughly 30% of these patients require NO insulin at all once on dialysis

Clinical Pearls
  • A study reducing dose from 0.5 to 0.25 units/kg/day in patients with eGFR <45 cut the hypoglycemia rate from 30% to 15.8% — a striking demonstration of how much dose reduction alone can do.
  • In a large cohort, insulin use combined with eGFR <30 carried a hazard ratio of 5.62 for serious hypoglycemia compared to patients with eGFR ≥90 not on insulin — among the highest-risk medication/renal-function combinations in general medicine.
  • Always ask about dialysis schedule when adjusting insulin around hemodialysis sessions — the hypoglycemic effect is most pronounced on dialysis days and the day after.

Does Renal Function Affect IV Insulin Too?

Clinical Pearls
  • Yes — overall insulin clearance (hepatic + renal combined) falls with renal impairment regardless of route. Exogenous insulin, whether given IV or subcutaneously, enters the peripheral circulation directly and bypasses the portal-vein first-pass hepatic clearance that the body's own pancreatic insulin gets — this makes ALL exogenous insulin proportionally more dependent on renal clearance than endogenous insulin.
  • The practical safety margin differs sharply by route, though. An IV infusion is reassessed against point-of-care glucose roughly hourly, and because IV insulin's plasma half-life is only ~4-6 minutes, a rate change reaches its new steady state within about 20-30 minutes — renal-mediated 'stacking' gets caught and corrected almost in real time by the titration protocol itself.
  • A fixed subcutaneous dose cannot be taken back once given — the depot continues releasing insulin for hours (3-5h for rapid-acting, up to 42h for degludec) regardless of how the patient actually responds. This is exactly why the CKD dose reductions in the table above matter more for subQ dosing than for IV dosing: with an infusion, the hourly titration is largely doing the renal adjustment in real time, whereas with a subQ dose you get one attempt to set the starting dose correctly before the patient is committed to that depot's full duration of action.
  • Some institutional DKA/HHS protocols still recommend a lower starting IV infusion rate in patients with significant renal or hepatic dysfunction as an extra margin of safety — reasonable, but ongoing hourly titration remains the primary safety mechanism for the IV route, unlike the subQ route where the upfront dose reduction IS the primary safety mechanism.

IV insulin infusion dosing for hyperglycemic crises is weight-based and rate-driven rather than TDD-driven — a fundamentally different calculation from outpatient dosing.

IV Insulin Infusion — Starting Rates

ConditionStarting rateNotes
DKA (severe)0.1 units/kg/hour, fixed-rate IV infusionBolus (0.1 units/kg IV) is optional/controversial — 2024 consensus and many current protocols favor infusion alone without a bolus
DKA (2009 ADA alternative)0.1–0.14 units/kg/hour without a bolusOlder consensus statement; still reflected in many institutional protocols
HHS0.05 units/kg/hour, fixed-rate IV infusionHalf the DKA starting rate — HHS patients are often more insulin-sensitive and at higher risk of over-correction
Mixed DKA/HHS featuresTreat as DKA — 0.1 units/kg/hourSignificant ketonemia/acidosis with hyperosmolality present
Nurse-driven variable-rate protocolMinimum 1 unit/hourEnsures adequate acidosis resolution even on a sliding institutional scale
Clinical Pearls
  • Do NOT start insulin until potassium is confirmed >3.3 mEq/L — insulin drives potassium intracellularly and can precipitate dangerous hypokalemia and arrhythmia if given first.
  • Target glucose decline is roughly 50-75 mg/dL/hour — falling faster increases cerebral edema risk (especially pediatric patients) without added benefit.
  • Once glucose falls below ~200-250 mg/dL, add dextrose-containing fluids and continue the insulin infusion (do not stop it) until the anion gap has closed / ketosis has resolved — the goal is resolving ketoacidosis, not just normalizing glucose.

Transition From IV to Subcutaneous Insulin

StepDetail
Resolution criteriaBeta-hydroxybutyrate <0.6 mmol/L AND (venous pH ≥7.3 OR bicarbonate ≥18 mEq/L) AND patient tolerating oral intake
Timing overlapGive subcutaneous basal insulin 2 hours (some protocols 2-4h) before stopping the IV infusion — NEVER stop the drip first
Estimating subQ TDDBase on: (a) prior home insulin dose, (b) weight-based estimate 0.3-0.6 units/kg/day, or (c) extrapolated infusion rate over the prior 6-8 stable hours × 24
Basal/bolus split at transition50% of calculated TDD as once-daily basal (glargine/detemir), 50% divided as rapid-acting before each meal
Renal/age adjustment at transitionIf eGFR <45 or age >70: reduce basal insulin to a conservative fixed dose (e.g., 0.1 units/kg every 12 hours) rather than the full weight-based estimate
Clinical Pearls
  • The single most dangerous transition error is stopping the IV infusion before subcutaneous insulin has had time to reach therapeutic levels — this causes rebound hyperglycemia and can precipitate recurrent DKA.
  • Some emerging protocols add a low-dose basal insulin analog (0.15-0.3 units/kg) early, alongside the ongoing IV infusion, which may shorten infusion duration and length of stay without increasing hypoglycemia risk.

Subcutaneous-Only Protocols (Mild-Moderate DKA)

StepDose
InitialRapid-acting insulin (e.g., lispro) 0.2 units/kg subcutaneous, then reassess
Continued (glucose still >250 mg/dL)0.1 units/kg subcutaneous every 2 hours until glucose <250 mg/dL
Basal coverageConsider adding glargine 0.3 units/kg subcutaneously (or the patient's usual home dose) alongside the short-acting doses
Clinical Pearls
  • Growing evidence supports subcutaneous-only management for mild-to-moderate DKA (and even some severe cases in select protocols) with outcomes comparable to IV infusion, avoiding an ICU-level drip and central/frequent line access.
  • Type 1 diabetics and patients with pancreatic-insufficiency-related diabetes tend to be more insulin-sensitive — anticipate a lower effective dose and watch more closely for hypoglycemia in this population.

The rare but real scenario: a patient with hyperglycemia and no accessible history, no known home insulin regimen, and no calculator at hand — just a weight, or in the most extreme case, only a glucose number. These are deliberately rough, last-resort methods; use real data whenever it becomes available.

Method 1 — You Know Weight (No Other History)

StepMental math
1. Estimate TDDWeight (kg) × 0.5 units/kg/day — the standard, moderate-insulin-resistance assumption for an insulin-naive adult
2. Adjust for context cluesLooks elderly/frail/cachectic or known renal disease → use ×0.3 instead of ×0.5. Looks obese, on steroids, or has truncal adiposity/acanthosis → consider ×0.7-1.0
3. Derive correction factor1800 ÷ estimated TDD = mg/dL drop per unit of rapid-acting insulin
4. Correction dose(Current glucose − 150 target) ÷ correction factor, rounded DOWN to the nearest whole unit
Clinical Pearls
  • Worked example: unknown 70 kg adult, no other history. Estimated TDD = 70 × 0.5 = 35 units. Correction factor = 1800 ÷ 35 ≈ 51 mg/dL/unit. Glucose is 350 → (350−150) ÷ 51 ≈ 3.9 → give 3-4 units and recheck in 2-3 hours.
  • When genuinely uncertain between two weight multipliers, use the LOWER one — the cost of slight undertreatment (recheck and give more) is far lower than the cost of unrecognized hypoglycemia in a patient you know nothing about.

Method 2 — You Only Have a Glucose Number (No Weight, No History)

SituationRough estimate
Absolute last resort — truly no other dataAssume an average correction factor of ~50 mg/dL per unit of rapid-acting insulin (the commonly cited population-average sensitivity)
Correction dose(Current glucose − 150) ÷ 50, rounded DOWN
Clinical Pearls
  • This is the least reliable method on this page — individual correction factors legitimately range from ~15 to 100+ mg/dL per unit, so a population average can be quite wrong for any given patient.
  • Favor a small, conservative first dose (many clinicians would cap an unknown-patient empiric correction at 4-6 units regardless of the math) and recheck glucose in 2-3 hours rather than fully 'correcting to target' in one dose.
  • As soon as ANY additional data becomes available — a home insulin bottle, a pharmacy record, a family member's knowledge of their regimen, or simply weight — immediately switch to Method 1 or a full TDD-based calculation instead of continuing to rely on this estimate.

General Safety Principles for Unknown-History Dosing

Clinical Pearls
  • Recognize this scenario for what it is: an emergency estimate, not a substitute for individualized dosing. Transition to real data (weight-based TDD, home regimen, renal function) as soon as it's available.
  • Hypoglycemia in a patient with no history is far more dangerous than transient hyperglycemia, since you have no way to anticipate an unusually sensitive patient (renal failure, adrenal insufficiency, sepsis, malnutrition, alcohol use, elderly/frail status all increase sensitivity).
  • When in doubt, treat conservatively, recheck frequently, and involve additional history-gathering (family, pharmacy, prior records, medical alert jewelry) as a parallel priority alongside acute treatment.

01

Lactation-Related Breast Conditions

The 2022 ABM protocol reversed decades of "heat, massage, pump more" teaching. Most providers — and most patients — are still working from the old script.

The paradigm shift

ABM Clinical Protocol #36 (revised 2022) reframes mastitis as a spectrum — engorgement → ductal narrowing/inflammatory mastitis → bacterial mastitis → phlegmon/abscess — rather than separate conditions, driven by ductal narrowing and stromal edema, often in the context of hyperlactation (oversupply). The old causal model (milk stasis/"clogged ducts" from incomplete emptying) is not well supported by current evidence, which is why the entire treatment approach changed.

What Changed, Old vs. New

Old adviceCurrent guidance
Heat/warm compressesIce/cold compresses — a warm-shower-plus-antipyretic approach showed no benefit in trial data
Aggressive massage to "break up the clog"Avoid deep massage entirely — it causes tissue trauma and can precipitate phlegmon/abscess. If any massage is used, gentle lymphatic-drainage-style sweeping only
Pump more / empty the breast completelyFeed on normal demand; avoid overstimulation/oversupply-driving behavior — the goal is reducing hyperlactation, not maximizing drainage
Sterilize pump parts to fight "yeast/candida mastitis""Candida mastitis" has no scientific support as a diagnosis; routine sterilization isn't recommended
Interrupt breastfeeding, treat as contagiousBacterial mastitis is not contagious to the infant and does not require interrupting breastfeeding
Castor oil / topical products / saline soaksAvoid — no benefit shown, and combined with massage can actively cause tissue damage
Calibrate your confidence appropriately

Not every element of the 2022 protocol is equally strong evidence — published commentary has pointed out that several specific recommendations (lymphatic drainage massage technique, therapeutic ultrasound, oral lecithin) rest on low-quality evidence (level 3, grade C) themselves, and the "hyperlactation/dysbiosis" model is a reasonable but not fully proven theoretical framework. Treat this as current best guidance that reversed clearly wrong old teaching, not as a fully settled, high-certainty evidence base in every detail.

Management by Stage

1
Engorgement (typically days 3–5 postpartum, later with cesarean): supportive — well-fitting supportive (not tight) bra, feed on demand, gentle hand expression for comfort only (not to fully empty), ice, alternating ibuprofen and acetaminophen.
2
Ductal narrowing / inflammatory mastitis: same supportive measures; reduce any oversupply-driving behaviors (excessive pumping "just in case"); NSAIDs for inflammation. Most cases resolve without antibiotics at this stage.
3
Bacterial mastitis (fever, systemic symptoms, or failure to improve in 24–48h): antibiotics targeting S. aureus — dicloxacillin or cephalexin first-line; clindamycin or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole if MRSA risk/confirmed. Continue breastfeeding/pumping on the affected side throughout.
4
Phlegmon or abscess: ultrasound to distinguish (phlegmon = inflammatory mass without discrete fluid collection, generally continues on antibiotics; abscess = discrete fluid collection requiring drainage). Needle aspiration (often ultrasound-guided, sometimes serial) is preferred over incision and drainage when feasible, to preserve lactation and reduce scarring; I&D reserved for larger or aspiration-refractory abscesses.

Practical Latch & Positioning — Genuine Prevention

Unlike massage/heat/pumping frequency, proper latch and positioning have a real, mechanistic prevention rationale — a poor latch causes ineffective, asymmetric drainage of the breast and nipple trauma, both plausible contributors to the ductal narrowing/inflammation end of the spectrum.

  • Deep latch: baby's mouth should cover a large portion of the areola, not just the nipple; lips flanged outward, chin pressed into the breast, nose free.
  • Vary positions across feeds (cradle, football/clutch, side-lying, laid-back) — this changes which ducts are most effectively drained and reduces repetitive strain on the same area of tissue.
  • Address pain at the source — persistent nipple pain beyond the first several days is a signal to reassess latch (or evaluate for infant tongue-tie) rather than something to simply tolerate.
  • Avoid an overly rigid schedule or reflexive "pump after every feed" habit in an otherwise well-supplied patient — this is precisely the oversupply-driving pattern the 2022 protocol identifies as a risk factor.

Nipple Discharge & Benign Breast Disease

The discharge pattern — not the presence of discharge itself — is what should drive the workup.

Nipple Discharge — Physiologic vs. Pathologic

FeaturePhysiologicPathologic — warrants workup
LateralityBilateralUnilateral
Number of ductsMultipleSingle duct
OnsetExpressed (requires manipulation)Spontaneous
CharacterMilky, green, or dark — non-bloodyBloody or clear/serous

Pathologic-pattern discharge warrants diagnostic mammography (if age-appropriate) and ultrasound, with referral for ductogram or surgical duct excision if imaging doesn't explain it — the leading causes are intraductal papilloma (most common cause of bloody discharge) and, less commonly, ductal carcinoma in situ. Galactorrhea (bilateral, milky, often outside pregnancy/lactation) has a different differential entirely — prolactinoma, hypothyroidism, and dopamine-antagonist medications (antipsychotics, metoclopramide) are the classic causes to screen for with a prolactin and TSH.

Common Benign Breast Findings

  • Fibroadenoma: well-circumscribed, mobile, rubbery mass, most common in patients under 30; can be observed if imaging-confirmed and stable, or excised if enlarging, symptomatic, or patient preference.
  • Simple cyst: anechoic, well-circumscribed on ultrasound — no further workup needed if truly simple and asymptomatic. Aspiration for symptomatic/painful cysts; any complex or complicated-appearing cyst on imaging needs further characterization rather than assumption of benignity.
  • Fibrocystic changes: the most common benign breast finding overall — diffuse nodularity/lumpiness, often cyclic and hormonally related. A clinical diagnosis of exclusion once a discrete, imaging-suspicious mass has been ruled out.
  • Galactocele: milk-filled cyst, specific to the lactating breast — can become infected if not resolved (Module 1 cross-reference), typically managed with aspiration if symptomatic.

Mastalgia (Breast Pain)

Cyclic mastalgia (tied to the menstrual cycle, typically premenstrual, often bilateral) is common and rarely indicates serious pathology — first-line management is a well-fitting supportive bra, and evening primrose oil has weak evidence at best (consistent with its similarly weak track record in Vol. 5's obstetric applications). Non-cyclic mastalgia (unrelated to cycle, often unilateral/focal) has a broader differential (costochondritis, trauma, and — though less common than patients fear — a focal breast lesion) and deserves a more complete exam and imaging workup if focal and persistent.

Medication Safety in Lactation & Milk Supply

Full pharmacologic depth for the medications you're actually reaching for on shift — plus what genuinely helps low supply and what doesn't.

ED-Relevant Medication Safety in Breastfeeding

Class/agentLactation safety
Acetaminophen, ibuprofenPreferred analgesics — minimal transfer, extensive safety data
Codeine, tramadolAvoid. Both are metabolized via CYP2D6 to active opioid metabolites; ultra-rapid metabolizer mothers can produce dangerously high infant opioid exposure — codeine carries an FDA boxed warning after documented neonatal deaths. Use a different analgesic class entirely rather than trying to risk-stratify by "just a short course."
Morphine, oxycodone (short course, lowest effective dose)Generally compatible short-term; monitor infant for sedation/poor feeding; avoid prolonged or high-dose use
Penicillins, cephalosporins, most macrolidesCompatible — first-line antibiotic choices in a lactating patient
FluoroquinolonesGenerally considered compatible short-term per current data, though historically avoided — reasonable to use when clinically indicated and no equally effective alternative exists
Sulfonamides (TMP-SMX)Generally compatible in a healthy, full-term infant beyond the immediate neonatal period; avoid in a G6PD-deficient or ill/premature infant (hemolysis/kernicterus risk)
BenzodiazepinesOccasional/short-term use is generally compatible; avoid regular or high-dose use given sedation/poor-feeding risk in the infant
Iodinated & gadolinium contrastCompatible — no need to interrupt breastfeeding after either (Vol. 1, Module 5 parallel)
SSRIs (sertraline preferred)Compatible — see Vol. 3, Module 6 for full perinatal mental health depth
The one true red flag in this table

Codeine and tramadol are the two agents where "compatible with breastfeeding" teaching from a decade ago is now flatly wrong for some patients — this is a genuine practice-changing correction, not a minor nuance, given the documented neonatal fatality that prompted the FDA warning.

For anything not covered above, LactMed (NIH) and the InfantRisk Center are the fastest, most current point-of-care references — worth bookmarking rather than relying on memory for less common agents.

Low Milk Supply

  • Most common cause: ineffective removal, not true glandular insufficiency — poor latch, infrequent feeding/pumping, or early supplementation reducing demand are the leading fixable causes.
  • Real anatomic/physiologic causes: insufficient glandular tissue, prior breast surgery affecting ductal continuity, retained placental fragment (delays lactogenesis II — a genuinely dangerous-to-miss postpartum hemorrhage-adjacent cause), Sheehan syndrome, and uncontrolled hypothyroidism.
  • Galactagogues: domperidone and metoclopramide have the best evidence among pharmacologic options, but domperidone isn't FDA-approved for this indication in the U.S. (cardiac arrhythmia signal at high doses) and metoclopramide carries its own tardive dyskinesia risk with prolonged use — neither is a first-line, low-risk fix. Frequent, effective milk removal remains the highest-yield intervention before reaching for a galactagogue.
  • Herbal galactagogues (fenugreek, etc.) have weak, inconsistent evidence — reasonable to mention neutrally as low-risk/low-certainty-benefit, consistent with Vol. 5's general herbal evidence framework.
04

Chronic Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

Vol. 6 covered the acute hemorrhage response. This is the outpatient algorithm for the patient who's been bleeding heavily for months, not hours.

Classify by PALM-COEIN

Structural (PALM: Polyp, Adenomyosis, Leiomyoma, Malignancy/hyperplasia) causes are more often identified by imaging/biopsy; nonstructural (COEIN: Coagulopathy, Ovulatory dysfunction, Endometrial, Iatrogenic, Not otherwise classified) causes are more often identified by history and labs. Every reproductive-age patient gets a pregnancy test regardless of story.

Baseline Workup

  • CBC (anemia severity), TSH, pregnancy test always.
  • Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal) as first-line imaging for structural causes.
  • Endometrial biopsy for anyone ≥45, or younger with risk factors for endometrial hyperplasia/cancer (obesity, PCOS/chronic anovulation, tamoxifen use, Lynch syndrome per Vol. 7).
  • Coagulation workup (including von Willebrand screen) in adolescents with heavy bleeding since menarche, or any patient with a personal/family bleeding history (Vol. 6, Module 5 cross-reference).

Management Ladder

1
Levonorgestrel IUD — the single most effective medical option for heavy menstrual bleeding, with efficacy approaching that of endometrial ablation in comparative trials, plus contraceptive benefit.
2
Combined hormonal contraceptives (cyclic or continuous) or oral progestins — reasonable first-line alternatives, particularly when a patient wants a non-IUD option.
3
Tranexamic acid (cyclic, non-hormonal) — a good option for patients who want to avoid hormones entirely or who have a contraindication to them.
4
NSAIDs — modest reduction in blood loss, reasonable adjunct rather than a standalone solution for genuinely heavy bleeding.
5
Procedural/surgical (endometrial ablation, myomectomy/UAE if fibroid-driven per Module 7, hysterectomy) for medical management failure or patient preference for a definitive solution.

PCOS

Per the 2023 International Evidence-Based Guideline — the most current global consensus, with a genuinely expanded view of what PCOS actually is beyond "irregular periods and cysts."

Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria — 2 of 3 Required (Adults)

1
Ovulatory/menstrual dysfunction
2
Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism
3
Polycystic ovarian morphology (ultrasound, or AMH as an alternative in adults)

The 2023 update formally incorporated AMH as an alternative to ultrasound for assessing polycystic ovarian morphology in adults (not adolescents, where PCOM remains too nonspecific to use diagnostically). Four phenotypes result depending on which two-of-three criteria are met — phenotype A (all three present) carries the most pronounced metabolic profile.

Beyond the Reproductive Axis

The 2023 guideline explicitly broadens what PCOS management should screen for: metabolic risk (insulin resistance, dyslipidemia), cardiovascular risk factors, obstructive sleep apnea, and — heavily emphasized as previously under-recognized — a very high prevalence of anxiety and depression. Screen for mental health at diagnosis and periodically thereafter based on risk factors and life stage, with explicit screening for self-harm risk when indicated (Vol. 3, Module 6 principles apply directly here).

Management — Full Depth

  • Lifestyle intervention remains foundational — no single diet/exercise regimen outperforms others; weight stigma reduction and permission-based weighing are explicitly emphasized in the 2023 guideline's approach.
  • Combined oral contraceptives — first-line pharmacologic treatment for menstrual irregularity and hyperandrogenism; no specific formulation preferred, favoring lower-dose, better-tolerated options.
  • Metformin — recommended more broadly than before (not just for confirmed insulin resistance), for adults with BMI ≥25 to help with weight/metabolic management; more limited evidence in adults with lower BMI and in adolescents. Not routinely recommended in pregnancy.
  • Anti-androgens (spironolactone, finasteride, flutamide) — for hirsutism/acne when COCs alone are insufficient or contraindicated; all require reliable contraception given teratogenicity.
  • Inositol — shows some promise for insulin sensitivity/ovulatory function but has more limited clinical benefit than metformin per the current guideline — reasonable adjunct, not a metformin replacement.
  • Fertility management: letrozole is preferred first-line ovulation induction (Vol. 4, Module 2 detail), with clomiphene ± metformin as alternatives, and gonadotropins/ovarian surgery reserved for second-line.
  • Bariatric/metabolic surgery and anti-obesity medications may be considered per general population guidelines when indicated — PCOS itself doesn't change the standard eligibility framework.

Endometriosis

The single biggest recent shift: you no longer need a laparoscope to start treating it.

Laparoscopy is no longer required for diagnosis Tier A

Current position statements are explicit: laparoscopy should no longer be performed with the sole purpose of diagnosing endometriosis. A clinical diagnosis — severe dysmenorrhea, non-menstrual pelvic pain, dyspareunia, or infertility not otherwise explained, especially in adolescents/young women with pain that interferes with daily activities and doesn't respond to first-line analgesics — is sufficient to justify starting empiric hormonal treatment without surgical confirmation first. This meaningfully shortens time-to-treatment for a condition notorious for years-long diagnostic delay.

Imaging

Transvaginal ultrasound is reasonable first-line imaging to evaluate for endometrioma or rule out other causes of pelvic pain; MRI is preferred when deep infiltrating endometriosis is suspected (e.g., suspected bowel/bladder involvement). Laparoscopy with biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic gold standard when surgery is being pursued anyway (e.g., for a mass or fertility-related indication) — the change is that it's no longer a mandatory first step purely to confirm the diagnosis before treating.

Management Ladder

1
NSAIDs plus combined hormonal contraceptives or continuous progestins — first-line, aiming to reduce or eliminate menstrual bleeding frequency entirely rather than just treating pain reactively.
2
Oral GnRH antagonists (elagolix, or relugolix combination therapy with add-back estradiol/norethindrone) — newer than injectable GnRH agonists, with a major practical advantage: no initial flare-up (the testosterone/estrogen surge injectable agonists cause before suppression kicks in), dose-dependent hypoestrogenic effect allows titrating between efficacy and side effects, and they don't require monthly injections. They do not suppress ovulation, so contraception counseling still applies.
3
Injectable GnRH agonists (leuprolide) with add-back therapy — effective but with the initial flare and injection burden the newer oral antagonists were designed to avoid.
4
Surgical excision (laparoscopic) — for medical management failure, suspected deep infiltrating disease, an endometrioma, or a fertility-focused treatment plan.

Fertility preservation counseling (oocyte or ovarian tissue cryopreservation, Vol. 4 parallel) is a reasonable discussion point in patients with endometrioma or planned surgery who may want to preserve future fertility options, given surgery itself can reduce ovarian reserve.

Uterine Fibroids

Classification by location — not just size — is what actually determines the treatment options available.

FIGO Classification — Location Drives Everything

TypeLocationClinical relevance
0–2Submucosal (including intracavitary)Most likely to cause heavy bleeding; hysteroscopic resection is often feasible for these specifically
3–5IntramuralBulk symptoms, variable bleeding impact depending on cavity distortion
6–7Subserosal (including pedunculated)More likely to cause bulk/pressure symptoms than bleeding; pedunculated types carry a torsion risk
8Other (cervical, parasitic)Location-specific management

Medical Management

  • Oral GnRH antagonists (elagolix, relugolix combinations) — FDA-approved specifically for fibroid-related heavy menstrual bleeding, sharing the same no-flare, oral-dosing advantages described in Module 6 for endometriosis.
  • Injectable leuprolide — FDA-approved specifically for preoperative reduction of fibroid size/correction of anemia before surgery, limited to a 3-month course for this indication.
  • Off-label options: combined hormonal contraceptives, levonorgestrel IUD, and tranexamic acid all reduce fibroid-related bleeding, mirroring the Module 4 chronic AUB ladder.

Procedural & Surgical Options

OptionBest suited for
Hysteroscopic myomectomySubmucosal (Type 0–2) fibroids specifically — fertility-preserving
Laparoscopic/open myomectomyIntramural/subserosal fibroids in patients desiring fertility preservation
Uterine artery embolization (UAE)Symptomatic fibroids in patients who want to avoid surgery and don't require future fertility (data on fertility outcomes post-UAE is more limited than post-myomectomy)
MRI-guided focused ultrasound surgerySelected patients as a non-invasive option — not appropriate for very large or numerous fibroids
Endometrial ablationBleeding-predominant symptoms without a strong bulk component, and no future fertility desired
HysterectomyDefinitive treatment when childbearing is complete and other options have failed or aren't desired
A shared limitation across the minimally invasive options

UAE, ablation, and focused ultrasound are all generally not recommended for very large fibroids, large numbers of fibroids, or submucosal/pedunculated subserosal fibroids — the latter due to a real risk of fibroid necrosis and expulsion complications. Patient selection matters as much as the procedure choice itself.

Chronic Vulvar Dermatoses

The condition most likely to be under-treated out of unfamiliarity, and the one where an old, disproven treatment (topical testosterone) still occasionally surfaces in practice.

Lichen Sclerosus

Chronic inflammatory dermatosis of the anogenital skin — classically ivory-white, thin, patchy plaques of the labia/perineum/perianal area, causing pruritus, pain, and progressive architectural scarring if untreated. Most common in postmenopausal women but occurs at any age, including prepubertally.

Topical testosterone — a genuinely outdated treatment

Testosterone was the historical mainstay of treatment and is no longer used — RCTs found it ineffective, with an unacceptable side-effect profile. If you encounter a patient still using it, or older documentation recommending it, this is worth actively correcting.

  • First-line: high-potency topical corticosteroid (clobetasol propionate ointment) — a typical induction regimen is nightly for up to 3 months, then transitioning to a maintenance regimen (clobetasol 2–3×/week, or transition to a mid-potency steroid nightly).
  • Long-term maintenance steroid use is generally safe — vulvar skin thinning and scarring result from the underlying disease itself, not from appropriate steroid use, which is a genuine and important reassurance point for patients (and providers) worried about "steroid atrophy."
  • Biopsy is not always required for a classic presentation, but is warranted for persistent pain, firm nodules, erosions/ulcers that don't resolve, or any suspicion of malignancy — lichen sclerosus carries a real, elevated risk of vulvar squamous cell carcinoma, which is the main reason for long-term follow-up.
  • Refractory disease: topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus) as second-line; consider alternative diagnoses first (contact dermatitis, superimposed candida/HSV/bacterial infection, hypoestrogenism, steroid-induced rebound dermatitis, or coexisting vulvodynia) before assuming the lichen sclerosus itself is simply refractory.
  • Follow-up: every 3–6 months for the first 2 years, then at least annually indefinitely, given both the recurrence tendency and the malignancy surveillance rationale.

Vulvodynia

Chronic vulvar pain (≥3 months) without a clearly identifiable cause on exam — a diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out infection, dermatoses (including lichen sclerosus above), and other identifiable pathology.

  • Localized provoked vulvodynia (pain triggered by touch/pressure, classically at the vestibule — "provoked vestibulodynia") is the most common subtype, often identified with a cotton-swab test mapping specific points of tenderness.
  • Generalized/unprovoked vulvodynia presents as more diffuse, spontaneous burning pain.
  • Management is multimodal: pelvic floor physical therapy (addressing the frequently co-occurring pelvic floor hypertonicity) has some of the better evidence in this space; topical lidocaine for provoked pain; tricyclic antidepressants or gabapentin for centrally-mediated/neuropathic-pattern pain; vestibulectomy is reserved for refractory, well-localized provoked vestibulodynia after conservative measures fail.
  • Validate the diagnosis explicitly with patients — vulvodynia is frequently dismissed or misattributed to anxiety/psychological causes before a correct diagnosis is reached, and that delay itself is a documented source of patient distress independent of the pain itself.

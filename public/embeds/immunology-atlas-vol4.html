01 · Neuromuscular

Myasthenic Crisis

The autoimmune emergency where the pulse ox lies to you, and where the wrong reflex (treat the numbers, not the trend) costs the most time.

“Sats are 96% and the ABG looks fine — so this MG patient with worsening weakness isn't in trouble yet, right?”

Myasthenia gravis is caused by autoantibodies against the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor (or, less commonly, MuSK) at the neuromuscular junction, progressively impairing signal transmission with fatigable weakness. Myasthenic crisis is respiratory failure from that same weakness affecting the diaphragm and accessory muscles — and it is a hypercarbic, neuromuscular pump failure, not a primary oxygenation problem. That distinction is the single most important thing to internalize: SpO₂ and even a normal ABG are late, unreliable indicators here — by the time hypoxemia or hypercapnia shows up, the patient has already lost significant respiratory reserve.

What to trend instead

MeasureNormalConcerningConsider intubation
Forced vital capacity (FVC)≥60 mL/kgTrending down<20 mL/kg (“20/30/40 rule”)

Check these serially (roughly every 2 hours) and trust the trend over any single value. Intubate electively once the numbers cross the threshold above — waiting for hypoxemia or hypercarbia to develop means waiting for a late, ominous sign in a disease process that can progress rapidly once respiratory muscles fatigue.

Airway management specifics

Myasthenic patients are classically resistant to succinylcholine (higher/unpredictable dose requirements due to receptor pathology) and simultaneously more sensitive to non-depolarizing agents (rocuronium, vecuronium) — the practical takeaway is to use a non-depolarizing agent at a reduced dose rather than leaning on succinylcholine for rapid sequence intubation.

Treatment sequencing — the order matters

StepDetail
Identify and treat the triggerInfection is the most common precipitant; also consider a recently added offending drug or rapid immunosuppressant taper
Hold acetylcholinesterase inhibitors if intubatedPyridostigmine/neostigmine increase secretions and offer no benefit to the underlying crisis once mechanically ventilated
Start rapid therapy: PLEX or IVIGComparable efficacy overall; some recent comparative data favor plasmapheresis for extubation at 2 weeks and functional outcome, at the cost of a higher complication rate. IVIG is typically dosed 2 g/kg divided over 5 days
Corticosteroids — often deliberately delayedRoughly 50% of patients experience transient worsening of weakness 5–10 days after starting steroids — many protocols defer steroid initiation until after PLEX/IVIG has been started and the patient is stabilized, rather than starting everything simultaneously
Myth: “start steroids right away — more immunosuppression started sooner is always better.” In myasthenic crisis specifically, early high-dose steroids can transiently worsen weakness before they help, which is exactly why they're often sequenced after the rapid-acting therapy rather than alongside it.
Verify before use: PLEX vs IVIG comparative outcome data is still evolving and based on modest cohort sizes — confirm current neurology/institutional preference; steroid timing practice also varies by institution.
02 · Systemic Lupus

Lupus Flare & Nephritis

The question that has to be answered before any other decision gets made: is this the disease, or is this an infection wearing the disease's face?

“This lupus patient on mycophenolate has a fever and feels worse all over — flare or infection, and how do I actually tell?”

SLE can flare in nearly any organ, but the ED-relevant emergencies cluster around a handful of patterns: lupus nephritis (rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis), CNS lupus (seizure, psychosis, “lupus cerebritis”), serositis (pericarditis that can progress to tamponade, pleuritis), pulmonary involvement (pneumonitis, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage), and severe hematologic manifestations (deep cytopenias, thrombotic microangiopathy). The single hardest and most consequential judgment call layered on top of all of these: most SLE patients are chronically immunosuppressed, so a new fever or new organ dysfunction is genuinely ambiguous between flare and infection — and treating an infection as a flare (more immunosuppression) is a well-documented way to kill a patient.

Flare vs. infection — imperfect but useful discriminators

Favors flareFavors infection
Low complement (C3/C4) with rising anti-dsDNA titerComplement typically less affected acutely (though can be depressed at baseline)
Multi-system pattern consistent with the patient's known lupus phenotypeLocalizing signs/symptoms (cough, dysuria, focal pain) pointing to a single source
Gradual symptom evolution over days matching prior flare patternRigors, very high fever, rapid deterioration
Active urinary sediment (RBC casts) without clear infectious sourceElevated procalcitonin (used cautiously — not perfectly discriminating in SLE)
Neither column is a reliable rule-out on its own. This is a genuinely difficult distinction even for rheumatology, and the two can coexist (infection triggering a flare). The safer default in an ambiguous, sick-looking patient is to treat empirically for infection while pursuing the flare workup, rather than assuming flare and escalating immunosuppression first.

Lupus nephritis

Presents with hematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, edema, and a rising creatinine; active urinary sediment (dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts) supports glomerular inflammation over another cause of kidney injury. Definitive classification requires renal biopsy, which won't happen in the ED — the ED role is recognition, ruling out competing/treatable causes of AKI, and prompt nephrology/rheumatology involvement, since induction therapy (high-dose steroids plus cyclophosphamide or mycophenolate) is a specialty-directed decision.

Practical ED synthesis: stabilize, treat any identified infection aggressively regardless of flare suspicion, pulse corticosteroids are reasonable empirically for a clearly life- or organ-threatening flare (severe cerebritis, tamponade, diffuse alveolar hemorrhage) after infection has been reasonably excluded or is being treated in parallel, and involve rheumatology/nephrology early rather than trying to fully characterize the flare in the ED.
Verify before use: specific induction regimens (cyclophosphamide vs. mycophenolate, steroid dosing/route) are rheumatology-directed decisions with evolving evidence — this section is oriented toward ED recognition and stabilization, not induction protocol selection.
03 · Vasculitis

Pulmonary-Renal Syndrome: ANCA Vasculitis vs. Goodpasture

Two different antibodies producing the same terrifying combination — hemoptysis plus a rapidly rising creatinine — with meaningfully different treatment, especially now.

“Hemoptysis and a creatinine that's doubled in three days — is this ANCA vasculitis or Goodpasture, and does it change what I do tonight?”

Figure 2. Both conditions converge on the same dangerous presentation; the confirmatory antibody test and, increasingly, the treatment approach diverge.
ANCA-associated vasculitis (GPA / MPA / EGPA)Goodpasture (anti-GBM disease)

Other cluesUpper airway involvement (sinusitis, saddle-nose, subglottic stenosis) in GPA; asthma + eosinophilia in EGPA; palpable purpura, mononeuritis multiplexIsolated pulmonary-renal presentation; smoking/hydrocarbon exposure can trigger the pulmonary component

The teaching-point contrast worth remembering: these two pulmonary-renal syndromes used to be managed almost identically with routine plasma exchange for both. That's no longer accurate for ANCA vasculitis — plasma exchange remains standard for Goodpasture, but the evidence base for ANCA vasculitis has moved. Treating them as interchangeable is now a real (and easy) mistake.

How the ANCA vasculitis evidence actually moved

Figure 3. From routine plasma exchange to a much more selective role, over roughly two decades of trial data.
Walsh M, et al. Plasma Exchange and Glucocorticoids in Severe ANCA-Associated Vasculitis. N Engl J Med. 2020;382(7):622–631 (PEXIVAS).

The largest-ever vasculitis trial (704 patients, severe AAV with renal involvement and/or diffuse pulmonary hemorrhage) found that adding plasma exchange to standard induction immunosuppression did not reduce the composite outcome of death or end-stage kidney disease. A separate arm of the same trial found a reduced-dose glucocorticoid regimen was non-inferior to standard dosing, with fewer serious infections — which is why reduced-dose steroids, not just the plasma exchange finding, changed practice from this trial. Post-hoc analysis did find plasma exchange produced faster early eGFR improvement, which has kept a narrower, more individualized role for plasma exchange alive in patients with the most severe renal presentations and minimal chronic damage on biopsy.

Verify before use: current society guidance (KDIGO, ACR/EULAR) on the specific role of plasma exchange for ANCA vasculitis with severe renal involvement continues to be refined post-PEXIVAS — confirm current recommendation with nephrology/rheumatology before assuming it's categorically excluded.
04 · Thrombotic Emergency

Catastrophic Antiphospholipid Syndrome (CAPS)

A thrombotic storm that looks exactly like sepsis with DIC — the diagnosis you have to think of, because nothing about the initial picture points to it specifically.

“Multi-organ failure, thrombocytopenia, and a lactate that keeps climbing despite broad-spectrum antibiotics and fluids — what am I missing?”

CAPS is the rare, most severe end of the antiphospholipid syndrome spectrum — widespread small-vessel thrombosis causing multi-organ failure that develops over days rather than the more indolent large-vessel thrombosis of “ordinary” APS. By definition, it involves three or more organ systems within about a week, and the clinical picture (shock, multi-organ dysfunction, thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolysis) is easily and frequently mistaken for sepsis with DIC or thrombotic microangiopathy — which is exactly why it's under-recognized and why recognition, not confirmatory testing, is the rate-limiting step.

Typical triggerDiagnostic approach
Infection (most common)Antiphospholipid antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-β2-glycoprotein I) — triple positivity carries the highest risk
SurgeryHistopathologic confirmation of small-vessel thrombosis when a biopsy is feasible — often it isn't, given how sick these patients are
Anticoagulation withdrawal/subtherapeutic dosing“Definite” vs. “probable” CAPS classification criteria exist, but treatment should not wait for that classification to be finalized
Pregnancy, malignancy
Don't wait for the antibody panel to result. In a patient with rapid multi-organ small-vessel thrombosis and a clinical picture that doesn't fit cleanly as sepsis/DIC — especially with a known or newly suspected autoimmune background — empiric triple therapy is started on clinical suspicion, with confirmatory testing sent but not awaited.

Triple therapy — and why the number of components matters

Figure 4. CAPS Registry mortality by treatment completeness — the gap between partial and full triple therapy is large.
ComponentDetail
AnticoagulationTherapeutic heparin preferred initially — treats and helps prevent progression of the underlying thrombotic process
High-dose glucocorticoidsPulse methylprednisolone in the acute setting
Plasma exchange and/or IVIGBoth have supporting registry data; plasma exchange and IVIG are not mutually exclusive and are sometimes combined in refractory cases
International CAPS Registry (Cervera R, et al., multiple publications)Mortality was 28.6% with full triple therapy, 41.1% with partial combinations of these treatments, and 75% when none of the three components were used. Historical mortality of 50%+ in early cohorts (pre-2000) has fallen to roughly 25–33% in more recent series, attributed largely to more consistent triple therapy use.

For triple-therapy-refractory disease, rituximab and eculizumab (complement C5 blockade) have case-report and small-series support, reflecting the role of complement activation in CAPS pathophysiology — these are specialist (hematology/rheumatology) decisions, not ED-initiated therapies.

Verify before use: mortality figures come from registry (observational) data, not randomized trials, and reflect a heterogeneous, rare patient population — treat as a strong directional signal for triple therapy rather than a precise individual-patient prognosis.
05 · Practical Synthesis

The Common Thread

Five different diseases, one recurring bedside lesson.

Practical synthesis

Across all five conditions in this volume, the same pattern repeats: the objective trend (serial NIF/FVC, complement/anti-dsDNA trajectory, rising creatinine, antibody titers) matters more than any single snapshot value, and in several of these diseases (myasthenic crisis, CAPS) the standard vital-sign reassurance — a normal SpO₂, a not-yet-critical lactate — actively lags the true severity of illness. When an autoimmune emergency is on the differential, treat clinical trajectory and specialist involvement as more informative than the first set of numbers, and don't let a reassuring-looking single data point delay escalation.

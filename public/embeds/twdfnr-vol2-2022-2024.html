01 · Endocrine / Pharmacy

Routinely Holding Metformin in the Hospital

Cohen DA, Ricotta DN, Parikh PD. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routinely holding metformin in the hospital. J Hosp Med. 2022;17(3):207–210. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3644

Original rationale

Fear of metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA) with any acute illness, renal impairment, hypoxia, or hemodynamic instability — a legacy of the biguanide class, whose earlier member phenformin was pulled from the market for exactly this reason.

Why it persists

"Hold home meds on admission" is a blanket order-set default. Metformin gets swept up automatically regardless of admission severity, and it's rarely restarted proactively before discharge.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Metformin is pharmacologically distinct from phenformin, with a far lower lactic-acidosis signal — large reviews (including a Cochrane review) have not found a significantly increased risk of lactic acidosis with metformin across a broad range of kidney function.
  • Reflexively holding metformin drives rebound hyperglycemia and increased reliance on inpatient sliding-scale insulin as monotherapy — itself a separate, previously-flagged TWDFNR entry with its own hypoglycemia risk.
  • Metformin is frequently never restarted at discharge due to medication reconciliation gaps, leaving patients with worse long-term glycemic control than before admission.

When holding is still appropriate

Significant AKI, hemodynamic instability or shock, hypoxia, sepsis, advanced liver disease, or around contrast-enhanced procedures in patients with baseline renal impairment — hold selectively based on actual risk factors, not as a blanket admission default.

Bottom line

Don't hold metformin reflexively in stable, well-perfused patients with normal renal function. Reserve holding for patients with genuine risk factors for lactic acidosis, and build restarting it into discharge planning.

02 · Medical Education

Toxic Quizzing in Medical Education

Kinnear B, DeCoursey B, Caya T, Baez J, Warm EJ. Things We Do for No Reason™: Toxic quizzing in medical education. J Hosp Med. 2022;17(6):481–484. doi: 10.1002/jhm.12846
Cited by the American College of Physicians as one of the most important articles of 2022.

Original rationale

"Pimping" — rapid-fire cold-call quizzing of trainees — was framed as Socratic teaching, believed to sharpen recall and prepare trainees for high-stakes, high-stress clinical decisions.

Why it persists

Attendings who were pimped as trainees often believe it worked on them: in a 2019 survey, 45% (56/125) of medicine attendings agreed that being pimped by their own teachers helped their learning. Faculty rarely get development on alternatives.

What the evidence actually shows

  • True Socratic questioning builds toward understanding; toxic quizzing under threat of public humiliation is a different mechanism entirely, despite the shared label.
  • The commonly-invoked Yerkes–Dodson law is itself frequently misapplied here — performance rises with stress only up to a point, after which it drops; toxic quizzing routinely pushes learners past that inflection point rather than optimizing it.
  • Overreliance on recall-under-threat suppresses meaningful follow-up questions and intellectual dialogue — the opposite of the stated educational goal.
  • Multiple qualitative and survey studies document real evidence of harm (stress, humiliation, disengagement), not merely trainee discomfort.

What still works

Well-calibrated, supportive questioning that scaffolds toward a concept — the problem is the humiliation/threat framing, not questioning trainees per se.

Bottom line

Replace recall-under-threat with supportive, scaffolded questioning calibrated to the learner's level — examine your own teaching intent before defaulting to how you were taught.

03 · Critical Care / Sepsis

Using Lactate as Our Resuscitation Guide in Sepsis

Juffs B, Russo E. Things We Do for No Reason™: Using lactate as our resuscitation guide in sepsis. J Hosp Med. 2024;19:133–135. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13168
Cross-references your Sepsis Atlas — this pairs directly with the SSC 2026-anchored resuscitation volumes.

Original rationale

Serial lactate is cheap, fast, and trendable, and lactate normalization/clearance was written into Surviving Sepsis Campaign resuscitation targets and the SEP-1 core measure.

Why it persists

It's baked into sepsis order sets and quality metrics — a single number is simpler to chase than a composite clinical assessment.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Lactate is nonspecific — it rises with beta-agonist use, seizures, liver dysfunction, certain malignancies, and even metformin itself, not only with tissue hypoperfusion (the "lacto-bolo reflex" critique, Spiegel et al., J Thorac Dis 2020).
  • The ANDROMEDA-SHOCK trial found that targeting capillary refill time produced similar or better outcomes than lactate-targeted resuscitation, with less fluid administered and less organ dysfunction at 72 hours in the CRT arm.
  • Chasing a single elevated lactate value with repeated fluid boluses can drive unnecessary volume administration in patients who are already adequately resuscitated but have a persistently elevated lactate for a non-hypoperfusion reason.

Where lactate still helps

As an initial severity marker and for trending overall trajectory — the issue is treating it as the sole, isolated trigger for additional fluid boluses.

Bottom line

Don't chase a single lactate number with repeated fluid boluses. Use it alongside capillary refill, mottling, and other perfusion markers rather than as an isolated resuscitation endpoint.

04 · Toxicology / Cardiology

Discontinuing β-Blockers in Patients Who Use Cocaine

Mazumdar JK, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Discontinuing β-blockers in patients who use cocaine. J Hosp Med. 2024;19:524–526. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13245

Original rationale

The "unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation" theory: beta-blockade during cocaine toxicity was thought to leave alpha-receptors unopposed, worsening coronary vasoconstriction and hypertension.

Why it persists

Taught as toxicology dogma for decades; many EMRs flag or hard-block beta-blocker orders on any cocaine-positive history or UDS, regardless of timing or acuity.

What the evidence actually shows

  • The unopposed-alpha theory rests on limited, older physiologic data rather than clinical outcome trials.
  • More recent retrospective cohorts and a systematic review/meta-analysis of beta-blocker use in cocaine-associated chest pain have not shown the predicted harm — several found no increase in adverse cardiac outcomes, and some suggested benefit.

Where real caution remains

The theoretical concern is strongest in acute, ongoing intoxication with non-selective beta-blockade — cardioselective agents in patients with a remote or chronic use history (not acutely intoxicated) appear substantially safer.

Bottom line

A history of cocaine use alone is not sufficient justification to withhold or discontinue beta-blockers. Reserve real caution for active, acute intoxication rather than blanket avoidance based on history.

05 · Infectious Disease / Diagnostics

Routine Respiratory Pathogen Panels for Emergency Department and Hospitalized Patients

Levin S, Mayer D, Puius YA, Arora S, Gileles-Hillel A. Things We Do for No Reason™: Routine respiratory pathogen panels for emergency department and hospitalized patients. J Hosp Med. 2024;19:1170–1173. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13365

Original rationale

Multiplex PCR panels detect a dozen-plus respiratory viruses and atypical organisms fast — comprehensive results feel like better diagnostics and better isolation/treatment decisions.

Why it persists

Perceived diagnostic thoroughness, patient/family expectation of "knowing what it is," and reflexive inclusion in respiratory-complaint order sets.

What the evidence actually shows

  • For most viral targets on these panels, no specific antiviral treatment exists, so a positive result frequently doesn't change management.
  • Systematic reviews of rapid respiratory panel testing have not consistently shown reduced antibiotic prescribing — clinicians often still treat empirically for possible bacterial co-infection regardless of the panel result.
  • Co-detection of multiple pathogens (including asymptomatic viral shedding) can create diagnostic confusion rather than clarity, at real added cost.

Where targeted testing still helps

Influenza/COVID/RSV-specific rapid testing retains real actionability — antiviral eligibility, isolation cohorting, and surveillance. The issue is the broad multiplex panel ordered reflexively rather than testing tied to a specific decision.

Bottom line

Reserve broad respiratory pathogen panels for patients where the result will change a specific management decision — antiviral eligibility, isolation needs, or immunocompromised work-up — rather than ordering reflexively for every respiratory complaint.

06 · Hospital Operations

Discharge Before Noon

Dunn AN, Lu EP. Things We Do for No Reason™: Discharge before noon. J Hosp Med. 2024;19:1174–1176. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13367

Original rationale

"Discharge before noon" (DBN) became a widely tracked hospital throughput metric on the theory that earlier discharges free beds sooner, reduce ED boarding, and improve flow.

Why it persists

Baked into hospital dashboards and unit-level quality/incentive metrics, creating administrative pressure on hospitalist and case-management teams to hit the number.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Studies of DBN as a throughput lever have generally found it does not reliably reduce overall length of stay or meaningfully improve ED boarding at the system level.
  • Chasing the metric creates perverse incentives: rushing discharge decisions, discharging patients before they're truly ready, or front-loading paperwork the night before at the expense of a fresh clinical reassessment the morning of discharge.
  • DBN treats a symptom (bed availability) rather than the actual bottleneck — which is more often mid-day/afternoon admission surges and downstream placement delays, not morning discharge timing.

When earlier discharge still makes sense

For patients unambiguously ready and simply awaiting a ride or straightforward logistics — prioritizing their paperwork earlier in the day is reasonable. The problem is treating DBN as a system-level quality target rather than a case-by-case convenience.

Bottom line

Stop tracking discharge-before-noon rate as a primary quality/throughput metric. Direct flow interventions at the actual bottlenecks — admission processing and placement/subacute bed availability — instead.

07 · Cardiology / Geriatrics

Discontinuing Anticoagulation in Older Patients With Atrial Fibrillation and a High Risk of Falls

Wang S, Mesias M, et al. Things We Do for No Reason™: Discontinuing anticoagulation in older patients with atrial fibrillation and a high risk of falls. J Hosp Med. 2024 (online Jul 21, 2024)/2025. doi: 10.1002/jhm.13464

Original rationale

Falls are assumed to raise the risk of intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) on anticoagulation, so documented fall risk gets treated as a de facto contraindication to starting or continuing anticoagulation for stroke prevention in AF.

Why it persists

An intuitive but largely unquantified fear — reinforced by anecdote and malpractice anxiety — that outweighs, in practice, the much better-quantified stroke-prevention benefit.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Only 40–60% of older AF patients who are appropriate anticoagulation candidates actually receive it — fall risk is among the most frequently cited reasons for withholding treatment, despite the article noting that data substantiating the concern are lacking.
  • A large Medicare-beneficiary analysis of high-documented-fall-risk patients (mean age 80) found ICH rates of 2.8 per 100 patient-years — a number that has to be weighed directly against AF's substantial stroke risk, not treated as an automatic veto.
  • A frequently cited decision-analysis (Man-Son-Hing et al., Arch Intern Med 1999;159(7):677–685) calculated that a patient would need to fall roughly ~295 times in one year for the bleeding risk of anticoagulation to outweigh its stroke-prevention benefit — illustrating how far documented "fall risk" typically is from actually tipping the risk-benefit calculation.

When withholding is still reasonable

Truly recurrent, severe, unwitnessed falls with high-impact mechanism, or fall risk combined with another major independent bleeding risk factor (concurrent antiplatelet therapy, prior ICH) — individualize rather than withhold based on a fall-risk flag alone.

Bottom line

Fall risk alone is rarely sufficient justification to withhold stroke-prevention anticoagulation in AF — the math favors anticoagulation for the great majority of these patients. Reserve discontinuation for genuinely exceptional bleeding risk.

Emergency Medicine · Dermatology

ER Dermatology Atlas

The 10 primary lesions, common secondary changes, and the single most important bedside test in dermatologic triage: the blanch test.

Primary Lesions

Macule

Flat, non-palpable color change &lt;1cm (e.g., a freckle, a petechia).

Patch

Flat, non-palpable color change ≥1cm — the larger counterpart of a macule.

Papule

Raised, palpable lesion &lt;1cm (e.g., a closed comedone, an early insect bite).

Plaque

Raised, palpable, flat-topped lesion ≥1cm, often formed by coalescing papules (e.g., a psoriatic plaque).

Nodule

Raised lesion &gt;1cm with significant depth/mass extending into the dermis or subcutis, distinct from a plaque's more superficial elevation.

Vesicle

Small (&lt;1cm), fluid-filled, raised lesion — clear or serous fluid (e.g., early HSV/VZV, early contact dermatitis).

Bulla

Large (≥1cm), fluid-filled, raised lesion — the larger counterpart of a vesicle (e.g., bullous impetigo, bullous pemphigoid, a friction blister).

Pustule

Raised lesion filled with visibly purulent (cloudy/yellow-white) fluid — implies neutrophilic content, not necessarily active infection (e.g., acne pustule vs. folliculitis).

Wheal (Urticaria)

Raised, edematous, pink-to-red papule/plaque that is transient — an individual wheal should resolve within 24 hours (this timing is diagnostically important for distinguishing true urticaria from urticarial vasculitis, which persists &gt;24h per lesion).

Purpura

Non-blanching color change from blood extravasated into the skin/mucosa — &lt;4mm is petechiae, ≥4mm is purpura by convention; the defining, must-check feature is that it does NOT blanch with pressure (see the blanch test below).

Scale

Not a primary lesion but a common secondary finding — visible flaking/shedding of the stratum corneum, reflecting abnormal keratinization (e.g., psoriasis, tinea, seborrheic dermatitis).

Secondary Changes

Crust

Dried serum, blood, or purulent exudate on the skin surface — forms after a vesicle/bulla/erosion dries (e.g., the honey-colored crust of impetigo).

Excoriation

A linear erosion from scratching — a behavioral marker of significant pruritus, and its presence/pattern can itself be diagnostically useful (e.g., sparing the mid-back, the one place a patient can't reach).

Lichenification

Thickened skin with accentuated skin lines/leathery texture from chronic rubbing/scratching — a marker of CHRONICITY, distinguishing a long-standing process from an acute one.

Erosion

Superficial loss of epidermis only (heals without scarring) — distinct from an ulcer, which extends into the dermis.

Ulcer

Full-thickness loss of epidermis extending into the dermis (or deeper) — heals with scarring, unlike an erosion.

Atrophy

Thinning of the skin, often from chronic topical corticosteroid use or as an intrinsic disease feature (e.g., lichen sclerosus) — visible as fine wrinkling, translucency, or a shiny surface.

The Blanch Test

Press a clear glass slide (diascopy) or a gloved finger firmly onto the lesion. If the color FADES/blanches, the color is from dilated blood vessels (an inflammatory erythema) — reassuring for most benign and inflammatory rashes. If the color does NOT fade, the color is from extravasated blood outside the vessels (true purpura/petechiae) — this changes the differential immediately toward vasculitis, coagulopathy, meningococcemia, or another serious systemic process, and should prompt urgent evaluation rather than routine rash management.

A repeatable skeleton for describing any skin finding, a worked example, and a skin-of-color caveat that changes how erythema should be assessed.

The Documentation Skeleton

1. Distribution

Localized vs. generalized; specific pattern (flexural, extensor, photodistributed, dermatomal, acral, truncal) — state this explicitly, don't rely on an attached photo alone.

2. Number &amp; Arrangement

Solitary vs. multiple; grouped (herpetiform), linear, annular (ring-shaped), reticulated (net-like), or randomly scattered.

3. Primary Lesion

Name the primary lesion type from the morphology primer above (macule, papule, plaque, vesicle, bulla, pustule, wheal, purpura) — this is the single most important documentation element and should come before any descriptive adjectives.

4. Color &amp; Blanching

Erythematous, violaceous, hyperpigmented, hypopigmented, skin-colored — and explicitly state blanching status for any red/purple lesion, since this single detail is often the difference between a reassuring and an emergent read.

5. Size

Measure in cm/mm — avoid relative-only descriptors ("quarter-sized") since they don't translate for a future reader or support objective tracking of change.

6. Secondary Change

Scale, crust, excoriation, lichenification, erosion, ulceration, atrophy — secondary changes often tell you about chronicity or patient behavior (scratching) as much as the primary process.

7. Border

Well-demarcated vs. poorly-demarcated/blending into normal skin — sharp demarcation vs. ill-defined borders is itself a useful diagnostic clue (e.g., contact dermatitis often has a surprisingly sharp, geometric border matching the causative exposure).

Worked Example

Worked example (hives): "Generalized distribution involving the trunk and proximal extremities, numerous discrete and coalescing pink-to-red WHEALS ranging 1-4cm, blanching with pressure, no associated purpura, individual lesions reportedly resolving within hours per patient report (consistent with true urticaria rather than urticarial vasculitis). No mucosal/lip/tongue involvement, no respiratory distress."

Skin-of-Color Caveat

Erythema is frequently harder to appreciate on darker skin tones and can appear violaceous, gray, or simply as a subtle change in skin texture/sheen rather than classic "redness" — palpation for warmth/induration and comparison to adjacent unaffected skin become proportionally MORE important exam techniques in these patients, and relying on color descriptors alone risks under-triaging a genuinely concerning finding (e.g., early necrotizing fasciitis or cellulitis can be substantially under-recognized this way).

01

Stevens-Johnson Syndrome / Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis (SJS/TEN)

Appearance

Painful, dusky-red to violaceous macules/targetoid lesions that rapidly progress to flaccid bullae and full-thickness epidermal SLOUGHING; Nikolsky sign is POSITIVE (lateral pressure on adjacent skin causes further epidermal detachment). Mucosal involvement (oral, ocular, genital) is present in the large majority of cases and is often the earliest sign, sometimes preceding the skin findings by 1-3 days.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

A T-cell-mediated cytotoxic reaction (keratinocyte apoptosis via the Fas-FasL and perforin/granzyme pathways) most commonly triggered by a medication started in the preceding 1-8 weeks — classic culprits include allopurinol, sulfonamide antibiotics, anticonvulsants (lamotrigine, carbamazepine, phenytoin), and NSAIDs. SJS (&lt;10% BSA detachment) and TEN (&gt;30% BSA) are considered the same disease spectrum, distinguished by extent.

Documentation Language

"Dusky, targetoid macules with epidermal sloughing involving [X]% BSA, Nikolsky sign positive. Oral/ocular/genital mucosal involvement: [present, describe]. New medication in the preceding 1-8 weeks: [agent, start date]. Estimated BSA detachment supports SJS (&lt;10%) / SJS-TEN overlap (10-30%) / TEN (&gt;30%) classification."

Treatment

STOP the offending drug immediately — this is the single highest-yield intervention and delay directly worsens mortality. Aggressive supportive care mirrors burn management: IV fluid resuscitation, wound care with non-adherent dressings, and early ophthalmology consultation for ANY ocular involvement (can cause permanent blindness if undertreated). No single systemic immunomodulatory therapy (IVIG, cyclosporine, systemic corticosteroids) has definitively proven superior in controlled trials, though many burn/dermatology centers use one per local protocol.

Disposition

Burn unit or ICU-level admission is standard, especially for &gt;10% BSA involvement — this is not a floor-admission diagnosis. Emergent dermatology and ophthalmology consultation on presentation.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Recheck/reassessment is continuous (ICU-level monitoring) rather than an outpatient interval — mortality is highest in the first 1-2 weeks; SCORTEN score calculated on admission and at 48h predicts mortality and helps guide level of care.

Clinical Pearl

A patient with new oral erosions/conjunctival injection and a recent new medication should be treated as evolving SJS/TEN UNTIL skin findings clarify the picture — don't wait for frank epidermal sloughing to escalate, since earlier recognition and drug discontinuation directly improves outcomes.

02

Necrotizing Fasciitis

Appearance

Erythema that spreads RAPIDLY (can visibly progress within hours) with PAIN OUT OF PROPORTION to exam findings — this pain/exam mismatch is the single most important early clue, since surface findings can look deceptively mild while deep fascial destruction is already extensive. Later, more specific signs include hemorrhagic bullae, skin necrosis/dusky discoloration, crepitus (gas-forming organisms), and rapidly progressive systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, hypotension).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Polymicrobial or monomicrobial (classically group A Streptococcus or, in immunocompromised/diabetic patients, mixed flora including anaerobes) infection spreading along the fascial planes — the fascia's relatively poor blood supply allows rapid bacterial spread and tissue necrosis before the overlying skin shows dramatic change, explaining the exam-lag phenomenon.

Documentation Language

"Rapidly progressive erythema/edema over [site], pain [X/10] disproportionate to visible skin findings. [Hemorrhagic bullae/crepitus/skin necrosis if present]. Systemic toxicity: [fever, tachycardia, hypotension]. Surgical consultation obtained emergently given clinical concern for necrotizing soft tissue infection."

Treatment

Emergent broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (typically vancomycin/piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem, PLUS clindamycin specifically for its antitoxin effect against toxin-producing strains) and EMERGENT surgical consultation for debridement — surgical debridement is the definitive treatment, and antibiotics alone do not substitute for source control in established necrotizing infection.

Disposition

OR for emergent surgical debridement, ICU-level admission — this is a surgical emergency, and time to debridement is directly tied to mortality; do not delay surgical consultation while awaiting imaging if clinical suspicion is high.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Not an outpatient-timeline diagnosis — reassessment is continuous/hourly in the acute phase; often requires SERIAL debridements over subsequent days as the true extent of necrotic tissue is determined progressively.

Clinical Pearl

Pain out of proportion to exam findings, in the right clinical context (diabetes, immunosuppression, recent skin break, IV drug use), should override a reassuring-looking surface exam — imaging (CT/MRI showing fascial gas/fluid) can support the diagnosis but should never delay surgical consultation if clinical suspicion is high, since imaging can also be falsely reassuring early in the disease course.

03

Meningococcemia / Purpura Fulminans

Appearance

Petechiae that rapidly coalesce into larger, NON-BLANCHING purpuric patches with a stellate/irregular ("gunmetal gray") appearance, often starting on the extremities/dependent areas and spreading centrally — accompanied by fever, hypotension, and rapid clinical deterioration in true meningococcemia/purpura fulminans, distinguishing it from more benign petechial causes.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Neisseria meningitidis (or, in purpura fulminans specifically, any cause of severe DIC/protein C-S deficiency, including severe sepsis from other organisms) causes endothelial damage and microvascular thrombosis, leading to widespread cutaneous hemorrhage and, if untreated, progressive tissue ischemia/necrosis of the affected skin and digits.

Documentation Language

"Non-blanching purpuric lesions, [stellate/irregular borders], [distribution and rate of spread documented with time-stamped photo if possible]. Associated fever [X]°C, hemodynamic status [vitals]. Blanch test negative. Empiric antibiotics administered at [time] — document timing explicitly given its prognostic importance."

Treatment

EMPIRIC IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone or another 3rd-generation cephalosporin) given IMMEDIATELY on clinical suspicion — do not wait for confirmatory testing (blood cultures, LP) to administer the first antibiotic dose, since delay is directly associated with increased mortality. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and vasopressor support for septic shock; droplet precaution isolation for the patient and post-exposure prophylaxis consideration for close contacts.

Disposition

ICU-level admission — this is one of the most rapidly fatal infections in medicine if treatment is delayed, with mortality rising measurably for every hour antibiotics are delayed.

Duration / Recheck Timing

No outpatient interval applies — continuous ICU monitoring; close contacts should receive chemoprophylaxis (rifampin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone depending on population) within 24 hours of exposure identification for maximal effectiveness.

Clinical Pearl

In a febrile patient with ANY new non-blanching rash, empiric antibiotics should be given BEFORE the diagnostic workup is complete, not after — this is one of the few dermatologic presentations where treatment genuinely cannot wait for a name.

04

DRESS Syndrome (Drug Reaction with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms)

Appearance

Widespread morbilliform (measles-like) eruption, often becoming edematous/infiltrated (a helpful distinguishing feature from a simple morbilliform drug rash — DRESS lesions feel thicker/more indurated on palpation) with striking FACIAL EDEMA in a substantial fraction of cases — a distinctive finding not typical of most other drug eruptions.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

A delayed (typically 2-8 weeks after drug start — notably longer than most drug reactions) hypersensitivity reaction associated with reactivation of latent herpesviruses (HHV-6 specifically) as part of its pathophysiology, triggered classically by anticonvulsants (especially aromatic ones — phenytoin, carbamazepine), allopurinol, and sulfonamides — the delayed onset relative to drug start is a key distinguishing feature from more immediate drug hypersensitivity reactions.

Documentation Language

"Widespread morbilliform, edematous/indurated eruption with facial edema, onset [X weeks] after starting [drug]. Fever [X]°C. Labs: eosinophilia [absolute count], atypical lymphocytes, LFT elevation [values] — screening for internal organ involvement (liver, kidney, lung, heart) actively pursued given DRESS's systemic, multi-organ nature."

Treatment

Stop the offending drug immediately. Systemic corticosteroids are the mainstay for significant organ involvement, though duration and taper require careful, often prolonged management given DRESS's tendency to FLARE with steroid taper — this relapsing course during treatment is a distinctive management challenge compared to most other drug eruptions.

Disposition

Admission for internal organ screening (LFTs, renal function, cardiac and pulmonary evaluation as indicated) and monitoring — DRESS mortality (roughly 10% in some series) is driven by organ involvement, most often hepatic, rather than the skin findings themselves.

Duration / Recheck Timing

This is a prolonged disease course — skin and systemic findings can persist or relapse over WEEKS TO MONTHS even after stopping the causative drug and starting treatment, and steroid tapers must often be extended over months given the flare risk with faster tapering.

Clinical Pearl

Facial edema plus a widespread drug rash with a delayed (weeks-long) onset after starting a new medication should immediately raise DRESS on the differential rather than being dismissed as a routine morbilliform drug eruption — the systemic organ screening this triggers is what actually catches the potentially fatal internal organ involvement.

05

Erythroderma (Exfoliative Dermatitis)

Appearance

Diffuse erythema and scaling involving &gt;90% of the body surface area — a description of EXTENT/SEVERITY rather than a single specific diagnosis, since erythroderma can be the final common pathway of many underlying conditions (psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, drug reaction, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, or idiopathic).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Widespread cutaneous inflammation from any of the above underlying causes disrupts the skin's normal barrier and thermoregulatory function across nearly the entire body surface simultaneously — this is why erythroderma causes systemic physiologic derangement (see treatment) that a more localized rash of the same underlying disease would not.

Documentation Language

"Diffuse erythema and scaling involving approximately [X]% BSA. Underlying/preceding diagnosis: [known psoriasis/eczema exacerbation, new drug exposure, unknown]. Temperature dysregulation, fluid status, and nutritional/protein status assessed given erythroderma's systemic physiologic impact."

Treatment

Because the skin barrier is disrupted over nearly the whole body, patients lose the ability to regulate temperature and fluid balance normally — treat as a systemic illness: IV fluids, temperature regulation (erythrodermic patients can become hypothermic despite looking inflamed/red), and treatment directed at the underlying cause once identified (withdraw a causative drug, treat an underlying flare of psoriasis/eczema).

Disposition

Admission is generally warranted given the systemic fluid/thermoregulatory risk, even though the skin findings themselves may look less immediately alarming than SJS/TEN — don't underestimate erythroderma's systemic risk just because there's no frank sloughing/blistering.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Resolution timeline depends entirely on the underlying cause and how quickly it's identified/treated — can range from days (drug-induced, once the drug is stopped) to weeks (an underlying flare of chronic disease); dermatology follow-up is essential to identify/manage the underlying trigger long-term.

Clinical Pearl

Always ask "what is the underlying disease here" rather than treating erythroderma as its own diagnosis — the skin finding is a shared final pathway, and management/prognosis differ enormously depending on whether the cause is a treatable drug reaction versus an underlying cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, which requires an entirely different workup.

06

Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS)

Appearance

Diffuse, tender erythema followed by superficial, FLACCID bullae and sheet-like epidermal peeling — Nikolsky sign is positive, similar to SJS/TEN, but critically SPARES the mucous membranes (a key distinguishing feature from SJS/TEN, where mucosal involvement is prominent). Predominantly affects infants and young children.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Exfoliative (epidermolytic) toxins produced by certain Staphylococcus aureus strains (often from a distant, sometimes clinically inapparent focus of infection — conjunctivitis, omphalitis, or a minor skin infection) cleave desmoglein-1, causing a split within the superficial epidermis (distinct from SJS/TEN's full-thickness epidermal necrosis) — this more superficial split explains SSSS's generally better prognosis and faster healing than TEN.

Documentation Language

"Diffuse tender erythema with superficial flaccid bullae and sheet-like desquamation, Nikolsky positive, mucous membranes SPARED. Age: [infant/young child]. Possible primary infection focus: [identified/being sought]. Distinguishing from SJS/TEN specifically documented given the mucosal-sparing finding."

Treatment

IV anti-staphylococcal antibiotics (e.g., nafcillin/oxacillin, or vancomycin if MRSA risk/local resistance patterns warrant) targeting the toxin-producing source infection; supportive skin care similar in principle to a burn (though generally less extensive/severe than TEN given the more superficial split) — gentle handling to avoid further epidermal shearing.

Disposition

Admission for IV antibiotics and monitoring, particularly in infants given fluid/thermoregulatory risk from the skin barrier disruption, though generally less intensive than SJS/TEN management.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Healing is typically faster than TEN given the more superficial split — often within 1-2 weeks with appropriate antibiotic treatment of the underlying source, WITHOUT scarring (since the epidermis, not the dermis, is what's affected).

Clinical Pearl

Mucosal SPARING is the single most useful bedside feature distinguishing SSSS from SJS/TEN when a young child presents with diffuse blistering/peeling skin — this distinction matters enormously since management (antibiotics targeting a toxin-producing infection vs. drug withdrawal + burn-level supportive care) diverges significantly between the two.

01

Cellulitis

Appearance

Poorly-demarcated erythema, warmth, swelling, and tenderness — typically unilateral on an extremity; mark the border with a skin pen at the initial visit to objectively track progression/improvement on recheck.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Bacterial infection (most commonly Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species) of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue, typically entering through a break in the skin barrier (a wound, insect bite, or interdigital fissure from tinea pedis) — purulent/fluctuant cellulitis suggests a Staph/MRSA-predominant process and possible underlying abscess, while non-purulent cellulitis is more classically streptococcal.

Documentation Language

"Poorly-demarcated erythema, warmth, tenderness over [site], borders marked with skin pen at [time] for interval tracking. Purulent drainage/fluctuance: [present/absent — changes antibiotic selection]. Systemic symptoms: [fever, chills — absent supports outpatient management]."

Treatment

Oral antibiotics for non-purulent cellulitis without systemic toxicity (cephalexin or dicloxacillin covering Strep/MSSA; add TMP-SMX or clindamycin empirically if MRSA risk factors present). IV antibiotics (vancomycin, or cefazolin if low MRSA suspicion) for systemic toxicity, rapid progression, or failed outpatient treatment.

Disposition

Outpatient management is appropriate for most uncomplicated cases; admission for systemic toxicity, rapid progression despite oral antibiotics, immunocompromise, or facial/orbital involvement (higher risk of serious complication).

Duration / Recheck Timing

Recheck in 48-72 hours to confirm the marked border isn't advancing — this is the single most useful objective follow-up practice for cellulitis; expect visible improvement (not full resolution) by that interval on appropriate antibiotics, with full resolution typically over 7-10 days total.

Clinical Pearl

Marking the erythema border with a skin pen at the initial visit turns a subjective "does it look better" recheck into an objective measurement — this is a simple, high-value habit that meaningfully improves recheck accuracy.

02

Cutaneous Abscess

Appearance

Fluctuant, tender, erythematous nodule/mass, often with a visible or palpable pointing head — distinguish from simple cellulitis by the presence of fluctuance, which indicates a discrete fluid/pus collection requiring drainage rather than antibiotics alone.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Localized collection of pus within the dermis/subcutaneous tissue, most commonly Staphylococcus aureus (including a substantial proportion of community-acquired MRSA in many regions) — often originates from an infected hair follicle, sebaceous cyst, or minor skin break.

Documentation Language

"Fluctuant, tender [size]cm mass at [site] with [pointing head/surrounding cellulitis extent]. I&amp;D performed under local anesthesia, [packing placed/loop drain placed/no packing per wound size], purulent material sent for culture given [MRSA risk factors/treatment failure risk]."

Treatment

Incision and drainage (I&amp;D) is the primary, definitive treatment — antibiotics ALONE are inadequate for a true abscess regardless of size, since undrained purulent material will not resolve with antibiotics alone. Adjunctive oral antibiotics (TMP-SMX or clindamycin for MRSA coverage) are added per current guidance for abscesses with significant surrounding cellulitis, systemic symptoms, immunocompromise, or in high-risk locations (face, hands, genitalia).

Disposition

Outpatient management after I&amp;D is standard for most cases; admission for extensive surrounding cellulitis, systemic toxicity, or abscesses in higher-risk anatomic locations.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Recheck in 48-72 hours if packing was placed (for removal/reassessment) or sooner if worsening; most abscesses fully resolve within 1-2 weeks after adequate drainage.

Clinical Pearl

A fluctuant mass that's been treated with antibiotics alone without drainage and isn't improving hasn't necessarily failed antibiotic selection — it's more likely failed because it needed I&amp;D in the first place; reassess for fluctuance before escalating antibiotics further.

03

Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

Appearance

Grouped vesicles on an erythematous base, STRICTLY unilateral and confined to a single dermatome, stopping sharply at the midline — pain/burning/paresthesia often PRECEDES the visible rash by 1-5 days, which can cause diagnostic confusion (mimicking cardiac, pleuritic, or abdominal pain depending on the dermatome involved) before lesions appear.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus (VZV) from the dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglia, where it has persisted since a prior primary varicella (chickenpox) infection — reactivation risk rises with age and immunosuppression, reflecting age/immune-related decline in VZV-specific T-cell immunity that normally keeps the latent virus suppressed.

Documentation Language

"Grouped vesicles on erythematous base, strictly unilateral, confined to [dermatome], stopping at midline. Pain/paresthesia onset: [X days] prior to visible rash. Ophthalmic involvement assessed given V1 dermatome distribution [Hutchinson's sign present/absent] — if positive, urgent ophthalmology referral given corneal involvement risk."

Treatment

Oral antiviral therapy (valacyclovir, famciclovir, or acyclovir) started ideally within 72 hours of rash onset to reduce severity/duration and postherpetic neuralgia risk — still reasonable to start beyond 72 hours if new vesicles are actively forming, ongoing pain, or in immunocompromised/older patients. Gabapentin or other neuropathic pain agents for significant pain. Emergent ophthalmology referral for any V1 (ophthalmic division) involvement given corneal/vision risk (herpes zoster ophthalmicus).

Disposition

Outpatient management for uncomplicated cases; admission for disseminated zoster (typically in significantly immunocompromised patients, presenting with lesions outside the original dermatome) or any CNS/ophthalmic complication.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Antiviral course: typically 7 days. Lesions crust over within 7-10 days; postherpetic neuralgia (pain persisting &gt;90 days after rash onset) is the most common complication and risk rises significantly with age &gt;60 — counsel on this risk explicitly at the index visit.

Clinical Pearl

Unilateral, dermatomal pain preceding a visible rash by several days is a classic, easily-missed early zoster presentation — consider it in the differential for unexplained unilateral chest, abdominal, or facial pain in an older or immunocompromised patient even before any rash is visible.

04

Eczema Herpeticum

Appearance

Widespread, monomorphic (uniform-appearing), PUNCHED-OUT vesicles/erosions superimposed on a background of atopic dermatitis (or another disrupted skin barrier condition) — often with fever and systemic symptoms, distinguishing it from a simple eczema flare.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

HSV infection disseminates widely and rapidly across skin with a disrupted barrier (the hallmark of atopic dermatitis specifically) — the same impaired barrier/immune dysregulation that defines atopic dermatitis (see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas) removes the normal containment that would otherwise limit HSV to a localized cold-sore-type presentation.

Documentation Language

"Widespread punched-out vesicles/erosions on a background of known atopic dermatitis, fever [X]°C, onset [X days], rapid spread noted. Viral PCR/Tzanck prep [performed/pending]. IV acyclovir initiated empirically at [time] given clinical suspicion, without awaiting confirmatory testing."

Treatment

IV acyclovir empirically as soon as eczema herpeticum is suspected — do NOT wait for viral PCR/culture confirmation to begin treatment given the risk of rapid dissemination and systemic HSV infection, particularly in young children. Treat any secondary bacterial superinfection if present.

Disposition

Admission for IV antiviral therapy and monitoring, particularly in infants/young children or with extensive involvement — this is a genuine dermatologic emergency superimposed on a very common chronic condition, and recognizing it requires actively considering it rather than defaulting to "just a bad eczema flare."

Duration / Recheck Timing

IV acyclovir typically continued until clear clinical improvement, then often transitioned to oral antiviral to complete a course; lesions crust and heal over 1-2 weeks with appropriate treatment.

Clinical Pearl

A patient with known eczema who develops a sudden, widespread, punched-out vesicular eruption with fever should be treated for eczema herpeticum immediately, not managed as a routine eczema flare with escalated topical steroids — topical steroids in this setting can actually worsen the viral dissemination.

05

Erythema Multiforme

Appearance

Classic TARGET lesions (three concentric zones: dusky center, pale ring, erythematous outer ring) — typically acral (hands/feet), symmetric, and, in contrast to SJS/TEN, mucosal involvement (if present) is generally limited to ONE site and less severe, and there is NO significant epidermal detachment/Nikolsky sign.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Most commonly triggered by HSV infection (recurrent herpes labialis is the classic association, with EM often appearing about a week after a cold sore outbreak) — a hypersensitivity-type reaction to the virus rather than direct viral skin infection; less commonly drug-induced or associated with Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection.

Documentation Language

"Target lesions with three concentric zones, acral/symmetric distribution. Mucosal involvement: [absent/limited to one site — distinguishing from SJS/TEN]. Nikolsky sign negative, no epidermal detachment. Recent HSV outbreak or Mycoplasma symptoms: [present/absent]."

Treatment

Supportive care (antihistamines, topical corticosteroids/analgesics for symptomatic relief) for most cases; treat any identified underlying trigger (though treating an already-resolved HSV outbreak doesn't change the current EM episode). Recurrent EM associated with recurrent HSV may warrant prophylactic antiviral therapy to reduce future episode frequency.

Disposition

Outpatient management for the typical (EM minor) presentation; admission only if significant mucosal involvement affecting oral intake/hydration or diagnostic uncertainty with SJS/TEN.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Self-limited, typically resolving over 1-2 weeks even without specific treatment; recurrent HSV-associated EM can recur multiple times per year in some patients, which is the specific population considered for prophylactic antiviral therapy.

Clinical Pearl

The key bedside distinction from SJS/TEN is the ABSENCE of significant epidermal detachment/Nikolsky sign and the typically limited (not multi-site, not severe) mucosal involvement — when in doubt about which you're seeing, err toward the more cautious SJS/TEN workup and disposition, since missing early SJS/TEN carries far greater consequence than over-triaging EM.

06

Henoch-Schönlein Purpura (IgA Vasculitis)

Appearance

Palpable purpura (raised, non-blanching) classically distributed on the lower extremities and buttocks — often accompanied by the other classic tetrad components: arthralgia/arthritis, abdominal pain, and renal involvement (hematuria/proteinuria), though not all components need be present simultaneously.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

An IgA-mediated small-vessel vasculitis, most common in children, often following an upper respiratory infection — IgA immune complex deposition in small vessels (skin, joints, GI tract, kidneys) drives the multi-organ presentation, explaining why a purely dermatologic read of "just a rash" misses the systemic disease.

Documentation Language

"Palpable, non-blanching purpura over bilateral lower extremities/buttocks. Associated symptoms: [arthralgia/arthritis, abdominal pain, gross/microscopic hematuria]. Urinalysis obtained given renal involvement risk. Recent URI: [present/absent]."

Treatment

Supportive care (NSAIDs for joint pain, though used cautiously given potential renal involvement/GI symptoms) for most cases; systemic corticosteroids for significant abdominal pain or select severe presentations, though their role in preventing long-term renal complications remains debated in the literature.

Disposition

Outpatient management with close follow-up for most children; admission for severe abdominal pain (evaluate for intussusception, a recognized HSP complication), significant GI bleeding, or substantial renal involvement.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Urinalysis/renal function should be monitored for at LEAST 6 months after diagnosis even after the rash resolves, since renal involvement can manifest or evolve well after the skin findings have cleared — this extended surveillance window is a key, easily-overlooked management detail.

Clinical Pearl

Don't discharge a child with HSP without arranging the extended renal surveillance follow-up — the skin rash resolving is not the same as the disease being over, since delayed-onset or evolving nephritis is the complication that actually drives long-term morbidity in this disease.

07

Stasis Dermatitis (The Cellulitis Mimic)

Appearance

Bilateral (usually), poorly-demarcated erythema, scaling, and hyperpigmentation over the lower legs/ankles, often with associated edema and sometimes weeping/crusting — the BILATERAL, chronic, non-tender-to-minimally-tender presentation is the key distinguishing feature from true cellulitis, which is classically unilateral and more acutely tender.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Chronic venous insufficiency causes venous hypertension, capillary leakage of red blood cells and fibrin into the dermis (producing the characteristic hemosiderin-driven hyperpigmentation), and a resulting chronic inflammatory response — not an infectious process, despite its frequent misdiagnosis and treatment as bilateral cellulitis in acute care settings.

Documentation Language

"Bilateral, poorly-demarcated erythema and hyperpigmentation over the lower legs/ankles with associated edema and [scaling/weeping]. Tenderness: [minimal/absent]. Distinguished from cellulitis given bilateral symmetric presentation, chronicity, and absence of systemic symptoms/fever."

Treatment

Leg elevation and compression therapy are the foundational, most effective treatments (addressing the underlying venous hypertension directly), NOT antibiotics — this is a frequently over-treated condition in acute care settings, where bilateral leg erythema is reflexively treated as cellulitis. Topical corticosteroids for significant inflammation/itching; treat any genuinely superimposed infection if truly present (unilateral worsening, fever, or other infectious signs layered on top of chronic bilateral changes).

Disposition

Outpatient management with primary care or vascular follow-up for underlying venous insufficiency evaluation and management (compression stockings, possible venous intervention).

Duration / Recheck Timing

Improvement with elevation/compression is typically visible over days to a couple weeks, though this is fundamentally a CHRONIC condition requiring ongoing compression therapy long-term to prevent recurrence/progression, not a short antibiotic course.

Clinical Pearl

Bilateral leg erythema is stasis dermatitis until proven otherwise — true bilateral cellulitis is genuinely rare, and reflexively prescribing antibiotics for bilateral "cellulitis" is one of the most common avoidable antibiotic-overuse patterns in acute care dermatology; look for the asymmetry, acute tenderness, and systemic symptoms that would instead point to true infection.

01

Measles

Appearance

Erythematous, blanching maculopapular rash starting on the FACE/hairline and spreading cephalocaudally (head to trunk to extremities) over 3 days — preceded by the classic prodrome of the "3 Cs" (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis) and fever; Koplik spots (small white/blue-gray spots on an erythematous buccal mucosal background) are pathognomonic and typically precede the skin rash by 1-2 days.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Measles morbillivirus, an airborne/highly contagious paramyxovirus — the rash reflects a T-cell-mediated immune response to virus-infected cells in the skin, not direct cytopathic viral skin damage, which is part of why the rash appears several days into the illness rather than at initial symptom onset.

Documentation Language

"Cephalocaudal-spreading erythematous maculopapular rash, day [X] of illness, following [3-4 day] prodrome of fever/cough/coryza/conjunctivitis. Koplik spots [present/absent, timing relative to rash]. Vaccination status: [documented]. Public health reporting: [initiated] given reportable disease status."

Treatment

Supportive care (antipyretics, hydration) — no specific antiviral treatment exists. Vitamin A supplementation is recommended by the WHO/AAP for all children with acute measles given documented reduction in morbidity/mortality, particularly relevant in vitamin A-deficient populations but recommended broadly.

Disposition

Outpatient management for uncomplicated cases with strict isolation precautions (airborne, given how highly contagious measles is); admission for complications (pneumonia, encephalitis, or significant dehydration).

Duration / Recheck Timing

Rash and fever typically resolve over 7-10 days total illness course; the patient remains contagious from 4 days before rash onset to 4 days after — this contagious window, not just visible symptom duration, should guide isolation/return-to-school timing.

Clinical Pearl

This is a REPORTABLE disease requiring immediate public health notification — with rising rates of vaccine hesitancy and localized outbreaks, measles should be on the differential for a compatible presentation even in regions where it was previously considered eliminated, and unvaccinated status should raise (not lower) your index of suspicion.

02

Fifth Disease (Erythema Infectiosum)

Appearance

Classic "slapped cheek" bright red facial erythema, followed 1-4 days later by a lacy, reticulated erythematous rash on the trunk/extremities that can wax and wane (notably re-appearing with heat exposure, bathing, or sun exposure) for weeks after the acute illness.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Parvovirus B19 — importantly, by the time the rash appears, the patient is generally NO LONGER contagious (the viremic/contagious phase precedes the rash), an important and often counterintuitive point for return-to-school/daycare counseling.

Documentation Language

"Slapped-cheek facial erythema followed by lacy reticulated rash on trunk/extremities, [timing]. Pregnancy status of household/close contacts assessed given parvovirus B19 fetal risk. Sickle cell/hemolytic anemia history assessed given aplastic crisis risk."

Treatment

Supportive care only (antipyretics, hydration) — the illness is generally mild and self-limited in immunocompetent children.

Disposition

Outpatient management for the typical case; the main clinical concerns requiring closer attention are exposure in PREGNANT contacts (risk of fetal hydrops/loss, particularly in the first half of pregnancy) and in patients with underlying hemolytic anemia/sickle cell disease (risk of transient aplastic crisis from parvovirus B19's tropism for erythroid progenitor cells).

Duration / Recheck Timing

The rash itself can wax and wane for 1-3 weeks, particularly with heat/sun/bathing exposure — counsel families explicitly that rash recurrence over these weeks does NOT indicate ongoing contagiousness or a new illness.

Clinical Pearl

Because the child is no longer contagious once the rash appears, there's no medical reason to exclude them from school/daycare based on the rash itself — this is genuinely useful, reassuring guidance that differs from the isolation logic applied to most other pediatric exanthems.

03

Scarlet Fever

Appearance

Fine, diffuse, sandpaper-textured erythematous rash, often starting in flexural creases (axillae, groin) and spreading, with characteristic PERIORAL SPARING (circumoral pallor) and a "strawberry tongue" (initially white-coated with red, swollen papillae, later becoming uniformly red/beefy) — accompanies streptococcal pharyngitis symptoms (sore throat, fever, tonsillar exudate).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Group A Streptococcus infection (typically pharyngitis) producing an erythrogenic exotoxin that causes the characteristic rash — distinguishing this from simple strep throat matters because scarlet fever carries the same downstream rheumatic fever/post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis risk if untreated, making antibiotic treatment equally important as for uncomplicated strep pharyngitis.

Documentation Language

"Fine sandpaper-textured erythematous rash with circumoral pallor and strawberry tongue, [flexural distribution/spread pattern]. Concurrent pharyngitis: [tonsillar exudate, fever, tender cervical lymphadenopathy]. Rapid strep/throat culture: [performed, result]. Antibiotics initiated: [agent]."

Treatment

Oral penicillin or amoxicillin (first-line) for 10 days — the same treatment as uncomplicated strep pharyngitis, since scarlet fever is simply strep pharyngitis with a toxin-mediated rash rather than a distinct infection requiring different antibiotic choice.

Disposition

Outpatient management for uncomplicated cases; return to school/daycare typically permitted after 24 hours of antibiotic treatment and clinical improvement (fever resolution).

Duration / Recheck Timing

Symptoms improve within 24-48 hours of starting antibiotics; the rash itself can take 1-2 weeks to fully resolve and is often followed by DESQUAMATION (peeling), particularly of the hands/feet, over the following 2-3 weeks — counsel families that this delayed peeling is an expected, benign part of the resolution process, not a new problem.

Clinical Pearl

Circumoral pallor (sparing around the mouth) combined with a sandpaper-textured rash and strawberry tongue is close to pathognomonic — this combination should prompt strep testing/treatment even before classic pharyngitis findings are fully assessed.

04

Hand-Foot-Mouth Disease

Appearance

Small vesicles/erosions on the palms, soles, and oral mucosa (buccal mucosa, tongue), often accompanied by oval/football-shaped vesicles or gray macules — fever and mild systemic symptoms typically precede or accompany the rash; oral lesions can cause significant pain limiting oral intake, which is often the primary clinical concern rather than the skin findings themselves.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Coxsackievirus A16 (classic) or Enterovirus 71 (associated with more severe disease/neurologic complications in some outbreaks) — a highly contagious enteroviral infection spread via fecal-oral and respiratory routes, common in daycare/school settings.

Documentation Language

"Vesicles/erosions on palms, soles, and oral mucosa, fever [X]°C, oral intake status: [adequate/limited by pain]. Hydration status assessed given oral lesion pain risk for reduced intake. Neurologic exam: [normal — screening given rare but serious enterovirus 71-associated neurologic complications]."

Treatment

Supportive care — antipyretics/analgesics for pain (including for oral lesions, where a "magic mouthwash"-type topical analgesic can help maintain oral intake, similar in principle to the oncology mucositis management discussed elsewhere), and hydration monitoring given the oral pain's impact on intake.

Disposition

Outpatient management for the vast majority of cases; admission only for significant dehydration from poor oral intake or the rare neurologic complication (ataxia, myoclonus, or more severe encephalitis-type presentations, more associated with Enterovirus 71).

Duration / Recheck Timing

Self-limited, typically resolving over 7-10 days; the virus can be shed in stool for WEEKS after clinical resolution, though standard return-to-school guidance is generally based on fever resolution and lesion crusting rather than complete viral clearance.

Clinical Pearl

The oral lesions, not the skin findings, are usually the actual clinical problem in HFMD — a child refusing to drink due to painful mouth sores is the presentation that should drive your hydration assessment and management focus, more so than the relatively benign-appearing hand/foot vesicles.

05

Acute Urticaria (Hives) — Pediatric

Appearance

Well-demarcated, raised, pink-to-red wheals of variable size, intensely pruritic, with INDIVIDUAL lesions resolving within 24 hours (though new lesions can continuously form elsewhere, giving the appearance of a persistent rash even as individual wheals come and go) — this 24-hour-per-lesion rule distinguishes true urticaria from urticarial vasculitis.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Mast cell degranulation releasing histamine and other mediators, triggered by a viral illness (the most common pediatric cause), a new food, medication, or, in many cases, no identifiable trigger at all (idiopathic) — IgE-mediated in allergic cases, though many pediatric viral-associated cases are not classically IgE-mediated.

Documentation Language

"Well-demarcated, blanching wheals, [distribution], individual lesions reportedly resolving &lt;24h per parent report. No angioedema, no respiratory/GI symptoms, no hypotension — supporting isolated urticaria over anaphylaxis. Possible trigger: [recent viral illness/new food/medication]."

Treatment

Oral antihistamines (a non-sedating agent like cetirizine for daytime, diphenhydramine can be added at night for its sedating effect to help with sleep given the itch) as first-line; topical treatments (zinc oxide-containing lotions/calamine) can provide additional symptomatic itch relief. Reserve systemic corticosteroids for severe/refractory cases given limited evidence of added benefit for typical acute urticaria and real side-effect burden in children.

Disposition

Outpatient management for isolated urticaria without angioedema/respiratory/GI/cardiovascular symptoms; treat as anaphylaxis (epinephrine, closer monitoring/admission) if ANY of those additional features are present — urticaria alone is not anaphylaxis, but the two exist on a spectrum and mixed presentations need to be triaged toward the more serious end.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Acute urticaria by definition resolves within 6 weeks (chronic urticaria is defined as lasting beyond that point and warrants a different, more extensive workup); most pediatric viral-associated cases resolve within days to 1-2 weeks.

Clinical Pearl

Always specifically ask about and examine for angioedema (lip/tongue/facial swelling), respiratory symptoms, and GI symptoms (vomiting, abdominal pain) before reassuring a family that urticaria is "just hives" — their absence is what actually supports a benign disposition, and this should be explicitly documented, not just assumed.

06

Pediatric Atopic Dermatitis Flare

Appearance

Erythematous, scaly, excoriated patches in an age-dependent distribution (facial/extensor in infants, flexural in older children — see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas for full detail) — in the acute care setting, the key task is distinguishing a routine flare from a superimposed complication (eczema herpeticum, bacterial superinfection).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Same filaggrin-barrier-defect and Th2-skewed immune pathophysiology detailed in the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas — the acute care encounter is typically prompted either by flare severity, parental concern, or a superimposed complication rather than the baseline disease itself.

Documentation Language

"Known atopic dermatitis, acute flare, [distribution, % BSA, secondary changes]. Screened for and [ruled out/concerning for] eczema herpeticum (punched-out monomorphic vesicles, fever) and bacterial superinfection (honey-colored crusting, purulent drainage, disproportionate warmth/tenderness)."

Treatment

Topical corticosteroids for an uncomplicated flare (see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas for potency selection by site); treat any identified bacterial superinfection with appropriate topical or oral antibiotics; escalate immediately to eczema herpeticum management (IV acyclovir, admission) if that specific pattern is identified — see the Urgent tier.

Disposition

Outpatient management for an uncomplicated flare with primary care/dermatology follow-up; admission only if eczema herpeticum, extensive bacterial superinfection, or significant dehydration/systemic illness is identified.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Topical steroid course for an acute flare: typically 1-2 weeks, then return to maintenance emollient care — see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas for the full chronic management framework.

Clinical Pearl

The acute care visit for an eczema flare is a genuine opportunity to actively screen for eczema herpeticum and bacterial superinfection specifically, rather than reflexively treating every flare the same way — a few extra seconds looking for punched-out vesicles or honey-crusting changes the entire management path for a subset of these visits.

01

Allergic/Irritant Contact Dermatitis (ED Presentation)

Appearance

Well-demarcated (allergic) or more diffuse (irritant) erythematous, often vesicular/weeping patches corresponding to a specific contact site — a shape that doesn't match a known dermatosis pattern (linear streaking, a geometric shape matching jewelry/a plant) is itself a diagnostic clue — see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas for full detail.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Type IV delayed hypersensitivity (allergic) or direct chemical/physical skin barrier damage (irritant) — see the Outpatient Dermatology Atlas for the full mechanistic distinction.

Documentation Language

"[Geometric/linear/streaked] dermatitis at [site] corresponding to [suspected exposure]. Onset [X hours-days] after exposure. No systemic symptoms, no evidence of angioedema/anaphylaxis."

Treatment

Topical corticosteroids for the inflammatory component; oral antihistamines for pruritus; identify and counsel on avoiding the causative exposure — the single most important intervention, with no medication substituting for allergen/irritant avoidance.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases; escalate only if widespread involvement with facial/airway swelling raises concern for a more significant allergic reaction.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Improvement typically begins within days of avoiding the exposure; topical steroid course generally 1-2 weeks for the acute reaction.

Clinical Pearl

Poison ivy/oak/sumac classically shows LINEAR streaking where the plant brushed against skin — nearly diagnostic on its own even without a clear reported exposure history.

02

Tinea (Dermatophyte Infection)

Appearance

Annular (ring-shaped) erythematous patch with a raised, SCALY ACTIVE EDGE and relative CENTRAL CLEARING — the active border is where to scrape for KOH prep, since the center may show minimal fungal elements by comparison.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Dermatophyte fungi (Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton species) infect the stratum corneum, feeding on keratin — transmission via direct contact (person-to-person, animal-to-person for some species) or fomites (shared towels, gym mats).

Documentation Language

"Annular erythematous patch with raised scaly active border and central clearing at [site]. KOH prep: [performed, result]. Distinguishing from nummular eczema/psoriasis given the annular configuration with active-edge scale pattern."

Treatment

Topical antifungals (terbinafine, clotrimazole) for localized disease; oral antifungals (terbinafine, griseofulvin in children) for tinea capitis (scalp — topical agents don't adequately penetrate the hair follicle) or extensive/refractory disease.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all presentations.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Topical treatment: typically 2-4 weeks for resolution of localized tinea corporis/cruris/pedis. Oral treatment for tinea capitis: typically 6-8 weeks given the need to treat through the hair follicle's growth cycle.

Clinical Pearl

Tinea incognito (a dermatophyte infection that's been treated with a topical corticosteroid, altering/obscuring its classic annular appearance) should be considered in any "eczema" that isn't responding as expected to steroids — always ask about prior topical steroid use on a rash before accepting an atypical-appearing lesion at face value.

03

Morbilliform Drug Eruption

Appearance

Widespread, symmetric, erythematous macules/papules (measles-like — "morbilliform") typically starting on the trunk and spreading to extremities, usually appearing 1-2 weeks after starting a new medication — the most common drug reaction pattern, and important to distinguish from the more concerning DRESS/SJS-TEN patterns.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

A delayed (type IV) hypersensitivity reaction to a medication — nearly any drug can cause this pattern, though antibiotics (especially penicillins, sulfonamides) are among the most common culprits.

Documentation Language

"Widespread symmetric morbilliform eruption, onset [X days-weeks] after starting [medication]. NO mucosal involvement, NO facial edema, NO blistering/skin sloughing, NO systemic toxicity/fever — actively documented to distinguish from SJS/TEN and DRESS."

Treatment

Stop the offending drug if it's not essential (or substitute if it is); oral antihistamines and topical corticosteroids for symptomatic relief — supportive care is generally sufficient for this uncomplicated pattern.

Disposition

Outpatient management for the typical case; the essential task at the index visit is actively screening for and ruling out the more dangerous mimics (SJS/TEN, DRESS) via the documentation elements above.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Resolves over 1-2 weeks after stopping the causative drug; can occasionally still worsen for a few days after discontinuation before improving ("drug rash flare") — counsel families/patients on this expected initial trajectory so it isn't mistaken for treatment failure.

Clinical Pearl

The critical ED task for ANY new drug rash is actively looking for and documenting the ABSENCE of red-flag features (mucosal involvement, facial edema, blistering, systemic toxicity) — a morbilliform rash without these features is reassuring, but the absence needs to be explicitly assessed and charted, not assumed.

04

Impetigo

Appearance

Superficial erosions with characteristic HONEY-COLORED CRUST (non-bullous, most common form) or, less commonly, flaccid bullae (bullous impetigo) — typically on the face (classically around the nose/mouth) or extremities, especially in children.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Staphylococcus aureus (bullous form specifically, via exfoliative toxin — a localized, milder version of the same toxin mechanism as SSSS) or Streptococcus pyogenes (non-bullous form, though S. aureus also commonly causes non-bullous disease) — a superficial bacterial skin infection, highly contagious via direct contact/fomites.

Documentation Language

"Superficial erosions with honey-colored crust [or flaccid bullae] at [site], [extent]. No surrounding cellulitis/systemic symptoms suggesting a more invasive process. Topical vs. oral antibiotic selected based on extent."

Treatment

Topical mupirocin for limited, localized disease; oral antibiotics (cephalexin or dicloxacillin, adding MRSA coverage like TMP-SMX/clindamycin if risk factors present) for more extensive disease or bullous impetigo.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Improvement typically within 3-5 days of appropriate treatment; return to school/daycare generally permitted after 24 hours of treatment given reduced contagiousness at that point.

Clinical Pearl

Impetigo is highly contagious via direct contact and fomites (towels, shared equipment) — counsel on hygiene measures (not sharing towels, hand hygiene, keeping lesions covered) alongside antibiotic treatment to reduce spread to close contacts.

05

Intertrigo

Appearance

Erythematous, sometimes macerated/weeping patches confined to skin folds (inframammary, inguinal, abdominal panniculus, axillary) — often with satellite papules/pustules if candida has superimposed on the primary irritant process.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Friction and moisture trapped within skin folds cause primary irritant inflammation, which frequently becomes secondarily colonized/infected with Candida albicans (explaining the satellite lesion pattern) given the warm, moist, occluded local environment these folds create.

Documentation Language

"Erythematous, [macerated/weeping] patches confined to [fold location], [satellite papules/pustules present, suggesting candidal superinfection]. Contributing factors: [obesity, diabetes, incontinence, hyperhidrosis] assessed."

Treatment

Keep the area dry (moisture-wicking powder, separating skin folds with dry gauze/cloth where feasible) as the foundational measure; topical antifungal (nystatin, clotrimazole) if candidal superinfection is suspected/confirmed; low-potency topical corticosteroid briefly for significant inflammation, used cautiously in occluded fold areas given increased absorption risk.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Improvement typically within 1-2 weeks with appropriate drying measures and antifungal treatment if indicated; recurrence is common without addressing underlying contributing factors (weight, diabetes control, moisture management) long-term.

Clinical Pearl

Address the underlying moisture/friction driver (not just prescribe an antifungal) for any real chance of preventing recurrence — intertrigo without addressing contributing factors like obesity or incontinence will predictably recur despite appropriate acute treatment.

06

Paronychia

Appearance

Erythema, swelling, and tenderness of the periungual (nail fold) tissue — acute paronychia often shows a visible fluctuant collection/pus, while chronic paronychia (typically from repeated water exposure/irritant contact) shows more indolent swelling without frank abscess.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Acute paronychia: typically bacterial (Staphylococcus aureus most common), often following minor trauma (nail-biting, manicure injury, hangnail) that breaches the nail fold's protective barrier. Chronic paronychia: typically a low-grade irritant/candidal process from repeated wet-work exposure rather than a single acute bacterial event.

Documentation Language

"[Acute: fluctuant, tender, erythematous periungual swelling / Chronic: indolent swelling, history of repeated water exposure]. I&amp;D performed [if fluctuant] under digital block. Culture obtained: [if applicable]."

Treatment

Acute paronychia with a visible fluctuant collection: incision and drainage (often simply with a needle or blade at the point of maximal fluctuance under digital block, given the small/superficial nature of most collections) plus oral antibiotics for surrounding cellulitis or significant infection. Chronic paronychia: address the underlying moisture exposure, topical antifungal/corticosteroid combination, and irritant avoidance rather than antibiotics.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Acute paronychia: improvement within days after drainage. Chronic paronychia: a genuinely prolonged process (weeks to months) requiring sustained behavior change (glove use for wet work) rather than a quick medication course — set this expectation explicitly to avoid frustration with a slower-than-expected response.

Clinical Pearl

Chronic paronychia is fundamentally a BEHAVIORAL/exposure problem (repeated water/irritant contact) more than an infectious one — a prescription alone without addressing the underlying wet-work exposure pattern will predictably fail to resolve it long-term.

01

Scabies

Appearance

Intensely pruritic (classically WORSE AT NIGHT), small erythematous papules with fine, thread-like BURROWS (a pathognomonic finding when visible) — classic distribution includes finger webs, wrists, axillae, waistline, and genitalia, generally sparing the face/scalp in adults (though infants can have facial/scalp involvement, an important distinguishing detail).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Sarcoptes scabiei mite burrows into the epidermis to lay eggs — the intense pruritus reflects a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to the mite, eggs, and fecal matter (scybala) rather than direct mechanical irritation alone, which is why itch can persist for 2-4 weeks even after successful treatment kills the mites ("post-scabetic itch").

Documentation Language

"Intensely pruritic papules, worse at night, with [burrows visible/not visible] at [finger webs/wrists/waistline/genitalia]. Close contacts with similar symptoms: [assessed]. Treatment and simultaneous treatment of household/close contacts discussed given transmission risk."

Treatment

Topical permethrin 5% cream applied neck-to-toe (or scalp-to-toe in infants), left on for 8-14 hours, is first-line; oral ivermectin is an alternative, particularly useful for outbreak settings (nursing homes, institutional settings) or when topical application/compliance is a concern. TREAT ALL HOUSEHOLD/CLOSE CONTACTS SIMULTANEOUSLY regardless of symptoms, since asymptomatic mite carriage during the incubation period is common and untreated contacts will re-infest the index patient.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases; crusted (Norwegian) scabies in immunocompromised patients (a markedly more severe, highly contagious variant with thick scaling and massive mite burden) may warrant more aggressive treatment and infection control precautions.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Itch can persist for 2-4 weeks after successful treatment (post-scabetic pruritus) — counsel on this explicitly so ongoing itch after treatment isn't mistaken for treatment failure and doesn't prompt unnecessary retreatment; wash bedding/clothing in hot water and dry on high heat (or seal in a bag for 72 hours if not washable) to eliminate mites in the environment.

Clinical Pearl

Persistent itch for a couple weeks after appropriate treatment is EXPECTED and does not mean treatment failed — this distinction prevents unnecessary repeat treatment courses and helps set appropriate patient expectations at the index visit.

02

Bed Bug Bites

Appearance

Grouped or LINEAR ("breakfast, lunch, dinner") erythematous papules, often with a central punctum, typically on exposed skin (face, neck, arms) — the linear/clustered arrangement reflects the bug's feeding pattern along a single exposed skin area during one feeding session.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Cimex lectularius (bed bug) bites — a purely mechanical/allergic reaction to the bite itself, with NO disease transmission risk (unlike many other arthropod bites/vectors) — this is an important reassurance point, since bed bugs are not known vectors for any human disease despite the significant psychological distress an infestation causes.

Documentation Language

"Grouped/linear erythematous papules with central punctum on exposed skin areas. History of new/used furniture, travel, or shared living space consistent with bed bug exposure. No systemic symptoms."

Treatment

Symptomatic treatment only — topical corticosteroids and oral antihistamines for pruritus; no antibiotic or antiviral treatment is indicated for the bite itself (though secondary bacterial infection from scratching, if present, is treated per standard impetigo/cellulitis management).

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases — the actual practical challenge is environmental remediation (pest control) rather than medical treatment of the bites themselves.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Individual bite lesions typically resolve over 1-2 weeks; ongoing new lesions reflect continued exposure/infestation rather than a medical treatment failure, and should prompt a pest control conversation rather than escalating topical treatment.

Clinical Pearl

Explicitly reassure patients that bed bugs do not transmit disease — the psychological distress of a bed bug infestation is often disproportionate to the actual medical risk, and this reassurance, alongside practical referral to pest control resources, is often more useful than any topical medication.

03

Tick Bite / Erythema Migrans (Lyme Disease)

Appearance

Erythema migrans: an expanding, often (though not always) "bull's-eye" annular erythematous patch with partial central clearing, typically appearing 3-30 days after a tick bite at or near the bite site, usually ≥5cm in diameter (this size threshold helps distinguish it from a simple local bite reaction, which is smaller and doesn't expand over days).

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Borrelia burgdorferi, transmitted by Ixodes tick species — transmission risk correlates with tick attachment duration, generally requiring at least 36-48 hours of attachment for meaningful transmission risk, which is why prompt tick removal matters even after the fact.

Documentation Language

"Expanding annular erythematous patch, [X]cm, [bull's-eye pattern present/absent], at site of known/suspected tick bite [X days ago], estimated attachment duration [if known]. Geographic exposure risk: [endemic area]. Associated symptoms: [fever, arthralgia, fatigue]."

Treatment

Oral doxycycline (first-line for most age groups, including now recommended for short courses in young children per updated guidance given revised understanding of tooth-staining risk at these doses/durations) for 10-14 days for uncomplicated erythema migrans; single-dose post-exposure prophylactic doxycycline can be considered for a high-risk tick bite (identified Ixodes tick, endemic area, ≥36h attachment) within 72 hours of tick removal, per specific criteria.

Disposition

Outpatient management for uncomplicated erythema migrans; evaluate for disseminated disease (cardiac — Lyme carditis with heart block, neurologic — facial palsy, meningitis) if compatible symptoms are present, which may warrant more urgent evaluation/admission.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Erythema migrans resolves within days of starting appropriate antibiotic treatment; the full antibiotic course (10-14 days) should be completed even after the rash resolves to ensure adequate treatment of the underlying infection.

Clinical Pearl

Not every erythema migrans lesion has the classic bull's-eye appearance — many are simply a uniform expanding erythematous patch, and requiring the classic bull's-eye pattern before considering the diagnosis will cause you to miss cases; size (≥5cm) and expansion over days are more reliable diagnostic features than the classic-but-inconsistent target appearance.

04

Pediculosis (Lice)

Appearance

Intense scalp (head lice), body, or pubic (pubic lice) pruritus with visible NITS (egg casings firmly adherent to hair shafts, unlike dandruff which flakes off easily) and, on close inspection, visible live lice — excoriations and secondary bacterial infection from scratching are common secondary findings.

Etiology / Pathophysiology

Pediculus humanus capitis (head lice), Pediculus humanus corporis (body lice, notably associated with poor hygiene access/crowded living conditions and a recognized vector for other diseases like typhus in specific contexts), or Pthirus pubis (pubic lice, typically sexually transmitted in adults — its presence in a child should prompt consideration of possible sexual abuse and appropriate further evaluation).

Documentation Language

"Intense [scalp/body/pubic] pruritus with visible nits firmly adherent to hair shafts and [live lice observed/not observed]. [If pubic lice in a child: appropriate further evaluation for possible sexual abuse discussed/initiated per protocol]."

Treatment

Topical permethrin 1% (OTC) is first-line for head lice; a second application 7-9 days later is recommended to catch any newly-hatched lice that survived the first treatment as eggs. Oral ivermectin or alternative topical agents (malathion, spinosad) for treatment-resistant cases. Body lice: improved hygiene/clothing laundering is central to treatment given the louse's habitat in clothing rather than directly on the body.

Disposition

Outpatient management for essentially all cases; pubic lice in a prepubertal child warrants the sexual abuse evaluation noted above rather than routine outpatient treatment alone.

Duration / Recheck Timing

Nit removal (fine-tooth combing) alongside the two-dose treatment regimen over 7-9 days is standard; "no-nit" school exclusion policies have been increasingly de-emphasized by the AAP given limited evidence supporting them and their disproportionate educational disruption — worth mentioning to frustrated families citing strict school policies.

Clinical Pearl

Nits (egg casings) remaining after appropriate treatment do NOT necessarily indicate treatment failure or active infestation — dead nits can remain firmly adherent to the hair shaft for a long time after successful treatment, and distinguishing this from a true treatment failure (live lice found on re-exam) prevents unnecessary repeat treatment courses.

16 Rapid-Fire Pearls

  1. Non-blanching = purpura until proven otherwise — always perform the blanch test (diascopy or firm finger pressure) on any red/purple lesion before reassuring a patient.
  2. Pain out of proportion to exam findings is the earliest and most important clue for necrotizing fasciitis — don't wait for skin necrosis or crepitus to escalate.
  3. Mucosal involvement + a new drug in the past 1-8 weeks = think SJS/TEN until proven otherwise, and stop the suspected drug immediately.
  4. Mucosal SPARING distinguishes SSSS from SJS/TEN in a child with diffuse blistering/peeling — this single detail changes the entire management pathway.
  5. Bilateral leg erythema is stasis dermatitis until proven otherwise — true bilateral cellulitis is rare; look for asymmetry and acute tenderness before reaching for antibiotics.
  6. A fluctuant abscess needs drainage, not just antibiotics — antibiotics alone will not resolve an undrained purulent collection regardless of size.
  7. Dermatomal pain preceding a visible rash by days is classic early zoster — consider it for unexplained unilateral chest/abdominal/facial pain even before any rash appears.
  8. A punched-out, monomorphic vesicular eruption on a background of known eczema, especially with fever, is eczema herpeticum until proven otherwise — start IV acyclovir empirically.
  9. Facial edema + a widespread drug rash with delayed (weeks-long) onset should raise DRESS syndrome — pursue organ-system screening (LFTs, renal function) immediately.
  10. In a febrile patient with a new non-blanching rash, give empiric antibiotics BEFORE the workup is complete — meningococcemia mortality rises with every hour of delay.
  11. Erythema migrans doesn't always look like a classic bull's-eye — an expanding, uniform erythematous patch ≥5cm after a tick bite still counts.
  12. HSP renal involvement can appear or evolve well after the skin rash resolves — arrange at least 6 months of urinalysis/renal function follow-up, not just resolution of the rash.
  13. Post-scabetic itch can persist 2-4 weeks after successful treatment — this is expected, not treatment failure, and shouldn't automatically prompt retreatment.
  14. Always treat ALL household/close contacts simultaneously for scabies, regardless of symptoms — asymptomatic carriage during the incubation period causes re-infestation otherwise.
  15. Erythema is harder to see on darker skin tones — rely more on palpation for warmth/induration and comparison to adjacent skin, since color-based descriptors alone risk under-triaging serious findings.
  16. Mark the border of cellulitis with a skin pen at the first visit — this turns a subjective recheck into an objective measurement of whether treatment is working.

ED Dermatology Workflow

1. Blanch Test

Any red/purple lesion gets the diascopy/finger-pressure test before anything else — this single step immediately reroutes the differential if positive for true purpura.

2. Systemic Screen

Fever, hemodynamic stability, mucosal involvement, and pain-out-of-proportion-to-exam are the four questions that separate the life-threatening tier from everything else — ask all four before moving on.

3. Distribution &amp; Pattern

Unilateral/dermatomal vs. bilateral/symmetric vs. generalized — this alone often narrows the differential dramatically (e.g., bilateral leg erythema toward stasis dermatitis, dermatomal toward zoster).

4. Exposure &amp; Timing History

New medications (with start date), recent travel, sick contacts, tick/insect exposure, and known chronic skin conditions (atopic dermatitis specifically, given the eczema herpeticum risk) — timing relative to rash onset is often the most diagnostically useful single piece of history.

5. Document Using the Skeleton

Distribution → number/arrangement → primary lesion → color/blanching → size → secondary change → border — see the Documentation Guide tab.

6. Treat, Consult, or Escalate

Match the tier: life-threatening tier triggers immediate consultation/admission in parallel with initial treatment, not after a full workup; urgent tier gets same-visit treatment with a defined recheck interval; common/pediatric/bites tier gets outpatient treatment with standard follow-up guidance.

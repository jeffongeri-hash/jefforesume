Thyroid Storm

Mortality 10–30% even with treatment — a clinical diagnosis you make and treat before confirmatory labs return.

PathologyWhat's Actually Happening

Thyroid storm is an exaggeration of ordinary thyrotoxicosis, not a distinct biochemical state — free T4/T3 levels are often similar to uncomplicated hyperthyroidism. The decompensation is thought to reflect a combination of an acute rise in free (unbound) hormone — transient saturation of TBG/transthyretin/albumin releases a disproportionate free-hormone surge — plus a heightened tissue response to thyroid hormone and adrenergic co-stimulation, tipping a compensated hyperthyroid state into multi-organ decompensation. This is exactly why storm is a clinical diagnosis (see scoring below), not a lab cutoff.

LabsWhat to Send — But Not Wait For

TSH (suppressed), free T4/T3 (elevated) confirm thyrotoxicosis but do not distinguish storm from uncomplicated hyperthyroidism and should never delay empiric treatment once the clinical score supports the diagnosis. Also send CBC, CMP, LFTs (hepatic dysfunction is part of the score), and a septic/precipitant workup per the trigger list above.

TreatmentThe Five-Point Cascade, in Order

  1. Thionamide first — PTU (loading 500–1000mg, then 250mg q4h) is often preferred acutely because, like propranolol below, it also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion (methimazole 20mg q4–6h is an alternative and preferred once stable/for maintenance, given PTU's hepatotoxicity risk with prolonged use).
  2. Iodine — at least 1 hour after the thionamide, never before. SSKI or Lugol's solution blocks new hormone release (Wolff-Chaikoff exploit, see Volume 1) — giving iodine before the thionamide blocks synthesis provides substrate that can paradoxically worsen thyrotoxicosis.
  3. Glucocorticoids — dexamethasone 2mg IV q6h or hydrocortisone 100mg IV q8h. Blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion, treats the relative adrenal insufficiency that can accompany severe thyrotoxicosis, and reduces systemic inflammation.
  4. Beta-blockade — propranolol preferred (60–80mg PO q4–6h, or 1–2mg IV slowly q10–15min) specifically because it also blunts T4→T3 conversion, unlike cardioselective agents. Caution: in a patient with underlying heart failure or thyrocardiac disease, non-selective propranolol can precipitate cardiogenic shock — use a short-acting, easily titratable agent (esmolol) instead, or diltiazem/verapamil for rate control if beta-blockade is contraindicated.
  5. Treat the precipitant and provide supportive care — cooling measures/acetaminophen for fever (avoid aspirin, which displaces T4 from TBG and raises free T4), fluids/glucose/electrolyte correction, ICU-level monitoring.
The order matters
Iodine before a thionamide, or a beta-blocker before establishing that the patient can tolerate it hemodynamically, are the two most consequential sequencing errors in this cascade. For refractory cases, cholestyramine (interrupts enterohepatic recirculation of thyroid hormone) or plasmapheresis (mechanical removal of circulating hormone) are escalation options.

Myxedema Coma

The mirror-image emergency to thyroid storm — and the one place in this entire atlas where treating the "obvious" problem first can kill the patient.

PathologyDecompensated Hypothyroidism

The extreme end of untreated or undertreated hypothyroidism — compensatory mechanisms that normally buffer severe thyroid hormone deficiency (see Volume 1) are overwhelmed by an intercurrent stressor, most commonly infection. Despite the name, coma is not required for diagnosis — altered mental status plus hypothermia in a hypothyroid patient is the core clinical picture. Rare (0.22–1 per million/year) but carries high mortality, with a striking female predominance (~8:1) and typical age >60.

Triggers

Infection (most common) Cold exposure Sedative/opioid medications Stroke, MI Levothyroxine non-adherence Surgery, trauma GI bleeding

LabsWhat Confirms It

Low free T4/T3, elevated TSH (primary hypothyroidism) or inappropriately normal/low TSH (central) — confirmatory, but treatment should never wait for results in a clinically compelling presentation given the high mortality of delay. Hypothyroidism belongs in the differential of every unexplained hyponatremia — a genuinely high-yield, easy-to-miss association. Also check glucose (hypoglycemia is a life-threatening precipitant requiring immediate correction) and a random cortisol before starting steroids.

TreatmentSteroids Before Thyroid Hormone — Always

The single most important sequencing rule in this file
Myxedema coma can coexist with adrenal insufficiency — either primary (autoimmune polyglandular overlap) or secondary (pituitary disease causing both central hypothyroidism and central adrenal insufficiency together). Giving thyroid hormone before corticosteroids accelerates cortisol metabolism and can precipitate a full adrenal crisis in a patient whose adrenal reserve was only marginally adequate. Always give stress-dose hydrocortisone first (or simultaneously) — never thyroid hormone alone.
  1. Draw a random cortisol, then start hydrocortisone 100mg IV, continuing 100mg IV q8h (total 200–400mg/day) until adrenal insufficiency is excluded by the cortisol result — this can be weaned once hypotension resolves and adequate adrenal reserve is confirmed.
  2. IV levothyroxine (T4): loading dose 200–400mcg IV (use the lower end in elderly, low-body-weight, or cardiac patients — high-dose T4 can precipitate arrhythmia or MI), then 50–100mcg IV daily until oral intake is possible.
  3. Consider adding liothyronine (T3) given impaired peripheral T4→T3 conversion in severe illness (see Volume 1 non-thyroidal illness discussion) — typically 5–10mcg IV q8h, using the lower end of dosing given concern that excessive T3 exposure may itself carry a mortality signal in some series; many protocols reserve T3 addition for patients who don't improve with T4 alone within 24 hours.
  4. Supportive care: secure the airway (intubate if GCS ≤8), passive rewarming for hypothermia (avoid aggressive active rewarming, which can cause vasodilation and cardiovascular collapse), correct hypoglycemia and hyponatremia, empiric antibiotics if sepsis can't be excluded (hypothyroidism can blunt fever and leukocytosis, masking underlying infection), ICU admission.

Monitor free T4 and total/free T3 every 1–2 days; failure of TSH to trend down or thyroid hormone levels to improve suggests a need to increase therapy, while a rising free T3 is a signal to reduce dosing given arrhythmia/MI risk.

Adrenal Crisis

Treat on clinical suspicion — never delay for confirmatory labs. This is the fastest-killing emergency in this file if missed.

Pathology

Absolute or relative cortisol deficiency during a physiologic stressor the body cannot compensate for without its normal 5–10x cortisol surge (see Volume 1) — loss of cortisol's permissive vascular tone (reduced catecholamine responsiveness) and glucose/electrolyte regulation produces refractory hypotension, hypoglycemia, and (in primary disease) hyperkalemia/hyponatremia from concurrent aldosterone deficiency.

Triggers

Infection/sepsis (most common) Abrupt glucocorticoid withdrawal after chronic use (see Volume 3a) Surgery/trauma without stress dosing Missed oral replacement doses + vomiting/diarrhea Bilateral adrenal hemorrhage/infarction (anticoagulation, antiphospholipid syndrome, meningococcemia — Waterhouse-Friderichsen) ICI-induced adrenalitis or hypophysitis (see Volume 5) Pituitary apoplexy

Labs

FindingWhy
Hyponatremia, hyperkalemiaPrimary adrenal failure only — aldosterone deficiency (see Volume 1); absent in secondary/tertiary insufficiency where the RAAS-driven mineralocorticoid axis is intact
HypoglycemiaLoss of cortisol's gluconeogenic/counter-regulatory support
Random cortisol <10 mcg/dL in an acutely ill/hypotensive patientStrongly suggestive; draw it, but don't wait for the result to treat
ACTHHigh in primary disease, low/inappropriately normal in secondary — send but don't delay treatment

Treatment

  1. Hydrocortisone 100mg IV bolus, then 200mg/24h (continuous infusion or 50mg IV q6h) — hydrocortisone specifically, for its combined glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid activity (see Volume 3a's steroid class comparison).
  2. Aggressive isotonic fluid resuscitation — 1L normal saline over the first hour, guided by hemodynamic response, with dextrose added if hypoglycemic.
  3. Treat the precipitant — empiric antibiotics if infection can't be excluded.
  4. Do not wait for labs to give the first hydrocortisone dose — draw a cortisol/ACTH if feasible without delay, then treat regardless.
Every known adrenal insufficiency patient needs a plan
Patients with known adrenal insufficiency should carry injectable hydrocortisone (or have clear sick-day dosing instructions: typically double-to-triple the oral dose during illness) and a medical alert indicator — most adrenal crisis deaths occur in patients with an established diagnosis whose crisis plan failed at some step, not in patients whose adrenal insufficiency was entirely unknown.

Hypercalcemic Crisis

Severe hypercalcemia (>14 mg/dL) — malignancy is the most common cause in hospitalized patients, and the 2022 Endocrine Society guideline shifted first-line therapy toward denosumab.

Pathology

Severe hypercalcemia causes a nephrogenic diabetes insipidus-like picture (impaired renal concentrating ability) → volume depletion → reduced GFR → reduced renal calcium clearance → worsening hypercalcemia, a self-reinforcing cycle that volume repletion is designed to interrupt. See Volume 1's calcium-phosphate axis for the underlying PTH/vitamin D/calcitonin/FGF23 physiology this crisis overwhelms.

Triggers/Causes

Labs

Corrected/ionized calcium confirms severity (>14 mg/dL = severe per Endocrine Society definition); PTH distinguishes PTH-mediated (primary hyperparathyroidism/parathyroid carcinoma) from PTH-independent causes (malignancy, vitamin D-related — see the full Volume 3c calcium/PTH interpretation table); phosphate, creatinine, and — where granulomatous disease or lymphoma is suspected — 1,25-(OH)₂-vitamin D.

Treatment

  1. IV isotonic saline — the cornerstone of initial management, correcting volume depletion and improving GFR/renal calcium clearance; typically 200–300mL/hr initially, titrated to urine output and volume status.
  2. Antiresorptive therapy — current (2022) Endocrine Society guidance conditionally recommends denosumab over IV bisphosphonate for hypercalcemia of malignancy, a shift from older bisphosphonate-first practice. Denosumab is not renally cleared, making it preferred in renal impairment where bisphosphonates (zoledronic acid, pamidronate) are relatively contraindicated or require dose adjustment.
  3. Calcitonin — added for severe cases (>14 mg/dL) alongside the antiresorptive agent. Fast onset but tachyphylaxis limits use to 48–72 hours — it's a bridge, not a standalone therapy, while the slower-onset antiresorptive agent takes effect (bisphosphonate max effect ≤7 days; denosumab max effect 14–23 days).
  4. Glucocorticoids — first-line specifically for calcitriol-mediated hypercalcemia (granulomatous disease, lymphoma) since steroids directly suppress the extrarenal 1α-hydroxylase activity driving it (Volume 1) — not a general hypercalcemia treatment outside this specific mechanism.
  5. Loop diuretics — historically used after adequate volume repletion, but now de-emphasized; only appropriate once euvolemic, and never as a substitute for fluid resuscitation.
  6. Dialysis — reserved for severe hypercalcemia with renal failure or when standard therapy is contraindicated/ineffective.
Match the therapy to the mechanism
Bisphosphonates and denosumab both work by suppressing osteoclast-mediated bone resorption — appropriate for malignancy-associated and PTH-mediated hypercalcemia, but not the primary lever for calcitriol-mediated hypercalcemia, where the driving mechanism is excess intestinal calcium absorption, not bone resorption — glucocorticoids address that mechanism directly.

DKA & HHS

Two ends of the same insulin-deficiency spectrum, distinguished by how much residual insulin is left to suppress ketogenesis — built directly on Volume 1's pancreatic physiology.

PathologyWhy Ketones Need Near-Total Insulin Absence

As established in Volume 1: insulin potently suppresses hormone-sensitive lipase and ketogenesis at very low concentrations — far lower than the concentration needed to normalize glucose. This is why DKA (near-total insulin deficiency, classically T1DM) produces both hyperglycemia and ketoacidosis, while HHS (relative insulin deficiency, classically T2DM) has just enough residual insulin to suppress lipolysis/ketogenesis but not enough to prevent severe hyperglycemia — explaining HHS's more extreme glucose elevations and minimal ketosis.

TreatmentThe Core Sequence

  1. IV isotonic fluids first — before insulin, particularly in HHS where profound volume depletion dominates; typically 1–1.5L in the first hour.
  2. Potassium before insulin if K<3.3 mEq/L — insulin drives potassium intracellularly (Volume 1) and can precipitate life-threatening hypokalemia/arrhythmia if given to a patient who is already hypokalemic; replete first.
  3. IV insulin infusion (typically 0.1 units/kg/hr after an optional bolus) — continued until the anion gap closes in DKA (not just until glucose normalizes — ketoacidosis resolution is the endpoint, tracked via anion gap/bicarbonate, not glucose alone).
  4. Add dextrose once glucose falls to ~200–250 mg/dL — allows the insulin infusion to continue (needed to fully clear ketones) without causing hypoglycemia.
  5. Transition to subcutaneous insulin only once the gap has closed/ketoacidosis resolved, with overlap of the last subcutaneous dose and the insulin infusion (subcutaneous insulin's onset lag means stopping the infusion first risks rebound ketoacidosis).
Cerebral edema — the pediatric-predominant complication
Overly rapid correction of glucose/osmolality, especially in pediatric DKA, is associated with cerebral edema — a rare but devastating complication that's part of why glucose correction is deliberately gradual rather than aggressive once the acute resuscitation phase passes.

Pheochromocytoma Crisis

The one emergency in this atlas where giving a "standard" hypertensive-emergency drug in the wrong order can kill the patient — beta-blockade before alpha-blockade is never acceptable here.

Pathology

Massive catecholamine release (see Volume 2) from a pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma activates both α- and β-adrenergic receptors simultaneously. β-receptor activation normally provides some vasodilatory counterbalance (β2, skeletal muscle vasculature) to α1-mediated vasoconstriction — blocking the β-receptors first removes that counterbalance while leaving α1 vasoconstriction fully unopposed, producing a paradoxical, potentially catastrophic hypertensive surge.

Triggers

Abdominal palpation/positioning during imaging or surgery Anesthesia induction Metoclopramide, glucagon, high-dose steroids Beta-blocker given without prior alpha-blockade (iatrogenic) Tyramine-containing foods (in patients on MAO inhibitors) Radiographic contrast (historically implicated, now considered lower-risk with modern non-ionic contrast)

Labs

Plasma free metanephrines or 24-hour urine fractionated metanephrines confirm the diagnosis (see Volume 3c for the full interference/interpretation discussion) — but in a true hypertensive crisis, treatment is started on clinical suspicion without waiting for results.

TreatmentAlpha Before Beta — No Exceptions

  1. Alpha-blockade first — phentolamine (nonselective, competitive, IV, short-acting — used for acute crisis management) or phenoxybenzamine (nonselective, irreversible, longer-acting — used for pre-operative preparation over 10–14 days). Selective α1-blockers (doxazosin) are an alternative, though recent data suggest phenoxybenzamine may produce shorter, less severe intraoperative hypertensive episodes than doxazosin.
  2. Volume expansion — chronic catecholamine excess causes vasoconstriction-mediated volume contraction; alpha-blockade unmasks this, so isotonic fluids and liberal salt intake are used alongside alpha-blockade to prevent severe post-blockade hypotension.
  3. Beta-blockade only after adequate alpha-blockade (typically 2–3 days later) — added specifically to control the reflex tachycardia that alpha-blockade itself produces, not to treat the hypertension directly.
  4. Alternative/adjunct agents: nicardipine or nitroprusside for acute pressure control; metyrosine (inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting step in catecholamine synthesis — see Volume 2) reduces catecholamine production at the source and can be added for refractory cases or short-term pre-operative use.
  5. Surgery is definitive treatment once adequately blocked — timing and coordination with anesthesia are critical given the risk of intraoperative catecholamine surges during tumor manipulation.
The classic exam (and real-world) trap
Labetalol, despite having some alpha activity, is predominantly a beta-blocker and is not an appropriate substitute for sequential alpha-then-beta blockade in a pheochromocytoma crisis — this is one of the most tested and most clinically real pitfalls in this entire atlas. If a hypertensive crisis is suspected to be pheochromocytoma-driven, reach for phentolamine or nicardipine, not a beta-blocker or a combined agent with predominant beta activity.

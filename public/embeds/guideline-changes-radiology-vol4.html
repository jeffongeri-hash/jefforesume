What Changed · 2024–2026

Contrast media guidance was rewritten from the ground up

Across the 2024–2025 editions of the ACR Manual on Contrast Media, five long-standing bedside habits changed: "contrast-induced nephropathy" became a nuanced CI-AKI/CA-AKI framework with a much higher no-risk eGFR floor, breastfeeding "pump and dump" advice was retired, metformin hold thresholds loosened, gadolinium risk classification was simplified, and the shellfish-allergy premedication reflex was explicitly rejected.

01 · Overview

At a glance

03 · Deep Dive

"Pump and dump" is retired

Breastfeeding after contrast Major change, 2024

Roughly 1% of administered iodinated contrast is excreted into breast milk, and of that fraction, oral bioavailability to the infant is negligible. Gadolinium excretion into breast milk is similarly minimal and absorbed in clinically insignificant quantities by infant GI tract, with macrocyclic agents showing the lowest tissue retention profile. The prior 24–48 hour interruption recommendation is now considered overly conservative and unsupported by current evidence.

⚡ Shared decision, not a requirement

If a patient prefers to pause breastfeeding anyway, a 24-hour interruption remains an acceptable option — but this should be framed as the patient's choice, not routine medical advice. Don't default to recommending interruption.

Pre-contrast screening is now targeted, not universal — routine creatinine/eGFR for every contrast patient is no longer recommended. Screening is reserved for patients with risk factors: known/suspected CKD, prior AKI, dialysis, renal transplant, single kidney, kidney surgery or cancer history, or known albuminuria. Strongest remaining risk factors for actual CA-AKI: pre-existing eGFR <30, diabetes with CKD, volume depletion, and concomitant nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides). Age >75, CHF, hypertension, and single kidney alone are debated/weak factors — clinically meaningful mainly in the context of reduced eGFR.

✓ Verify before use

This does not mean renal function is irrelevant — it means the threshold for concern moved. An eGFR of 35 that would have triggered prophylaxis protocols under older CIN-era teaching no longer does under current ACR guidance.

05 · Deep Dive

Metformin: the 30–44 hold zone is gone

Metformin & iodinated contrast Threshold loosened

Mechanism: contrast → CA-AKI → reduced metformin clearance → accumulation → metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA), rare but potentially fatal
Clinical contextAction
eGFR ≥30, no AKIContinue metformin — no hold required
eGFR <30 or known AKIHold at time of contrast; restart at 48h only after confirming stable renal function
Arterial catheter studies, any eGFRHold regardless of eGFR — renal artery embolism risk; restart per same 48h rule
Emergent contrast, eGFR unknownGive contrast; hold metformin after; check renal function at 48h before restarting
⚡ What actually changed

Prior guidance to hold metformin for eGFR 30–44 is no longer recommended. That middle zone — a common source of unnecessary held doses and inpatient confusion — is gone for routine IV contrast. Arterial catheter studies remain the exception, still holding regardless of eGFR.

06 · Deep Dive

Gadolinium risk classification simplified

GBCA / NSF reclassification Group I essentially gone from US market

All confirmed nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) cases have been linked to Group I (high-risk, linear) agents — gadodiamide, gadopentetate dimeglumine, gadoversetamide. These are essentially withdrawn from routine US use. Every agent in current routine use is now Group II (macrocyclic-preferred, low/negligible NSF risk), with zero confirmed NSF cases at standard doses. A newer agent, gadopiclenol, carries a provisional Group III designation pending broader high-risk-population safety data.

eGFR ≥30
Group II: standard dose, no special precautions, eGFR screening optional
CKD stage 3
No special precautions necessary — NSF after Group II GBCA is exceedingly rare in this population

Gadolinium retention in brain, bone, and skin with repeated dosing is real and dose-related — macrocyclic agents deposit significantly less than linear agents — but no adverse clinical effects have been established in patients with normal renal function.

07 · Myth-Busting

The shellfish-allergy premedication reflex is explicitly rejected

⚠ Long-standing dogma, explicitly rejected by ACR

Shellfish or iodine allergy confers no greater risk of a contrast reaction than any other allergy — this prior assumption is explicitly rejected in current ACR guidance. What does matter: a prior allergic-like reaction to the same contrast class (~5× increased risk of recurrence) and multiple unrelated allergies to food or environmental triggers (2–3× increased risk). Premedication should be triggered by reaction history, not shellfish status.

Also worth institutional correction: a prior physiologic reaction (nausea, warmth, flushing — dose-related, self-limited) does not warrant premedication. Only prior allergic-like or unknown-type reactions do. Even with premedication, breakthrough reactions occur in an estimated 10–17% of patients and tend to mirror the index reaction's severity.

08 · Deep Dive

Routine NPO before contrast isn't supported

Fasting before IV contrast Requirement removed

ACR position: fasting is not required before IV iodinated (CT) or gadolinium (MRI) contrast with modern nonionic, low-osmolality agents. This doesn't apply when sedation or general anesthesia is separately required — standard anesthesia NPO rules still apply for that reason alone.

09 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Stop telling breastfeeding patients to pump and dump after contrast — current guidance is to continue nursing without interruption.
  • An eGFR of 30–59 is not, by itself, a reason to withhold or delay IV iodinated contrast, prophylax with fluids, or order renal labs on a patient without CKD risk factors.
  • Metformin doesn't need to be held for eGFR 30–44 anymore — only below 30, with known AKI, or before arterial catheter studies.
  • Drop "shellfish allergy" from your premedication trigger checklist — it isn't one. Prior contrast-class reaction history is what matters.
  • Don't make patients fast before a contrast CT or MRI unless sedation is separately planned.
  • Contrast is not contraindicated in dialysis-dependent ESRD patients, and urgent dialysis-timing changes aren't required around it.

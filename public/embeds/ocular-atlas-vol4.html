Optic Neuritis

This is the textbook diagnosis for teaching the relative afferent pupillary defect — and the RAPD grading scale you use here is a skill that transfers directly to every other afferent visual pathway diagnosis in this atlas.

Demyelinating inflammation of the optic nerve, the most common cause of unilateral painful vision loss in the US and the initial presentation of multiple sclerosis in roughly one in five patients who go on to develop MS. Pain, especially with eye movement, is present in the large majority of patients and classically precedes or accompanies vision loss by a few days, typically resolving within a week even as visual loss continues to evolve over the following days. The fundus exam is frequently entirely normal (retrobulbar neuritis, inflammation posterior to the disc) in roughly two-thirds of cases; disc swelling (papillitis) is seen in the remaining minority.

**RAPD interpretation (Marcus Gunn pupil):** A relative afferent pupillary defect reflects asymmetric input to the pretectal light-reflex pathway between the two eyes and is one of the single most useful bedside signs in all of neuro-ophthalmology, since it is an objective, examiner-independent finding of asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal disease that can't be feigned or mistaken for a media opacity (a dense cataract does NOT cause an RAPD). It is tested with the swinging flashlight test: alternately illuminate each pupil for 2-3 seconds, swinging the light back and forth, and watch the response as light moves to the affected eye. A relative defect causes the affected pupil to paradoxically DILATE (rather than remaining constricted or constricting further) when light swings onto it, because the consensual input from the healthier eye had been maintaining more constriction than the affected eye's own direct input can sustain.

RAPD is commonly graded on a 1+ to 4+ (sometimes reported in log units via neutral density filters) scale of severity, though for most bedside clinical purposes simply documenting presence, absence, and a rough severity impression (mild/moderate/marked) is sufficient. Causes span the entire spectrum of unilateral or markedly asymmetric optic nerve or severe retinal disease: optic neuritis, ischemic optic neuropathy (arteritic or non-arteritic), compressive optic neuropathy (tumor), traumatic optic neuropathy, and severe/extensive retinal disease (large retinal detachment, CRAO, severe ischemic CRVO) — but critically, NOT a dense cataract, an uncorrected refractive error, or a purely efferent pupillary problem (e.g., isolated CN III palsy affecting pupil constriction) with a normal optic nerve/retina, since RAPD specifically isolates the afferent (sensory input) limb of the pathway.

Risk Factors
  • Multiple sclerosis (established or subsequently diagnosed) is the dominant association; optic neuritis is the presenting feature of MS in a substantial minority of eventual MS patients
  • Female sex and age 20-45 are the classic epidemiologic profile for typical demyelinating optic neuritis
  • Other demyelinating/inflammatory conditions increasingly recognized as distinct entities with different management/prognosis: neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD) and MOG-antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), both of which can mimic typical optic neuritis but carry different recurrence risk, treatment, and visual prognosis
Fundoscopic Findings

Normal in roughly two-thirds of cases (retrobulbar neuritis); disc edema/hyperemia (papillitis) in the remaining minority. A normal-appearing disc does NOT exclude the diagnosis in an appropriate clinical context (pain with eye movement, RAPD, reduced color vision) and should not be used to argue against it.

History
  • Pain characteristics (present in the large majority, classically worse with eye movement, typically resolves within about a week), timeline of vision loss (hours to days, stabilizing over 1-2 weeks).
  • Prior neurologic symptoms suggestive of demyelinating disease (sensory changes, weakness, bladder symptoms, prior episodes) relevant to MS risk stratification.
Exam
  • Visual acuity, color vision (dyschromatopsia is often disproportionately affected relative to acuity and is a sensitive sign), and formal visual field assessment if available.
  • Swinging flashlight test for RAPD (see pathophysiology section above for full technique and interpretation) — expected to be present given the disease is typically unilateral or markedly asymmetric.
  • Dilated fundus exam; document whether papillitis or a normal (retrobulbar) appearance is present.
Imaging/Labs
  • MRI brain and orbits with gadolinium is the key test — both confirms the diagnosis (enhancement of the optic nerve) and is the single strongest predictor of subsequent MS conversion risk based on brain lesion burden.
  • Consider NMOSD (aquaporin-4 antibody) and MOGAD (MOG antibody) serologic testing for atypical features: bilateral simultaneous involvement, severe/poor recovery, lack of pain, prominent disc edema, or a more fulminant course, since these carry different management and recurrence implications than typical demyelinating optic neuritis.

3
Consider IV corticosteroids for faster visual recovery

Set this expectation explicitly with patients considering treatment.

⏰ Started early in the course if pursued, per ONTT protocol (typically IV methylprednisolone followed by an oral taper)
4
Do not use oral corticosteroids alone at conventional doses

ONTT notably found that oral prednisone alone at standard (low) doses was associated with an INCREASED rate of recurrent optic neuritis compared to placebo — this is a specific, counterintuitive trial finding that should actively steer management away from oral-only conventional-dose steroid treatment.

⏰ N/A — a default-avoid based on ONTT findings
5
Refer to neurology for MS risk discussion and potential disease-modifying therapy

Based on MRI lesion burden and clinical presentation, neurology can discuss MS conversion risk and, where appropriate, disease-modifying therapy to reduce future relapse risk; this is a longitudinal relationship, not a single ED/ophthalmology visit.

⏰ Following MRI results
6
Reconsider the diagnosis if recovery doesn't begin within about 5 weeks

Typical demyelinating optic neuritis begins spontaneous improvement within about 3 weeks in the large majority of patients; failure to show any improvement by roughly 5 weeks should prompt reconsideration of alternative diagnoses (compressive lesion, NMOSD, MOGAD, sarcoid, infectious causes) rather than continued observation.

⏰ Reassessed at the 5-week mark if recovery hasn't begun

GCA / Arteritic Ischemic Optic Neuropathy

This is the one diagnosis on the entire sudden-vision-loss differential where treatment starts before any test result comes back — the framework below is built specifically to help you separate it from its mimics under time pressure.

Giant cell arteritis is a large/medium-vessel vasculitis, typically in patients over 50, that can occlude the posterior ciliary arteries supplying the optic nerve head, causing sudden, severe, often profound monocular vision loss with a pale, swollen disc. Unlike the non-arteritic form (NAION), this is frequently but not always accompanied by systemic symptoms (new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, polymyalgia rheumatica) — isolated ocular presentations without systemic symptoms occur and are a recognized diagnostic blind spot even in academic centers.

**Sudden vision loss differential framework:** Four bedside variables reliably sort the major causes of sudden vision loss into distinct diagnostic buckets: (1) painful vs. painless, (2) presence/absence and grade of an RAPD, (3) fundus appearance, and (4) monocular vs. binocular. Painless + monocular + RAPD + cherry-red spot on a pale retina → CRAO. Painless + monocular + RAPD + pale swollen disc + age over 50 with systemic symptoms → arteritic AION (GCA) until excluded. Painless + monocular + RAPD + hyperemic swollen disc + no systemic symptoms → non-arteritic AION. Painful + monocular + RAPD + normal or swollen disc + younger patient → optic neuritis. Painless + monocular + NO RAPD + normal fundus → consider a media opacity or non-organic cause, or a very mild/patchy retinal process. Painless + BINOCULAR + entirely normal pupil and fundus exam → cortical/occipital process (bilateral PCA territory stroke, PRES) — the mismatch between severe bilateral vision loss and a normal eye exam IS the finding, prompting emergent brain imaging rather than an eye-focused workup.

Risk Factors
  • Age over 50 (average age at diagnosis is typically in the 70s), with incidence rising further with each subsequent decade
  • Female sex and Northern European ancestry are both associated with higher incidence
  • Polymyalgia rheumatica (a substantial proportion of GCA patients have concurrent or preceding PMR symptoms)
Fundoscopic Findings

A pale, swollen (edematous) optic disc, often with associated flame hemorrhages at the disc margin; distinguished from the hyperemic (rather than pale) disc swelling more typical of non-arteritic AION. RAPD is present given the typically severe, asymmetric nature of vision loss. In advanced/delayed presentations, the disc may progress to pallor/atrophy over subsequent weeks.

History
  • Directed questioning for jaw claudication (pain with chewing that improves with rest), scalp tenderness (pain brushing hair), new/different headache, and PMR symptoms (proximal muscle stiffness/aching, worse in the morning) — patients rarely volunteer these unless asked directly.
  • Any preceding episodes of transient monocular vision loss (amaurosis fugax), which can precede permanent vision loss in GCA.
Exam
  • Visual acuity (often profoundly reduced, counting fingers or worse), RAPD, and dilated fundus exam for the pale swollen disc appearance described above.
  • Temporal artery palpation for tenderness or decreased/absent pulse (present in some but not all cases; a normal temporal artery exam does not exclude GCA).
Imaging/Labs
  • STAT ESR, CRP, platelets, and CBC in every patient over 50 with acute vision loss, regardless of how confidently the presentation looks like non-arteritic AION or CRAO — occult/isolated ocular GCA without systemic symptoms is a documented blind spot. Concordant elevation of ESR and CRP yields high sensitivity for a positive temporal artery biopsy; thrombocytosis further supports the diagnosis.
  • Temporal artery biopsy or temporal artery ultrasound (looking for the 'halo sign') for definitive confirmation — but treatment should never be delayed for these results if clinical suspicion is present.
1
Apply the sudden vision loss differential framework immediately

Use the painful/painless, RAPD, fundus appearance, and monocular/binocular framework (see pathophysiology section) to rapidly narrow the differential and specifically flag any patient over 50 with painless monocular vision loss and a swollen disc as a GCA rule-out, regardless of how the rest of the picture looks.

⏰ Within the first minutes of evaluation
2
Send STAT inflammatory markers on every patient over 50 with acute vision loss

ESR, CRP, platelets, and CBC should be reflexively ordered in this population as part of the initial vision-loss workup, not as a secondary thought if the 'usual' embolic/ischemic workup is unrevealing.

⏰ Immediately, in parallel with other vision-loss workup
3
Start high-dose corticosteroids on clinical suspicion alone

Do not wait for ESR/CRP results, and do not wait for biopsy confirmation — begin treatment as soon as GCA is a reasonable clinical consideration, since the fellow eye can become involved within days if treatment is delayed, and bilateral vision loss from a missed diagnosis is the scenario every subsequent review will focus on.

⏰ Immediately on clinical suspicion, before any confirmatory test results
4
Arrange temporal artery biopsy without delaying treatment

Biopsy (or temporal artery ultrasound where available and validated locally) should be arranged promptly, ideally within 1-2 weeks of starting steroids, since biopsy sensitivity decreases the longer treatment has been running, but treatment itself should never be held pending this test.

⏰ Within 1-2 weeks of steroid initiation, without delaying the steroids themselves
5
Continue and taper corticosteroids per rheumatology guidance

Long-term steroid management (often many months, sometimes 1-2+ years) with a slow taper guided by symptoms and inflammatory markers, in coordination with rheumatology given the systemic disease and long-term steroid-related morbidity considerations.

⏰ Long-term, rheumatology-coordinated
6
Screen the fellow eye and counsel on urgency of new symptoms

Educate the patient explicitly that any new visual symptoms in the fellow eye, or recurrence of jaw claudication/headache/scalp tenderness, warrants immediate re-evaluation, since fellow-eye involvement is the feared complication of delayed or inadequately treated disease.

⏰ At diagnosis and throughout treatment

  • Bilateral vision loss if the fellow eye becomes involved before treatment is adequately established
  • Long-term corticosteroid-related morbidity (osteoporosis, diabetes, infection risk, cataracts) given the often prolonged treatment course
  • Large vessel complications of GCA itself: aortic aneurysm/dissection risk with long-standing disease, relevant to long-term surveillance
Clinical Pearls
  • Every patient over 50 with acute vision loss gets ESR/CRP/platelets sent as part of the initial workup, regardless of how confidently the presentation looks like 'routine' NAION or CRAO — isolated ocular GCA without systemic symptoms is a real and documented blind spot.
  • Treat first, biopsy second — this is one of the only diagnoses in ophthalmology where empiric treatment on clinical suspicion, before any confirmatory test, is the correct standard of care given the stakes of delay.
  • A normal temporal artery exam (no tenderness, normal pulse) does not exclude GCA — don't let a reassuring vessel exam talk you out of treating when the rest of the picture fits.
Evidence Notes
  • Concordant ESR and CRP elevation yields high sensitivity for temporal artery biopsy positivity in GCA, and thrombocytosis further supports the diagnosis, though a meaningful minority of biopsy-proven cases have been reported with normal inflammatory markers, meaning a normal ESR/CRP does not fully exclude the diagnosis when clinical suspicion remains high.

Papilledema

The term is a diagnosis of exclusion in disguise: 'papilledema' specifically means disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure, and the entire workup exists to prove that before treating it as such.

Papilledema refers specifically to optic disc swelling caused by raised intracranial pressure (as distinct from the more general term 'disc edema,' which can have many other causes) — axoplasmic flow stasis within optic nerve fibers, driven by transmitted CSF pressure along the optic nerve sheath, causes the nerve fibers themselves to swell. It is typically bilateral, though can be asymmetric. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH, formerly pseudotumor cerebri) is a common cause, particularly in young obese women, but papilledema always requires excluding a structural or vascular cause of elevated ICP (mass lesion, venous sinus thrombosis) before an idiopathic label is applied.

Risk Factors
  • For IIH specifically: female sex (dominant demographic, historically cited around 90% of patients), obesity, and recent significant weight gain
  • Medications associated with elevated ICP/pseudotumor-like presentations: tetracyclines (including doxycycline/minocycline), vitamin A/retinoids, growth hormone, and others
  • For secondary causes of elevated ICP generally: any intracranial mass lesion, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (particularly relevant in hypercoagulable states, pregnancy, or with a hormonal contraceptive history), and hydrocephalus
Fundoscopic Findings

Bilateral (though possibly asymmetric) optic disc edema, graded by the modified Frisén scale (0-5): swelling classically begins in the nasal and superior/inferior sectors (grade 1) before spreading to the temporal sector (grade 2), with higher grades showing progressively more disc elevation, blurred margins, and eventual obscuration of major vessels at the disc margin. Peripapillary hemorrhages and cotton-wool spots can accompany more severe grades. Optic atrophy and pallor can develop with chronic, longstanding, or inadequately treated papilledema, signaling optic nerve damage.

History
  • Headache pattern (often worse lying down or with Valsalva), pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations (brief, seconds-long graying/blacking of vision, often positional), and diplopia (from a CN VI palsy as a false localizing sign of elevated ICP, see that entry).
  • Medication history (tetracyclines, vitamin A/retinoids, growth hormone), recent significant weight gain, and menstrual/hormonal history relevant to IIH risk.
  • Symptoms suggesting an alternative secondary cause: focal neurologic deficits, recent infection, hypercoagulable risk factors, or pregnancy (relevant to venous sinus thrombosis risk).
Exam
  • Dilated fundus exam with Frisén grading; visual acuity is often preserved until relatively late, so a normal acuity does NOT exclude significant papilledema or a need for urgent workup.
  • Formal visual field testing (peripheral field loss, particularly enlarged blind spot, is often present even when central acuity is preserved) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) where available to quantify disc swelling objectively.
  • Full neurologic exam, specifically screening for a CN VI palsy (false localizing sign) and any focal deficit that would suggest a structural lesion rather than IIH.
Imaging/Labs
  • MRI brain (with MRV) is mandatory BEFORE lumbar puncture, to exclude a mass lesion (which would make LP dangerous) and cerebral venous sinus thrombosis specifically.
  • Lumbar puncture in the lateral decubitus position, with accurate opening pressure measurement (patient's legs should be extended, not flexed, to avoid falsely elevated readings), plus CSF studies (cell count, protein, glucose, cytology/infectious studies as clinically indicated) to exclude infectious/neoplastic/inflammatory causes of elevated ICP.
  • The modified Dandy criteria for IIH require: papilledema, normal neurologic exam except for cranial neuropathies directly attributable to elevated ICP (i.e., CN VI palsy), normal neuroimaging with no alternative explanation for elevated ICP, and an elevated CSF opening pressure with otherwise normal CSF composition.

5
Start acetazolamide and recommend weight loss for confirmed IIH

Acetazolamide, titrated based on symptom response and tolerability, is first-line medical therapy; the IIH Treatment Trial (IIHTT) demonstrated meaningfully greater improvement in visual field, papilledema grade (on fundus photography), and quality of life measures with acetazolamide plus a weight-management program compared to weight-management/lifestyle modification with placebo. A modest 5-10% body weight reduction is specifically associated with meaningful clinical improvement.

⏰ At diagnosis, titrated over subsequent visits based on response and tolerability
6
Reserve surgical intervention for vision-threatening or medically refractory disease

Optic nerve sheath fenestration (ONSF) or CSF shunting (ventriculoperitoneal or lumboperitoneal) is considered for progressive vision loss despite maximal medical therapy, or for very severe presentations at initial diagnosis; venous sinus stenting is an evolving option specifically for patients with a demonstrated significant venous sinus stenosis contributing to the elevated pressure.

⏰ For medically refractory disease or urgent vision-threatening presentations
7
Treat any identified secondary cause directly

Venous sinus thrombosis requires anticoagulation (per standard VTE/thrombosis management) rather than standard IIH therapy alone; a mass lesion requires its own dedicated management pathway.

⏰ As soon as a secondary cause is identified
🔧 Procedure: Lumbar Puncture for Opening Pressure Measurement
  1. Confirm MRI (with MRV) has already excluded a mass lesion and venous sinus thrombosis before proceeding.
  2. Position the patient in the lateral decubitus position with legs extended (not flexed to the chest), since flexed positioning falsely elevates the measured opening pressure.
  3. Measure opening pressure with a manometer before any CSF is withdrawn for studies.
  4. Send CSF for cell count, protein, glucose, and any additional studies indicated by the clinical picture (infectious, cytology).
Contraindications:
  • Any mass lesion or condition causing a risk of herniation with CSF removal — this is precisely why MRI must precede LP in this workup.

CN III (Oculomotor) Palsy

The classic 'rule of the pupil' still organizes the initial approach, but current data show it's imperfect enough in both directions that pupil status alone shouldn't be the final word on whether to image.

The oculomotor nerve supplies the superior, inferior, and medial rectus muscles, the inferior oblique, and the levator palpebrae superioris, plus parasympathetic fibers to the pupillary sphincter and ciliary muscle. A complete palsy produces the classic 'down and out' eye position (from unopposed lateral rectus and superior oblique action), ptosis, and variable pupillary involvement. The parasympathetic pupillary fibers run superficially on the dorsomedial surface of the nerve, supplied by pial vessels, making them disproportionately vulnerable to external compression (aneurysm, tumor) but relatively protected from the internal microvascular ischemia (vasa vasorum territory) that affects the deeper somatic motor fibers — this is the anatomic basis of the classic 'rule of the pupil,' though it is not absolute: prospective data show ischemic lesions can involve the pupil in up to roughly 20% of cases, and early compressive lesions can spare it.

Risk Factors
  • Microvascular/ischemic: hypertension, diabetes, atherosclerotic disease, age over 50 — the classic 'vasculopathic' risk profile
  • Compressive: posterior communicating artery (PCom) aneurysm is the classic and most feared compressive cause, along with other intracranial aneurysms, tumors, and rarely uncal herniation
  • Giant cell arteritis should be considered in patients over 50, alongside the standard vasculopathic risk factor assessment
  • Trauma, and (rarely) inflammatory or infectious causes
Fundoscopic Findings

Not directly affected by an isolated CN III palsy itself, since the disease affects the nerve, not the retina or optic nerve head. Fundus exam remains relevant as part of a complete neuro-ophthalmic exam to exclude a coexisting or alternative process (e.g., papilledema suggesting elevated ICP as a unifying explanation in a complex presentation).

History
  • Onset (sudden onset and severe pain favor aneurysmal compression, though pain is a poor discriminator overall since it occurs in both ischemic and aneurysmal etiologies and does not reliably predict the underlying pathology), and vasculopathic risk factor history (diabetes, hypertension).
  • Age (under 50 substantially lowers the likelihood of microvascular ischemic etiology and raises relative concern for aneurysm even without pupil involvement).
Exam
  • Document the completeness of the palsy (complete vs. partial/incomplete, since a partial palsy that spares some muscles can still represent early evolving aneurysmal compression even with a normal pupil).
  • Careful pupil exam: compare size and reactivity between the two eyes; note that even 'pupil-sparing' by strict definition can include up to roughly 2mm of anisocoria in some microvascular cases, so precise measurement matters more than a binary 'normal/abnormal' impression.
  • Assess for other neurologic signs suggesting a more complex/central process (additional cranial neuropathies, long tract signs) that would push toward urgent MRI regardless of pupil status.
Imaging/Labs
  • Emergent CT and CT angiography (or MRI/MRA) of the brain to exclude a compressive aneurysm in ANY patient with a pupil-involving third nerve palsy — this is a time-critical medical emergency, not a routine outpatient referral.
  • Current practice increasingly recommends neuroimaging (MRI with gadolinium, ideally with dedicated thin-cut sequences) for essentially ALL patients with a new third nerve palsy regardless of pupillary status, given prospective data showing up to 16.5% of 'presumed microvascular' (pupil-sparing, vasculopathic-risk-factor-positive, over-50) palsies actually harbor a non-ischemic cause (midbrain infarct, tumor, inflammation) on subsequent workup.
  • ESR/CRP in patients over 50 to screen for GCA as a contributing/alternative diagnosis.
  • For patients under 50 with an isolated, non-pupil-involving palsy, imaging or intracranial angiography is still indicated given the lower baseline likelihood of microvascular disease in this age group.
1
Treat any pupil-involving third nerve palsy as an emergency

Emergent CT/CTA (or MRI/MRA) to exclude a compressive aneurysm; this should happen the same day/immediately, not on an outpatient timeline, given the risk of aneurysmal rupture/subarachnoid hemorrhage.

⏰ Immediately, same-day emergent imaging
2
Image essentially all new third nerve palsies given the imperfect 'rule of the pupil'

Current recommendations favor MRI with gadolinium (thin-cut sequences through the course of the nerve where available) for most new presentations, even those that appear classically pupil-sparing and vasculopathic in an older patient, given the meaningful rate of missed non-ischemic pathology in this 'presumed microvascular' population.

⏰ At or shortly after initial presentation, even for apparently lower-risk presentations
3
Pay special attention to partial/incomplete palsies

A partial palsy sparing some muscles, even with a normal pupil, deserves particular attention since this pattern is more likely to represent early evolving aneurysmal compression that simply hasn't yet involved the pupillary fibers; close interval re-examination (watching specifically for delayed pupil involvement, which can lag by 5-7 days) is warranted if imaging is initially unrevealing.

⏰ At initial presentation and on close interval follow-up
4
Screen for GCA in patients over 50

Send ESR/CRP given giant cell arteritis is a recognized, potentially treatable cause/contributor in this age group.

⏰ Same visit, in patients over 50
5
Manage confirmed microvascular ischemic palsy with risk factor optimization and observation

Once aneurysm and other structural causes are excluded, optimize vasculopathic risk factors (blood pressure, glucose control) and observe; the majority of microvascular CN III palsies resolve spontaneously over roughly 3 months.

⏰ Ongoing over the expected 3-month recovery window
6
Re-image if there is no improvement or evidence of aberrant regeneration

Failure to improve over 6-8 weeks, or the development of aberrant regeneration (a sign more typical of compressive or traumatic, not ischemic, injury), should prompt repeat/expanded imaging to reconsider a structural cause.

⏰ At 6-8 weeks if no improvement, or sooner if aberrant regeneration develops

Follow-upClose interval follow-up (days to a couple of weeks) for any presumed microvascular palsy to watch for delayed pupil involvement or lack of expected improvement; routine follow-up through the expected ~3-month recovery course otherwise.
Complications to Anticipate
  • Subarachnoid hemorrhage/aneurysm rupture if a compressive lesion is missed and left untreated
  • Aberrant regeneration (misdirected nerve fiber regrowth causing unusual co-contraction patterns, e.g., lid elevation with attempted downgaze) — more typical of compressive or traumatic injury than true ischemic palsy, and its presence should prompt reconsideration of the ischemic diagnosis
  • Persistent diplopia/strabismus requiring later strabismus surgery in a minority of cases that don't fully resolve
Clinical Pearls
  • The 'rule of the pupil' is a useful organizing framework, not an absolute rule — up to 20% of ischemic palsies can show some pupil involvement, and early aneurysmal compression can spare the pupil entirely, sometimes not manifesting pupil involvement until 5-7 days later.
  • A partial/incomplete palsy with a normal pupil is not necessarily reassuring — this pattern is specifically associated with early, evolving aneurysmal compression and deserves close follow-up or low-threshold imaging.
  • Prospective data showing up to 16.5% of 'presumed microvascular' palsies harbor non-ischemic pathology has genuinely shifted practice toward imaging essentially all new third nerve palsies, not just the pupil-involving ones.
Evidence Notes
  • Prospective studies have found that up to 16.5% of patients with clinically 'presumed microvascular' (pupil-sparing, vasculopathic risk factors, age over 50) third nerve palsies actually harbored non-ischemic pathology (midbrain infarcts, tumors, inflammation) on subsequent workup, supporting broader neuroimaging recommendations regardless of pupillary status.

CN IV (Trochlear) Palsy

The most common cause of vertical/torsional diplopia in adults, localized with a three-step bedside test that most clinicians remember imperfectly — here's the version that actually holds up.

The trochlear nerve is anatomically unique: it is the only cranial nerve to exit the dorsal (back) surface of the brainstem, and the only one that decussates (crosses to the opposite side) before reaching its target muscle. It supplies a single muscle, the superior oblique, whose primary actions are intorsion (inward rotation) and, secondarily, depression of the eye (particularly in adduction). Its long, thin intracranial course makes it disproportionately vulnerable to closed head trauma even at a distance from the nerve itself, which is why trauma is a leading cause of acquired (non-congenital) trochlear palsy.

Risk Factors
  • Congenital trochlear nerve absence/hypoplasia (a surprisingly common cause of what presents as "acquired" palsy in adulthood, often decompensating and becoming symptomatic later in life after years of subclinical compensation; imaging studies have found trochlear nerve absence in a majority of patients with congenital superior oblique palsy)
  • Head trauma, even relatively minor or at a distance from the nerve itself, given its long intracranial course and dorsal exit point
  • Microvascular ischemic risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis) for acquired ischemic palsy in older patients
  • Rarely: tumor, aneurysm (extremely rare for this nerve specifically), demyelinating disease, myasthenia gravis, GCA, and other inflammatory/autoimmune conditions
Fundoscopic Findings

Excyclotorsion of the affected eye may be visible on fundus exam as an abnormally low position of the fovea relative to the optic disc (loss of the normal intorsion provided by an intact superior oblique) — a useful confirmatory sign when the diagnosis is otherwise uncertain, though not routinely required if the three-step test is clearly positive.

History
  • Vertical, torsional, or oblique diplopia, characteristically worse on downward gaze (e.g., reading, walking down stairs) and gaze away from the affected side.
  • Any preceding head trauma, however minor.
  • A compensatory head tilt is a classic associated finding, typically away from the affected side in the majority of cases (though a paradoxical ipsilateral tilt occurs in a minority, particularly in congenital cases where the tilt reflects the patient's individual strategy for managing diplopia rather than a fixed rule).
Exam
  • The Parks-Bielschowsky-Helveston three-step test: (1) identify which eye is hypertropic (higher) in primary gaze; (2) determine whether the hypertropia worsens in gaze to the right or left; (3) determine whether the hypertropia worsens with head tilt to the right or left shoulder (the Bielschowsky head tilt test specifically). A trochlear palsy shows worsening hypertropia on head tilt TOWARD the affected side and gaze AWAY from the affected side.
  • Assess for a compensatory head tilt in the patient's resting posture, and examine old photographs (driver's license, family photos) where available — a longstanding head tilt visible in old photos strongly supports a congenital rather than acquired etiology, which meaningfully changes the urgency of further workup.
  • Full neurologic exam to exclude other cranial neuropathies suggesting a more complex central process.
Imaging/Labs
  • MRI brain is warranted for new acquired palsies, particularly to assess the brainstem/nerve course and exclude tumor or demyelinating disease, though the yield is lower than for CN III given the rarity of aneurysmal compression of this specific nerve.
  • ESR/CRP in patients over 50 to screen for GCA as a contributing cause.
  • Old photographs or a clear history of lifelong intermittent symptoms can obviate the need for extensive new workup if a congenital etiology is strongly supported.

⏰ At initial history-taking

Particularly relevant to exclude tumor or demyelinating disease; the threshold for imaging is somewhat lower in younger patients or those without clear trauma or vasculopathic risk factors.

Send ESR/CRP given the recognized association, alongside standard vasculopathic risk factor assessment.

⏰ Same visit, in patients over 50
5

Options include occlusion of one eye (patching) for symptomatic diplopia relief, or prism glasses to fuse the images, while awaiting the expected spontaneous resolution over weeks to a few months typical of microvascular ischemic palsies.

For palsies that don't resolve (more typical of congenital/traumatic causes) or cause persistent bothersome diplopia/head tilt, strabismus surgery (e.g., inferior oblique myectomy or other muscle procedures) is a reasonable elective option, with meaningfully better long-term motor and head-tilt outcomes reported when a trochlear nerve is still anatomically present versus congenitally absent.

⏰ Elective, for persistent/non-resolving symptomatic cases

CN VI (Abducens) Palsy

This is the classic 'false localizing sign' of neurology — a nerve palsy that can mean nothing more than generically elevated intracranial pressure anywhere in the skull, not a lesion actually located along the nerve's course.

The abducens nerve supplies a single muscle, the lateral rectus, responsible for abducting the eye. It has the longest intracranial course of any cranial nerve, traveling from its pontine origin along the clivus and through Dorello's canal before entering the cavernous sinus — this long course, particularly the segment tightly tethered against the clivus, makes it exquisitely vulnerable to stretch injury from any process that causes diffuse elevation of intracranial pressure or downward brainstem displacement, REGARDLESS of where the actual causative lesion is located. This is the basis for CN VI palsy being the classic 'false localizing sign': its presence doesn't localize disease to the nerve's actual anatomic path, but rather can simply reflect generically raised ICP from a lesion located anywhere in the cranium.

Risk Factors
  • Elevated intracranial pressure from any cause (papilledema/IIH, mass lesion, hydrocephalus) as the classic 'false localizing' mechanism
  • Microvascular ischemic risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, atherosclerosis) for isolated ischemic palsy in older patients, analogous to microvascular CN III and CN IV palsy
  • Trauma, particularly skull base fractures
  • GCA in patients over 50
  • In children specifically: pontine glioma and other posterior fossa tumors are important considerations for a new CN VI palsy and warrant a lower threshold for imaging
Fundoscopic Findings

Not directly affected by the CN VI palsy itself, but fundus exam is essential as part of the same visit to look for papilledema, since elevated ICP is a leading mechanism for this specific nerve palsy (see pathophysiology above) — finding papilledema alongside a CN VI palsy supports elevated ICP as the underlying explanation and should prompt the same urgent workup as papilledema presenting alone.

History
  • Binocular horizontal diplopia (images side by side, not vertically displaced), characteristically worse looking toward the affected side and at distance rather than near.
  • Headache, pulsatile tinnitus, or transient visual obscurations suggesting coexisting elevated ICP.
  • Trauma history, and in children, developmental/growth concerns or other symptoms suggesting a posterior fossa tumor.
Exam
  • Confirm isolated lateral rectus weakness (impaired abduction of the affected eye) without other cranial nerve or motor findings, which would suggest a more isolated, peripheral nerve process rather than a diffuse or brainstem process.
  • Full fundus exam specifically for papilledema.
  • Complete cranial nerve and neurologic exam to detect any additional deficits suggesting a brainstem, cavernous sinus, or skull base process rather than an isolated microvascular mononeuropathy.
Imaging/Labs
  • MRI brain (with attention to the brainstem, clivus, and cavernous sinus) is generally warranted for new isolated CN VI palsy, particularly in patients without clear vasculopathic risk factors, in children, or with any additional neurologic findings.
  • Consider the same papilledema/elevated ICP workup (MRI/MRV, lumbar puncture with opening pressure) if papilledema or other ICP-elevation symptoms are present alongside the palsy.
  • ESR/CRP in patients over 50 to screen for GCA.

Horner Syndrome

An acute, painful Horner syndrome after minor neck trauma is carotid dissection until neuroimaging says otherwise — a diagnosis where the eye finding is often the first clue to a life-threatening vascular process elsewhere.

Horner syndrome results from interruption of the three-neuron oculosympathetic pathway at any point along its course, producing the classic triad of ptosis (mild, from loss of Müller's muscle tone), miosis (from unopposed parasympathetic pupillary constriction), and anhidrosis (facial, variable depending on lesion location). The pathway's three orders are anatomically and clinically distinct: first-order neurons run from the hypothalamus through the brainstem and cervical spinal cord (lesions here are classically central, e.g., lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome); second-order neurons exit the spinal cord and travel over the lung apex before reaching the superior cervical ganglion (lesions here classically involve Pancoast tumor or iatrogenic injury, e.g., chest tube or neck surgery); third-order neurons travel from the superior cervical ganglion along the internal carotid artery through the cavernous sinus to the eye (lesions here classically involve carotid dissection, aneurysm, or cavernous sinus pathology).

Risk Factors
  • For third-order (postganglionic) Horner: carotid artery dissection (can follow relatively minor trauma or occur spontaneously, particularly with connective tissue disease such as Ehlers-Danlos or Marfan syndrome, or longstanding hypertension), carotid aneurysm, and cavernous sinus pathology
  • For second-order Horner: apical lung lesions (Pancoast tumor), and iatrogenic causes including chest tube placement, neck surgery, and central line placement (particularly internal jugular catheterization)
  • For first-order Horner: brainstem stroke (classically lateral medullary/Wallenberg syndrome), multiple sclerosis, and other central lesions along the hypothalamospinal pathway
  • Neuroblastoma is an important consideration in the pediatric population, where Horner syndrome can be an early presenting sign
Fundoscopic Findings

Not applicable; Horner syndrome affects the pupil and lid via the sympathetic pathway and does not produce fundus findings itself, though a full exam remains appropriate to exclude a coexisting unrelated process.

History
  • Pain specifically: acute Horner syndrome WITH pain (headache, neck or facial pain) should be treated as carotid dissection until proven otherwise, particularly following even minor recent neck trauma (including chiropractic manipulation) or in patients with connective tissue disease risk factors.
  • Associated symptoms suggesting a specific localization: ipsilateral shoulder/arm pain or hand weakness (apex of lung, brachial plexus, second-order lesion), or other brainstem symptoms (first-order lesion).
  • Tobacco use history (relevant to lung apex lesion risk).
  • In children: any history suggesting neuroblastoma should be pursued (abdominal mass, systemic symptoms).
Exam
  • Confirm the triad: mild ptosis (typically 1-2mm, less than the more complete ptosis of a CN III palsy), miosis (worse in dim light, since the affected pupil fails to dilate normally), and assess for facial anhidrosis (its presence/absence and distribution can itself provide localizing information, since second- and third-order lesions affect different facial sweating distributions after the sympathetic fibers diverge along the external vs. internal carotid arteries).
  • "Upside-down ptosis": a subtle elevation of the lower lid can accompany the upper lid ptosis, from loss of sympathetic tone to the analogous smooth muscle of the lower lid.
  • Distinguish from physiologic anisocoria (common, benign pupil size difference without ptosis or other findings) using pharmacologic testing when the diagnosis is uncertain on exam alone.
Imaging/Labs
  • Pharmacologic confirmation: topical apraclonidine 0.5-1% is now the preferred test of choice given practicality (cocaine is difficult to obtain) — instilled in both eyes, a POSITIVE test shows the Horner pupil dilating (denervation supersensitivity to the alpha-1 agonist effect) while the normal pupil shows little change or even mild constriction, producing a 'reversal' of the anisocoria. Cocaine testing (where available) instead shows the normal pupil dilating while the Horner pupil fails to dilate (anisocoria of 0.8mm or more considered diagnostic).
  • Hydroxyamphetamine can further localize a confirmed Horner to pre- vs. post-ganglionic (third-order) location, though it is used less often in current practice and, if cocaine was used first, requires waiting at least 72 hours before testing given pharmacologic interference.
  • Except in true emergencies, pharmacologic localization should ideally precede neuroimaging in adults to help direct which region to image; however, in an ACUTE, PAINFUL presentation with concern for carotid dissection, do not delay neuroimaging (CT angiography or MR angiography of the head/neck) for pharmacologic testing — imaging takes priority given the time-sensitive, life-threatening nature of dissection.
  • MRI/MRA or CTA of the head and neck, with attention to the carotid artery course, is preferred over ultrasound when carotid dissection is a possibility, given superior sensitivity for this diagnosis.

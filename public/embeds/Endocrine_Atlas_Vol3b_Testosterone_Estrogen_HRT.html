Peak Ages & Decline Trajectories

Testosterone fades gradually over decades; estrogen falls off a cliff over a few years. That single difference in shape explains most of why male and female hormone replacement are managed so differently.

TestosteroneA Gradual Slope, Not a Cliff

Life stageWhat's happening
Late teens–mid 20sLifetime peak — total testosterone typically averages roughly 600–700 ng/dL in this window, reflecting full HPG axis maturation from puberty (see Volume 1)
~Age 30–40Total testosterone begins a slow decline, averaging about 1–2% per year in population studies — though a well-validated normative model found no significant further decline in mean total testosterone from age 40 onward in men without comorbidity, with the dominant age-related change instead being increasing variance between individuals
Age 50+By some population estimates, average total testosterone has fallen roughly 20–30% from peak by age 50 — but free testosterone declines proportionally more than total, because SHBG rises steadily with age (see Volume 1 Foundational Principles for SHBG regulation), binding a larger share of whatever testosterone remains
The honest state of the evidence
Much of the widely-repeated "1–2% per year forever" framing comes from population averages that mix healthy and comorbid men together — comorbidity (obesity, diabetes, chronic illness), not aging itself, drives a large share of the observed decline. A peer-reviewed normative model (Bhasin et al./related lifespan modeling work) found testosterone plateaus in mean value after 40 in healthy men, with widening individual variability being the more accurate description than a uniform downward slope. This matters clinically: an older man with a "low-normal" level and no symptoms may simply be within his own healthy variance, not necessarily hypogonadal.

Diagnostic threshold: Hypogonadism is defined by symptoms plus confirmed low testosterone (typically <300 ng/dL) on two separate early-morning draws (testosterone follows a circadian rhythm, peaking ~8am and falling ~15–25% by afternoon — an afternoon draw can create a false-low result) — treatment decisions are never made on a single lab value or on levels alone without symptoms.

EstrogenA Cliff, Not a Slope

Life stageWhat's happening
Reproductive years (20s–30s)Estradiol cycles between roughly 30–400+ pg/mL across the menstrual cycle (see Volume 1 female HPG axis) — this is the body's peak lifetime estrogen exposure window
Perimenopause (typically mid-to-late 40s)Increasingly erratic cycles as remaining ovarian follicles become less responsive — estradiol can swing unpredictably, sometimes higher than baseline before the eventual decline, which is part of why perimenopausal symptoms can paradoxically include both estrogen-excess and estrogen-deficiency features at different points
Menopause (average age ~51 in the US)Defined retrospectively as 12 consecutive months without a period. Estradiol falls sharply over a relatively short window (in contrast to testosterone's decades-long slope) to sustained postmenopausal levels typically <20 pg/mL

This is the single biggest structural difference between the two hormone systems covered in this file: testosterone decline is gradual and variable enough that "when did it start" is often unanswerable for an individual patient, while estrogen decline is abrupt enough that menopause has a definable date.

Verify before relying
Population reference ranges vary meaningfully by assay platform and lab — always interpret an individual patient's result against their specific lab's reference range, not the general figures above.

Testosterone Replacement Therapy

A staged, multi-month timeline of effects, a 2023 cardiovascular safety trial that reshaped the conversation, and the fertility trade-off built directly into the HPG axis physiology from Volume 1.

OnsetHow Long Does It Actually Take to Work?

The most citable source on this exact question is a 2011 systematic review (Saad et al., European Journal of Endocrinology) pooling time-course data across testosterone trials — the timeline below is drawn from it.

EffectFirst detectableMaximum effect
Sexual interest/libido~3 weeksPlateaus ~6 weeks — little further gain beyond this point
Quality of life / general well-being3–4 weeksLonger, variable
Depressive mood symptoms3–6 weeks18–30 weeks
Erectile function / ejaculationSlower than libido — meaningful change can take up to 6 months3–6 months typical; some men up to ~1 year
Erythropoiesis (hematocrit/hemoglobin)~3 months9–12 months
Body composition / muscle mass~3 months (measurable strength/lean mass change)6–12 months
PSA / prostate volumeMarginal risePlateaus ~12 months — a further rise after that point warrants prostate evaluation, not just attribution to TRT
Bone mineral densitySlowest of all — not the primary driver of a treatment decision on the short-to-medium termMulti-year
Why this staged timeline matters practically
A patient who feels little different at 2 weeks isn't a treatment failure — libido is typically the earliest-responding domain (~3 weeks), while the changes patients often most want (muscle, energy stability, sexual function) take months. This is also why the standard practice is rechecking labs and symptoms at 6–8 weeks rather than 1–2 weeks — some studies suggest 25–38% of men need a dose adjustment at that first follow-up, precisely because the early window doesn't yet reflect the eventual steady-state response.

FormulationsRoute Options

Intramuscular injection

Testosterone cypionate/enanthate, typically weekly-to-biweekly. Produces peak-and-trough serum levels rather than steady state — some men notice symptom fluctuation across the injection cycle (energy/mood dipping before the next dose).

Transdermal (gel/patch)

Daily application, more stable steady-state levels than injections. Requires counseling on transfer risk to partners/children via skin contact — a real and clinically relevant consideration unique to this route.

Subcutaneous pellets

Implanted every 3–6 months — convenient adherence profile, but dosing can't be adjusted once placed, and minor procedural risks (infection, extrusion) apply.

Oral testosterone undecanoate

Newer oral formulations absorbed via the lymphatic system largely bypass first-pass hepatic metabolism — historically, oral testosterone was avoided due to the hepatotoxicity of older 17α-alkylated oral androgens, a concern this lymphatic-absorption formulation largely sidesteps.

MonitoringWhat to Track and When

Testosterone level — recheck at 6–8 weeks, then periodically Hematocrit/hemoglobin — baseline, then at 3–6 months (erythrocytosis is the most common dose-limiting side effect) PSA — baseline and per shared decision-making in appropriate age groups Lipid panel

Cardiovascular SafetyThe TRAVERSE Trial

For over a decade, TRT's cardiovascular safety was genuinely unsettled, resting on underpowered trials and conflicting observational data. The TRAVERSE trial (NEJM, 2023) — an FDA-mandated, adequately powered, placebo-controlled trial of over 5,000 hypogonadal men with existing cardiovascular disease or elevated cardiovascular risk — found testosterone therapy was non-inferior to placebo for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), providing the first solid reassurance in this specific higher-risk population.

Reassuring on MACE, but not a completely clean result
Despite the reassuring primary MACE finding, secondary safety signals in TRAVERSE (including higher rates of atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury, and venous thromboembolism in some analyses) mean this isn't an unqualified "all-clear" — ongoing monitoring and shared decision-making remain appropriate, and the trial's reassurance applies specifically to men who resemble its population (middle-aged/older, confirmed hypogonadism, elevated cardiovascular risk) rather than as a blanket statement for all possible TRT use cases.

Fertility Trade-offThe HPG Axis Cost of Treatment

As established in Volume 1's male HPG axis section, exogenous testosterone suppresses GnRH/LH/FSH via the same negative feedback the axis uses for endogenous testosterone — the axis can't tell the difference. Practical consequence: TRT reliably suppresses spermatogenesis and shrinks testicular volume over time (Leydig cells go relatively quiescent from LH withdrawal). For men who want to preserve fertility while treating symptomatic hypogonadism, alternatives that stimulate rather than replace the axis are used instead — hCG (mimics LH directly), clomiphene/enclomiphene (blocks estrogen negative feedback at the hypothalamus, raising endogenous LH/FSH), or gonadorelin — sometimes combined with low-dose testosterone.

ContraindicationsWhen Not to Start

Known or suspected prostate cancer Breast cancer in men Untreated severe obstructive sleep apnea Uncontrolled/untreated erythrocytosis Desire for near-term fertility (without concurrent fertility-preserving therapy) Severe untreated heart failure

Estrogen Replacement / Menopausal HRT

The single most consequential fact in this section: route of administration changes the risk profile as much as, or more than, the decision to treat at all.

IndicationsWhat HRT Is Actually For

Vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats) — the best-established indication Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — see dedicated Vaginal Estrogen section Osteoporosis prevention in appropriate candidates Premature/early menopause — replacement toward the age of natural menopause is generally favorable given the extended deficiency window

Route MattersOral vs. Transdermal

OutcomeOral estrogenTransdermal estrogen
VTE riskMeaningfully elevated (pooled RR ~1.9 vs. non-users) — oral estrogen undergoes hepatic first-pass metabolism that increases thrombin generation and induces resistance to activated protein CEssentially not elevated (pooled RR ~1.0) — bypasses hepatic first-pass, minimal effect on clotting factor synthesis
Stroke riskModestly elevatedNot clearly elevated in current evidence
Lipid/bone/glucose effectsBroadly similar between routesBroadly similar between routes
Why this changes practice
Current guidance (including UK NICE 2024) specifically favors transdermal estrogen for women at elevated VTE risk — obesity (BMI >30), prior VTE, known thrombophilia, migraine with aura, or smokers. The mechanistic explanation (first-pass hepatic effect vs. bypassing it) is exactly the kind of route-dependent pharmacology introduced generally in Volume 3a's Routes section, applied here to a different hormone class with the same underlying logic: hepatic first-pass metabolism isn't just about drug potency, it can alter which proteins the liver is induced to make.

The Timing HypothesisWhy the WHI Findings Don't Apply Uniformly

The Women's Health Initiative (WHI) — the trial responsible for decades of clinical caution around HRT — enrolled women with an average age of 63, many more than 10 years past menopause onset. Its finding of increased cardiovascular events does not generalize cleanly to a 50-year-old starting HRT at the onset of menopause.

PopulationCardiovascular signal
HRT started within 10 years of menopause or before age 60Trend toward reduced coronary heart disease risk (estrogen-only: HR ~0.59; combined estrogen-progestin: HR ~0.76, per WHI age-stratified reanalysis)
HRT started more than 10 years after menopause or after age 60Neutral-to-increased cardiovascular risk — proposed mechanism: in older arteries with established (often unstable, non-calcified) atherosclerotic plaque, estrogen may destabilize plaque rather than provide the endothelial benefit seen in younger, healthier vasculature

This "timing hypothesis" / "window of opportunity" is now well-supported across the reanalyzed WHI data, the KEEPS trial, and the ELITE trial. The Endocrine Society has explicitly stated that WHI-derived risk estimates (average age 63) should not be used to counsel a 50-year-old starting HRT at menopause onset.

ProgestogenWhy It's Needed — And When It Isn't

Unopposed estrogen drives endometrial proliferation and raises endometrial cancer risk in a woman with an intact uterus — a progestogen (progesterone or a synthetic progestin) must be co-administered to protect the endometrium. Women who have had a hysterectomy do not need a progestogen at all — estrogen-only therapy is used, which is also why WHI's two arms (estrogen-only vs. combined estrogen-progestin) showed somewhat different risk profiles, with estrogen-only generally appearing more favorable on breast cancer risk specifically.

Not all progestogens behave the same
Micronized progesterone (structurally identical to endogenous progesterone) appears to carry a more favorable cardiovascular and breast-tissue risk profile than synthetic progestins like medroxyprogesterone acetate (the specific progestin used in the WHI trial) — part of why some of the WHI's risk signal may reflect that specific progestin choice rather than progestogen therapy as a class.

Breast Cancer RiskDuration-Dependent, Not Immediate

Large cohort data show breast cancer risk is not significantly increased with less than 5 years of oral or transdermal estradiol use — risk rises significantly only with more than 5 years of use (standardized incidence ratio ~1.4 across estradiol, estriol, and vaginal estrogen preparations in one large cohort, though vaginal-only use trends lower — see the dedicated Vaginal Estrogen section for that distinction). This duration-dependence is a genuinely useful counseling point: short-term use for symptom control carries a different risk calculus than decade-plus continuous use.

ContraindicationsWhen Not to Start Systemic HRT

Personal history of breast cancer Unexplained vaginal bleeding (evaluate first) Active VTE or known thrombophilia (consider transdermal only, with specialist input) Active or history of estrogen-sensitive cancer Active liver disease Prior stroke or MI (relative, individualized)

Vaginal (Local) Estrogen

A fundamentally different risk category from systemic HRT — worth its own section specifically because it's so often lumped in with systemic therapy in patients' (and sometimes clinicians') minds.

IndicationGenitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

Vaginal atrophy, dryness, dyspareunia, and recurrent UTIs from estrogen-deficient urogenital tissue — distinct from vasomotor symptoms, and often persisting even in women who don't want or can't use systemic HRT. Local estrogen directly reverses the tissue-level atrophy without needing to raise circulating estrogen to premenopausal levels.

FormulationsCream, Tablet, Ring

FormulationNotes
Vaginal creamDosing flexibility, but less precise/consistent dosing than tablet or ring
Vaginal tablet (e.g., 10mcg estradiol)Ultra-low-dose formulations show minimal systemic absorption in pharmacokinetic studies — among the lowest systemic exposure of the available options
Vaginal ring3-month duration, consistent low-dose release, good adherence profile
Vaginal softgel capsule (newer formulations)Some newer low-dose formulations report up to 3x lower systemic estrogen levels than an approved low-dose vaginal tablet, reflecting continued formulation refinement toward minimizing systemic exposure

Systemic AbsorptionGenuinely Minimal at Standard Low Doses

At the low doses formulated specifically for local GSM treatment, systemic estradiol absorption is minimal — meaningfully different from the systemic exposure produced by oral or transdermal HRT covered in the previous section. This is the mechanistic basis for essentially everything else in this section.

Endometrial SafetyProgestogen Generally Not Required

Because systemic absorption is minimal at standard low doses, low-dose vaginal estrogen has not been associated with increased endometrial cancer risk even in women with an intact uterus — unlike systemic unopposed estrogen (see previous section), routine progestogen co-therapy is generally not required for low-dose vaginal preparations. This is a frequently misunderstood point in practice, since the "estrogen needs a progestogen if the uterus is intact" rule from systemic HRT doesn't carry over to the local, low-dose vaginal formulations.

Cardiovascular & Cancer RiskLarge Cohort Reassurance

Large postmenopausal cohort data (including WHI observational sub-analyses) found no significantly elevated risk of stroke, invasive breast cancer, colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, or VTE/pulmonary embolism among vaginal estrogen users compared to non-users — with some analyses even showing lower coronary heart disease, fracture, and all-cause mortality in users (though this likely partly reflects healthy-user selection rather than a proven protective causal effect).

Breast Cancer SurvivorsA Genuinely Nuanced Answer

PopulationCurrent evidence
Hormone receptor-negative tumorsLow-dose vaginal estrogen has not been associated with increased recurrence or mortality
Hormone receptor-positive, on tamoxifenSimilarly, no demonstrated increase in recurrence or mortality in current evidence
Hormone receptor-positive, on an aromatase inhibitorNo increased mortality signal, but some studies report a higher recurrence risk that remains controversial and unresolved — this is the one genuinely unsettled corner of the evidence base, plausibly because aromatase inhibitors already suppress estrogen to near-zero, so even minimal vaginal absorption represents a larger relative increase in local/systemic estrogen exposure than it would in a woman not on an AI
Practical takeaway
Low-dose vaginal estrogen is broadly considered a reasonable option for breast cancer survivors with GSM that hasn't responded to non-hormonal measures — but the aromatase-inhibitor subgroup specifically warrants shared decision-making with the patient's oncologist given the unresolved recurrence-risk signal, rather than treating this as settled across all breast cancer survivor subgroups uniformly.

Laboratory Values — Free T & Estrogen

The assay method matters as much as the number itself for both hormones — ordering the wrong test, or misreading which test you got back, is a common and consequential error.

Total TestosteroneThe Starting Point

Standard first-line test — must be drawn early morning (before 10am) given the circadian pattern discussed in the Peak Ages section, and confirmed on a second morning draw before diagnosing hypogonadism. Total testosterone includes SHBG-bound, albumin-bound, and free fractions together (see Volume 1 Foundational Principles for the general binding-protein framework) — which is exactly why it can be misleading when SHBG itself is abnormal.

Free TestosteroneThree Methods, Three Very Different Numbers

MethodAccuracyPractical use
Equilibrium dialysisGold standard — physically separates and measures the unbound fraction directlyNot widely available, slower turnaround, higher cost — reserved for cases where precision genuinely changes management
Direct analog immunoassay ("free testosterone, direct" on most commercial lab reports)Explicitly called unreliable by the Endocrine Society's 2018 guideline — systematically underestimates free testosterone and correlates poorly with equilibrium dialysis, yet remains the most commonly ordered method in the US purely due to cost and turnaround timeInterpret with real caution; a "normal" direct free T does not reliably rule out clinically low free testosterone
Calculated free testosterone (Vermeulen equation, from total T + SHBG ± albumin)Correlates strongly with equilibrium dialysis (r ≈ 0.97–0.99 in validation studies) — the Vermeulen formula does tend to overestimate free T by ~19% compared to equilibrium dialysis, but does so consistently across a wide range of SHBG/albumin/testosterone values, which is exactly what makes it clinically dependable despite the systematic offsetGenerally preferred over direct immunoassay by endocrinology guidelines when SHBG is abnormal or the clinical picture is borderline — requires only a total testosterone and SHBG (± albumin) to calculate
The single most common ordering error
A clinician who orders "free testosterone" without specifying the method typically receives whatever direct immunoassay the reference lab defaults to — the test explicitly discouraged by current guidelines. If a free testosterone result matters for a real clinical decision (borderline total T, abnormal SHBG from obesity/thyroid disease/liver disease — see Volume 1 for SHBG regulation), specifically request calculated free testosterone (requires total T + SHBG) or, where precision genuinely matters, equilibrium dialysis.

SHBG itself is worth checking whenever free T interpretation matters — conditions that raise SHBG (aging, hyperthyroidism, liver disease, HIV) lower free T despite a normal total T, while conditions that lower SHBG (obesity, insulin resistance, hypothyroidism, androgen use) raise free T despite a normal-or-low total T. This is the same SHBG regulation table introduced in Volume 1's Foundational Principles, now applied directly to lab interpretation.

Estrogen (Estradiol) AssaysThe Sensitivity Problem

Standard estradiol immunoassays were designed and validated for the relatively high concentrations found in premenopausal, reproductive-age women — they lose accuracy at the much lower concentrations found in men and postmenopausal women, where imprecise immunoassay results can be off by a wide margin at the low end of the range.

PopulationRecommended assayWhy
Reproductive-age womenStandard immunoassay is adequateConcentrations are well within the assay's validated, accurate range
Men, postmenopausal women, childrenUltrasensitive immunoassay or LC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry)Standard immunoassays lack the sensitivity/specificity to reliably distinguish low concentrations from each other or from zero — mass spectrometry is now the preferred reference method in these populations for both clinical and research use
Practical implication
A standard estradiol level drawn on a man or postmenopausal woman using a routine immunoassay platform (rather than a mass-spec-based or ultrasensitive assay) should be interpreted cautiously — if the result meaningfully changes management (e.g., monitoring aromatase inhibitor therapy, evaluating gynecomastia, or assessing a man on TRT for excess aromatization), confirm the lab is using an assay validated for the low end of the range rather than assuming any reported "estradiol" result was measured the same way across platforms.

Rounding Out the PictureLH/FSH

Both hormone panels benefit from pairing with gonadotropins to localize the problem along the HPG axis (see Volume 1): low testosterone/estradiol with elevated LH/FSH points to primary gonadal failure; low testosterone/estradiol with low or inappropriately normal LH/FSH points to a hypothalamic-pituitary (secondary) problem — the same primary-vs-secondary framework used throughout this atlas for every other axis.

Holistic & Lifestyle Modifiers

Nonpharmacologic factors that measurably move testosterone or estrogen — graded the same way as Volume 2's lifestyle cards, reputable evidence only.

🏋️ Testosterone — Lifestyle Factors
Resistance exercise
EstablishedRCTs consistently show resistance training raises testosterone acutely, with a delayed return to baseline compared to aerobic exercise (see Volume 1 HPG-male section). One important nuance from the same evidence base: prolonged high-intensity exercise without adequate recovery can suppress testosterone for up to 72 hours via cortisol antagonism — more is not always better.
Weight loss (in men with obesity)
EstablishedObesity lowers testosterone through multiple compounding mechanisms: increased peripheral aromatization of testosterone to estradiol in adipose tissue (see Volume 1 HPG-male aromatase discussion), insulin-resistance-driven suppression of hepatic SHBG production, and leptin resistance affecting GnRH pulsatility. Weight loss reliably raises total and free testosterone in this population — one of the more robust lifestyle-hormone relationships in this atlas.
Sleep
EstablishedTestosterone secretion is tied to sleep architecture — experimental sleep restriction lowers next-day testosterone in controlled studies, independent of the GH/sleep relationship covered in Volume 2.
Chronic heavy alcohol use
EstablishedDirect Leydig cell toxicity plus increased peripheral aromatization (alcohol-associated liver changes can upregulate aromatase activity) combine to lower testosterone and raise estradiol with chronic heavy use — part of the mechanism behind gynecomastia and testicular atrophy seen in advanced alcoholic liver disease.
Vitamin D supplementation
Not supportedA well-designed RCT in men with low testosterone and low-to-insufficient vitamin D found no effect of vitamin D supplementation on testosterone levels — a common supplement marketing claim that doesn't hold up even in the specific population (vitamin D deficient, low-T) where it would be most expected to work.
"Testosterone-boosting" herbal supplements (D-aspartic acid, tribulus, fenugreek, etc.)
Not supportedD-aspartic acid showed an effect in untrained men in early studies but no benefit — and in some data, a reduction — in resistance-trained men over a 3-month RCT. Most other commonly marketed herbal "test boosters" lack comparable rigorous trial support in humans at typical consumer doses.
🌸 Estrogen — Lifestyle Factors
Exercise (premenopausal women)
EstablishedRegular vigorous exercise is associated with modestly lower circulating estrogen in premenopausal women — part of the biologic rationale explored in breast cancer risk-reduction research, though this is an association from cohort data more than a tightly quantified dose-response relationship.
Body fat / adiposity (postmenopausal women)
EstablishedAfter menopause, adipose tissue becomes the dominant estrogen source via peripheral aromatization of adrenal androstenedione (see Volume 1 female HPG axis) — higher body fat directly translates to higher circulating estrone, which is the mechanistic link between obesity and increased postmenopausal endometrial/breast cancer risk.
Dietary fiber
EstablishedFiber binds estrogen metabolites in the gut, reducing the enterohepatic recirculation described in Volume 1's estrogen breakdown section — higher fiber intake is associated with modestly lower circulating estrogen levels in observational studies.
Soy / phytoestrogen intake
Mixed evidence, but likely minimal effectRigorous meta-analyses of RCTs found soy isoflavones had no significant effect on circulating estradiol in postmenopausal women, behaving more like selective estrogen receptor modulators than true estrogenic agents. Modest symptom benefit for hot flashes has been shown, but this does not appear to be mediated by a meaningful change in circulating estrogen itself.
"Hormone-balancing" supplement blends
Not supportedProprietary multi-ingredient blends marketed to "balance estrogen" generally lack rigorous trial evidence as combination products — the individually-studied factors above (fiber, exercise, body composition) have far stronger evidence than branded supplement blends.
Verify before relying
Lifestyle factors are adjuncts to, not substitutes for, appropriate diagnostic workup and — where indicated — pharmacologic hormone replacement covered elsewhere in this file.

Morphology Glossary

The primary lesion vocabulary underneath every differential in this atlas — size, elevation, and contents are what separate a papule from a nodule, a vesicle from a bulla, and a petechia from a purpura. The blanching test is the single fastest bedside triage step, since it's what separates ordinary vascular dilation from blood that's actually left the vessels.

Cross-sectional schematic. Size thresholds (1cm, 3mm) are teaching conventions, not strict cutoffs — use them as anchors, not hard rules.

ER Rash Differential Algorithm

74 diagnoses organized first by body location, then by morphology — the way a real bedside decision actually unfolds: where is it, and what does it look like. Each entry carries a pruritic/non-pruritic flag and a hallmark + pearl. Can't-miss diagnoses are flagged explicitly. Start with the Morphology Glossary above if you need the primary-lesion vocabulary refreshed first.

Face13 diagnoses

Includes periorbital and perioral involvement — don't forget dermatomal (V1-V3) patterns here, which carry their own can't-miss logic.

Macular/Patch (2)

Erysipelas

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkSharply demarcated, raised, tender, bright red plaque with a distinct border, classically over the cheeks/nose; fever and rapid onset.
Pearl: The sharp demarcation is the key feature distinguishing this from ordinary cellulitis (which has an indistinct, fading border) — erysipelas involves more superficial dermis/lymphatics.

Malar rash (acute cutaneous lupus)

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPhotosensitive erythematous rash over the malar eminences and nasal bridge, classically sparing the nasolabial folds.
Pearl: The nasolabial sparing is the single most useful discriminator from other facial erythemas (rosacea, seborrheic dermatitis, photosensitivity from other causes) — ask about it specifically on exam.
Papular/Plaque (5)

Acne vulgaris

Papular/PlaqueNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkComedones (open/closed), papules, pustules, and nodules/cysts in more severe disease; classic T-zone and jawline distribution.
Pearl: Distinguish from acneiform drug eruptions (steroid acne, EGFR-inhibitor rash) by the abrupt onset and monomorphic (all-same-stage) lesion pattern of the latter — true acne shows lesions at multiple stages simultaneously.

Rosacea

Papular/PlaqueNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkCentral facial erythema, telangiectasias, and papules/pustules without comedones; flushing triggers (heat, alcohol, spicy food) are classic.
Pearl: The absence of comedones is the key feature separating this from acne — treating rosacea like acne (harsh topical retinoids alone) often worsens the erythema.

Seborrheic dermatitis

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkGreasy yellow scale over erythematous patches in the nasolabial folds, eyebrows, and scalp margin.
Pearl: Worsens with stress and cold weather, improves in summer — a useful history clue distinguishing it from psoriasis, which doesn't follow the same seasonal pattern.

Molluscum contagiosum

Papular/PlaqueNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkDome-shaped, flesh-colored papules with a central umbilication; spread by direct contact/autoinoculation, common in children.
Pearl: In an adult with new widespread facial molluscum, consider HIV testing — extensive facial molluscum in adults is a recognized marker of immunosuppression.

Perioral dermatitis

Papular/PlaqueNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkGrouped small papules/papulopustules around the mouth with a characteristic clear zone immediately bordering the vermillion lip border; almost exclusively young women.
Pearl: Ask specifically about topical or inhaled corticosteroid use — it's a leading trigger, and treating it with more topical steroid (a common reflex when it's mistaken for eczema or acne) reliably makes it worse.
Vesicular/Bullous (3)

Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (V1)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkGrouped vesicles on an erythematous base in a strict V1 (ophthalmic) dermatomal distribution; Hutchinson's sign (vesicles on the nasal tip) predicts ocular involvement.
Pearl: Hutchinson's sign should trigger urgent ophthalmology involvement even before overt eye findings appear — nasociliary nerve involvement is what links the nose to the globe.

Herpes simplex (orofacial)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkGrouped, painful vesicles on an erythematous base, classically perioral; prodromal tingling/burning is common.
Pearl: Recurrent lesions in the same location are a strong clue — HSV reactivates at the same dermatomal site repeatedly, unlike most other vesicular eruptions.

Impetigo

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkHoney-colored crusted erosions, classically perioral/perinasal in children; can be bullous (S. aureus) or non-bullous (strep or staph).
Pearl: Highly contagious by direct contact — exclude from school/daycare until 24h of appropriate antibiotic therapy, and counsel on hand hygiene to prevent spread to household contacts.
Pustular (1)

Folliculitis

PustularNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkSmall follicular-based pustules, often after shaving, occlusion, or hot tub exposure.
Pearl: Hot-tub folliculitis (Pseudomonas) classically appears 8–48h after exposure and is often self-limited — distinguishing exposure history avoids unnecessary antibiotics.
Nodular (1)

Epidermoid (sebaceous) cyst

NodularNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkFirm, mobile, subcutaneous nodule, often with a central punctum; can become inflamed/infected causing acute tenderness.
Pearl: Don't incise and drain an inflamed cyst expecting the same ease as an abscess — the cyst wall/capsule often needs to be addressed for definitive resolution, or it will recur.
Urticarial/Wheal (1)

Angioedema

Urticarial/WhealNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkNon-pitting, non-pruritic swelling of the lips, eyelids, or tongue, without a clear wheal border; can be allergic (with urticaria/anaphylaxis) or bradykinin-mediated (ACE-inhibitor, hereditary).
Pearl: Isolated angioedema without urticaria/itching should raise bradykinin-mediated angioedema (ACE-inhibitor or hereditary C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency) — epinephrine and antihistamines are far less effective for this mechanism, and airway management takes priority.

Trunk17 diagnoses

Central distribution is often the first clue to a systemic/exanthematous process rather than a localized contact or infectious process.

Macular/Patch (6)

Cellulitis

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPoorly demarcated, spreading erythema with warmth, tenderness, and edema, without the sharp raised border of erysipelas; fever may or may not be present.
Pearl: The indistinct, fading border (vs. erysipelas's sharp raised edge) reflects deeper dermal/subcutaneous involvement — mark the leading edge with a pen at the bedside so you can objectively assess progression or improvement on antibiotics.

Scarlet fever

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkDiffuse, finely papular “sandpaper” rash with a sunburn-like erythematous background, accentuated in skin folds (Pastia's lines), circumoral pallor, and a “strawberry tongue”; follows streptococcal pharyngitis.
Pearl: This is the classic teaching exception in the numbered-exanthem list — it's caused by a bacterial (Group A strep) erythrogenic exotoxin, not a virus, despite historically being grouped with the viral exanthems as “second disease.” See the Viral Rashes page for the full comparison.

Pityriasis rosea

Macular/PatchPRURITIC
+
HallmarkHerald patch (single larger oval scaly patch) precedes a generalized eruption of smaller oval patches following skin cleavage lines in a “Christmas tree” pattern on the back.
Pearl: Ask specifically about a single larger patch appearing 1–2 weeks before the generalized eruption — patients often don't mention the herald patch unless asked directly.

Tinea versicolor

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkHypo- or hyperpigmented, finely scaly macules/patches on the trunk, more noticeable after sun exposure (affected areas fail to tan).
Pearl: A quick bedside potassium hydroxide (KOH) prep showing “spaghetti and meatballs” hyphae/spores confirms the diagnosis without needing to await culture.

Erythema migrans (early Lyme disease)

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkExpanding annular erythematous patch, classically with central clearing (“bullseye”), at or near a tick bite site, typically appearing 3–30 days post-exposure.
Pearl: Central clearing is present in a minority of cases — don't require it for the diagnosis; a single expanding annular lesion with an endemic-area tick exposure history is enough to treat empirically.

Viral exanthem (measles)

Macular/PatchPRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkCephalocaudal spreading erythematous maculopapular rash, appearing 2–4 days after fever/cough/coryza/conjunctivitis onset; Koplik spots (buccal mucosa) precede the rash.
Pearl: Koplik spots are pathognomonic and appear before the rash — look for them specifically in a compatible prodrome, and isolate/report per public health protocol given how contagious measles is.
Papular/Plaque (1)

Guttate psoriasis

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkSudden eruption of small (<1cm), drop-like, scaly papules/plaques on the trunk, classically 2–3 weeks after streptococcal pharyngitis.
Pearl: Ask about a recent sore throat — the post-streptococcal trigger is a distinguishing history point from other papulosquamous eruptions, and confirming/treating strep is part of management.
Vesicular/Bullous (4)

Varicella (chickenpox)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkCrops of lesions in different stages simultaneously (“dew drops on a rose petal”) — papules, vesicles, and crusted lesions all present at once; centripetal distribution (trunk first, spreading outward).
Pearl: The “different stages at once” pattern is the key discriminator from other vesicular exanthems, which tend to be more uniform in stage — also highly contagious until all lesions are crusted.

Disseminated (generalized) zoster

Vesicular/BullousPRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkWidespread vesicular eruption crossing dermatomal boundaries, typically in an immunocompromised host, sometimes preceded by localized zoster.
Pearl: Treat as systemic/disseminated disease requiring IV antiviral therapy and contact/airborne precautions — this is not simply “bad shingles.”

Bullous pemphigoid

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkTense, intact bullae on erythematous or normal-appearing skin in an older adult; negative Nikolsky sign (bullae don't extend with lateral pressure).
Pearl: Intensely pruritic urticarial plaques often precede the bullae by weeks — consider this diagnosis in an elderly patient with an itchy rash even before bullae appear.

Pemphigus vulgaris

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkFlaccid bullae that rupture easily, leaving painful erosions; positive Nikolsky sign (gentle lateral pressure extends blister/sloughs skin); oral erosions frequently precede skin involvement.
Pearl: Ask about oral pain/erosions specifically — they often precede cutaneous lesions by weeks to months and are frequently misattributed to aphthous ulcers before the skin findings clarify the diagnosis.
Petechial/Purpuric (4)

Meningococcemia

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPetechiae/purpura (often starting on the trunk/extremities) in a febrile, toxic-appearing patient; can progress rapidly to purpura fulminans.
Pearl: Petechiae plus fever plus a toxic appearance is sepsis until proven otherwise — don't wait for confirmatory testing to start empiric antibiotics; this is one of the fastest-moving true emergencies in this atlas.

IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura)

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPalpable purpura in dependent areas (buttocks, legs, sometimes trunk), often with abdominal pain, arthralgias, and hematuria; typically pediatric, often post-viral/post-strep.
Pearl: “Palpable” is the key exam word — palpable purpura signals vasculitis (inflammation in the vessel wall) rather than a simple thrombocytopenia/coagulopathy process, which is usually flat.

Rocky Mountain spotted fever

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPetechial/maculopapular rash classically starting on wrists and ankles before spreading centripetally to the trunk (including palms/soles); fever, headache, and a tick-exposure history.
Pearl: Don't wait for the rash to start doxycycline if the clinical/exposure story fits — the rash is often absent or subtle in the first several days, and delayed treatment significantly increases mortality.

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPetechiae and easy bruising with an isolated low platelet count, otherwise well-appearing patient, often following a viral illness in children.
Pearl: The patient's overall well-appearing status and isolated thrombocytopenia (without other cytopenias or systemic toxicity) helps distinguish this from more dangerous causes of petechiae like meningococcemia.
Urticarial/Wheal (2)

Acute urticaria

Urticarial/WhealPRURITIC
+
HallmarkWell-circumscribed, blanching, intensely pruritic wheals that are individually transient (each lesion resolves within 24h, though new ones may continually appear).
Pearl: Mark an individual lesion with a pen and recheck in a few hours — if it's still there unchanged beyond 24h, reconsider urticarial vasculitis rather than ordinary urticaria.

Urticarial vasculitis

Urticarial/WhealNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkWheal-like lesions that persist >24h, may be painful/burning rather than purely itchy, and can leave residual bruising/hyperpigmentation as they resolve.
Pearl: The combination of lesions lasting >24h plus residual bruising is what separates this from ordinary urticaria — it warrants a workup for an underlying autoimmune/inflammatory cause.

Extremities15 diagnoses

Flexural vs. extensor surface, and proximal vs. distal, are both worth documenting — they narrow the differential fast.

Macular/Patch (3)

Erythema migrans (extremity presentation)

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkSame expanding annular patch as the trunk presentation — extremities are a common tick-bite site, especially the lower legs.
Pearl: Check for multiple lesions — disseminated early Lyme disease can produce several annular lesions simultaneously, not just one.

Fixed drug eruption

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkSharply demarcated, dusky red-to-violaceous round patch/plaque that recurs in the exact same location with each re-exposure to the causative drug.
Pearl: Ask specifically about new or recently restarted medications and whether a similar spot has appeared before in the same spot — this recurrence-at-the-same-site pattern is essentially diagnostic.

Necrotizing fasciitis

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPain dramatically out of proportion to visible skin findings early on; skin later shows dusky/violaceous discoloration, hemorrhagic bullae, and crepitus as the process progresses, with rapid spread despite antibiotics.
Pearl: Early skin findings can look deceptively mild (just erythema and swelling) while the pain-out-of-proportion and rapid clinical deterioration are already present — this mismatch between exam and symptom severity is the single most important clue, and surgical exploration/debridement should not wait for imaging to “confirm” it.
Papular/Plaque (3)

Lichen planus

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkThe “6 P's”: planar (flat-topped), pruritic, purple, polygonal, papules, and plaques, classically on the flexor wrists/forearms; fine white lines (Wickham striae) may be visible on the surface.
Pearl: Check the oral mucosa (lacy white reticular pattern) and nails (longitudinal ridging) — mucosal and nail involvement support the diagnosis and help distinguish it from other papular eruptions.

Scabies

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkIntensely pruritic papules, often with visible burrows, classically in finger web spaces, wrists, axillae, and groin; pruritus characteristically worsens at night.
Pearl: Check close contacts — scabies spreads through prolonged skin contact, and treating only the index patient without treating household/close contacts simultaneously leads to reinfestation.

Guttate psoriasis (extremity involvement)

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkSame drop-like scaly papules as trunk presentation, can extend onto the proximal extremities.
Pearl: Same post-streptococcal history pearl applies — ask about a recent sore throat.
Vesicular/Bullous (3)

Allergic contact dermatitis (e.g., poison ivy/oak)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkPruritic vesicles/bullae often in linear or streaky configurations reflecting how the plant brushed against the skin; can weep and crust.
Pearl: The linear/streaky pattern is the single best clue that this is external contact rather than an internal/systemic process — truly systemic eruptions don't respect a linear brush-contact pattern.

Dyshidrotic eczema

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkIntensely pruritic, deep-seated “tapioca-like” vesicles on the lateral fingers and palms/soles.
Pearl: Often flares with stress, seasonal humidity changes, or nickel exposure — ask about hand-jewelry or metal-object contact triggers.

Bullous impetigo

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkFlaccid, fragile bullae that rupture leaving a thin varnish-like crust, from a localized staphylococcal exfoliative toxin effect.
Pearl: Distinguish from more severe staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome by the localized (not diffuse) distribution and the child's otherwise well appearance.
Pustular (1)

Folliculitis (extremity)

PustularNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkFollicular pustules, often after shaving (pseudofolliculitis) or hot tub exposure on the extremities.
Pearl: Same hot-tub/Pseudomonas exposure-history pearl as the facial presentation applies here.
Nodular (2)

Erythema nodosum

NodularNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkTender, red-to-violaceous nodules, classically on the anterior shins bilaterally; lesions never ulcerate and resolve with a bruise-like color change over weeks.
Pearl: A reactive process, not a primary skin disease — always look for the trigger: streptococcal infection, sarcoidosis, inflammatory bowel disease, pregnancy, or certain medications (notably OCPs and sulfonamides).

Cutaneous larva migrans

NodularPRURITIC
+
HallmarkIntensely pruritic, serpiginous, thread-like raised track that slowly migrates across the skin, classically on the feet after walking barefoot on contaminated sand/soil.
Pearl: A travel/exposure history (tropical beach, barefoot contact with sand) is the key clue — the larva is trapped in the epidermis and cannot complete its lifecycle in human skin, so it wanders visibly.
Petechial/Purpuric (2)

IgA vasculitis (lower extremity predominant)

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPalpable purpura concentrated on the lower extremities and buttocks (dependent, gravity-affected areas), often with joint and abdominal symptoms.
Pearl: The lower-extremity/dependent-area predominance reflects the vasculitis being worsened by hydrostatic pressure — encourage leg elevation as a simple adjunct while the underlying process is managed.

Rocky Mountain spotted fever (wrists/ankles)

Petechial/PurpuricNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkClassically starts specifically on the wrists and ankles before spreading centripetally — this is the textbook initial location.
Pearl: Examine the wrists and ankles specifically in a febrile patient with a tick-exposure history, even before a widespread rash is visible — this is the earliest site of involvement.
Urticarial/Wheal (1)

Cold urticaria

Urticarial/WhealPRURITIC
+
HallmarkWheals appearing specifically at sites of cold exposure (cold water, cold air, holding a cold drink), resolving as the skin rewarms.
Pearl: Screen for systemic cold-induced anaphylaxis risk before recommending activities like cold-water swimming — a simple ice-cube test on the forearm can support the diagnosis in clinic.

Palms & Soles8 diagnoses

A short list of diagnoses truly favors palms/soles — which is exactly what makes involvement here so diagnostically useful.

Macular/Patch (3)

Secondary syphilis

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkCopper-colored (reddish-brown), non-pruritic macules/papules specifically involving the palms and soles, often with a broader truncal papulosquamous rash.
Pearl: Palm/sole involvement in an otherwise nonspecific rash should prompt syphilis serology — few common conditions produce a truly palm/sole-predominant eruption, which is exactly what makes this finding so valuable.

Rocky Mountain spotted fever (palms/soles)

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPetechial/maculopapular rash extending to palms and soles as it progresses centripetally — palm/sole involvement is a late but classic finding.
Pearl: Palm/sole involvement is one of only a handful of conditions that produces this pattern — in a febrile patient with a tick exposure history, this finding should accelerate empiric doxycycline rather than await confirmatory serology.

Palmar erythema

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkSymmetric, mottled redness of the palms (especially the thenar/hypothenar eminences), blanching with pressure, non-tender and non-pruritic.
Pearl: A benign-appearing but genuinely informative sign — seen in pregnancy (a normal variant) but also in chronic liver disease, hyperthyroidism, and rheumatoid arthritis, so ask about those before assuming it's incidental.
Vesicular/Bullous (2)

Hand-foot-and-mouth disease (coxsackievirus)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkSmall oval vesicles/papules on the palms, soles, and buttocks, plus painful oral ulcers, typically in a young child with mild fever.
Pearl: The combination of oral ulcers plus palm/sole vesicles in an otherwise mildly ill child is essentially diagnostic — reassurance and supportive care are usually all that's needed.

Dyshidrotic eczema (palmar)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkDeep-seated, intensely pruritic vesicles on the palms, often at the lateral finger margins.
Pearl: Same nickel/metal-contact and stress-trigger pearl as the extremity presentation applies.
Target lesion (1)

Erythema multiforme

Target lesionNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkClassic target/iris lesions (three concentric zones of color) with acral (hands/feet, including palms) predominance; most commonly triggered by HSV infection, less often by mycoplasma or medications.
Pearl: Distinguish from Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN by the acral-predominant distribution, minimal-to-absent mucosal involvement, and a generally well-appearing patient — EM is far less dangerous, but the two are frequently confused.
Desquamative (2)

Kawasaki disease

DesquamativeNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkFever ≥5 days plus at least 4 of: bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, oral mucosal changes (strawberry tongue, cracked lips), polymorphous rash, extremity changes (edema/erythema followed by periungual desquamation), and cervical lymphadenopathy.
Pearl: Periungual desquamation is classically a finding of the subacute phase (week 2–3), not the initial presentation — don't wait for it to appear before considering the diagnosis, since coronary artery aneurysm risk is time-sensitive to treat (IVIG).

Toxic shock syndrome

DesquamativeNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkDiffuse macular “sunburn-like” erythema with fever, hypotension, and multi-organ involvement, followed by desquamation of the palms and soles 1–2 weeks later.
Pearl: The desquamation is a late finding — don't wait for it; the acute diagnosis rests on the fever/hypotension/diffuse-erythema/multi-organ picture plus a source (retained tampon/nasal packing/wound).

Mucosal / Intertriginous8 diagnoses

Oral, genital, and skin-fold involvement changes the differential substantially and often signals a more severe process.

Papular/Plaque (3)

Candidal intertrigo

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkMoist, macerated, beefy-red erythema in skin folds (groin, under breasts, abdominal panniculus) with satellite papules/pustules just beyond the main plaque border.
Pearl: The satellite lesions beyond the main border are the key distinguishing feature from simple intertriginous irritation/contact dermatitis, which doesn't produce true satellite lesions.

Tinea cruris

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+
HallmarkAnnular, scaling, pruritic plaque with a well-demarcated, slightly raised, active border in the groin, classically sparing the scrotum.
Pearl: Scrotal sparing is the key discriminator from candidal intertrigo, which typically does involve the scrotum/genital skin more diffusely.

Oral candidiasis (thrush)

Papular/PlaqueNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkWhite, curd-like plaques on the tongue/buccal mucosa that scrape off with mild bleeding underneath, distinguishing it from leukoplakia (which does not scrape off).
Pearl: Consider an underlying cause when it's unexpected — recent antibiotics/inhaled corticosteroids, dentures, diabetes, or immunocompromise (including undiagnosed HIV) in an adult without an obvious explanation.
Vesicular/Bullous (5)

Herpes simplex (oral/genital)

Vesicular/BullousPRURITIC
+
HallmarkGrouped, painful vesicles/erosions on an erythematous base; recurrences favor the same site with a prodrome of tingling/burning.
Pearl: Primary genital HSV can present with dramatic bilateral vesicles/erosions plus systemic symptoms (fever, malaise) mimicking a more diffuse process — don't let the severity of a primary outbreak throw off the diagnosis.

Pemphigus vulgaris (oral erosions)

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPainful oral erosions, often the presenting sign before skin involvement; positive Nikolsky sign on adjacent skin if present.
Pearl: Persistent, unexplained oral erosions/pain lasting weeks (not the days-long course of aphthous ulcers or HSV) should prompt biopsy consideration rather than repeated symptomatic treatment.

Stevens-Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkPainful mucosal erosions (oral, ocular, genital — often at least 2 sites) plus skin dusky/targetoid macules that progress to epidermal detachment; positive Nikolsky sign; typically drug-induced, onset 1–4 weeks after the culprit medication.
Pearl: Mucosal involvement at 2 or more sites plus skin sloughing is the hallmark that separates this from erythema multiforme — this is a dermatologic emergency requiring burn-unit-level care and immediate culprit drug discontinuation.

Aphthous ulcers

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkPainful, shallow, round oral ulcers with a gray-white base and erythematous halo, typically resolving within 1–2 weeks without scarring.
Pearl: Recurrent oral and genital ulcers together should raise Behçet's disease — a single-site, self-limited aphthous ulcer is common and benign, but a recurring bipolar (oral + genital) pattern is not.

Behçet's disease

Vesicular/BullousNON-PRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkRecurrent, painful oral aphthous ulcers plus recurrent genital ulcers, often with ocular inflammation (uveitis) and a positive pathergy test (papule/pustule forming at a needle-prick site).
Pearl: The recurring bipolar (oral + genital) ulcer pattern is what should trigger this diagnosis rather than repeatedly treating each ulcer episode as an isolated aphthous ulcer or presumed HSV.

Generalized / Diffuse13 diagnoses

Widespread rashes without a clear focal origin — this is where the true dermatologic emergencies concentrate.

Macular/Patch (3)

Erythroderma

Macular/PatchPRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkDiffuse erythema and scaling involving >90% of the body surface area, from an underlying cause (psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, drug reaction, or cutaneous T-cell lymphoma).
Pearl: Erythroderma itself is a medical emergency regardless of the underlying cause — patients lose thermoregulatory control and can develop high-output cardiac failure and significant fluid/protein losses through the skin.

Viral exanthem (roseola)

Macular/PatchNON-PRURITIC
+
HallmarkHigh fever for 3–5 days that resolves abruptly, followed by the appearance of a diffuse pink macular/maculopapular rash as the fever breaks; typically infants/toddlers.
Pearl: The rash appearing only after the fever resolves (not during) is the key sequence that distinguishes roseola from other childhood exanthems, which typically rash while still febrile.

Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease, parvovirus B19)

Macular/PatchPRURITIC
+
Hallmark“Slapped cheek” facial erythema followed by a lacy, reticular rash on the trunk and extremities; typically mild in children.
Pearl: Ask about pregnancy exposure specifically — parvovirus B19 in a pregnant contact carries a risk of fetal hydrops and warrants obstetric follow-up, even though the illness itself is mild in the child.
Papular/Plaque (3)

DRESS syndrome (drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)

Papular/PlaquePRURITICCAN'T MISS
+
HallmarkDiffuse morbilliform rash plus facial edema, fever, and lymphadenopathy, with eosinophilia and internal organ involvement (liver most commonly); classically delayed onset 2–8 weeks after starting the culprit drug.
Pearl: The delayed onset (weeks, not days) is what makes this easy to miss — patients and clinicians often don't connect the rash to a medication started over a month earlier; check LFTs and a CBC with differential in any diffuse drug rash with facial swelling.

Morbilliform drug eruption (simple)

Papular/PlaquePRURITIC
+

Crusted (Norwegian) scabies

Pearl: This is a laboratory-supported clinical diagnosis in the context of a systemic trigger (sepsis, trauma, malignancy, obstetric complication) — the skin findings are a marker of the underlying process, not the primary problem to treat.

Scary-Looking but Benign

The deliberate mirror image of the can't-miss flags elsewhere in this atlas: 14 conditions whose appearance alarms patients and junior clinicians out of proportion to their actual risk. Each entry walks through what it's mistaken for, what it actually is, and — the piece that matters most for counseling — its full clinical course from onset to resolution.

⚠ Why this page exists

Overcalling a benign, self-limited eruption as something dangerous drives unnecessary workups, antibiotics, and patient fear. Undercalling a genuinely dangerous one is the opposite failure. The skill this page builds is the same skill the can't-miss badges build elsewhere — just aimed at the other side of the error.

Dyshidrotic eczema

+
Looks likeA severe blistering disease or a contagious infection — the deep-seated, tense, “tapioca-like” vesicles on the palms and fingers look far more alarming than ordinary eczema.
ActuallyAn intensely pruritic but benign eczema variant. It is not infectious, not a blistering autoimmune disease, and not dangerous — the depth and tension of the vesicles come from the unusually thick palmar/plantar skin, not from severity.
CourseVesicles erupt over several days, often with intense itching before they're even visible. They typically don't rupture (thick skin holds them intact) and instead dry up and desquamate over 2–3 weeks. It's chronic-relapsing — expect recurrent flares tied to stress, humidity/season changes, or nickel exposure — but never progresses to anything systemic.
Pearl: Tell the patient plainly: this will not spread as an infection, will not scar, and will dry up and peel on its own over a few weeks even without treatment — topical steroids just speed up the itching relief.

Pityriasis rosea

+
Looks likeA dramatic new widespread eruption — patients (and sometimes clinicians) worry about a serious viral exanthem, a drug reaction, or even secondary syphilis given how quickly it covers the trunk.
ActuallyA benign, self-limited eruption, likely triggered by reactivation of HHV-6/7. It carries no systemic risk in an immunocompetent patient.
CourseA single herald patch appears first, often mistaken for ringworm. Over the following 1–2 weeks, smaller patches spread across the trunk in the Christmas-tree pattern. The eruption plateaus, then fades spontaneously over a total course of 6–8 weeks. Temporary post-inflammatory pigment change (more noticeable in darker skin tones) can linger after the rash itself clears, but resolves without scarring.
Pearl: The herald patch is the single most reassuring history point you can elicit — a patient who recalls one larger spot appearing a week or two before everything else fits a clean, benign timeline rather than something progressive.

Guttate psoriasis

+
Looks likeA sudden, violent-looking eruption of dozens to hundreds of scaly drops overnight — the abruptness alone makes it look infectious or allergic.
ActuallyA reactive, post-streptococcal eruption in a psoriasis-prone patient. It is not infectious and not an allergic emergency.
CourseLesions erupt over just a few days, typically 2–3 weeks after a strep pharyngitis episode. Many cases spontaneously clear over weeks to a few months, especially with sunlight exposure and topical therapy; a minority go on to develop chronic plaque psoriasis.
Pearl: Confirm and treat the strep trigger (throat culture, antibiotics if still active) as part of management — addressing the trigger is as relevant as treating the skin itself.

Erythema multiforme (minor)

+
Looks likeStevens-Johnson syndrome — the classic target/iris lesions look dramatic enough that patients and trainees alike often jump straight to the most severe diagnosis on sight.
ActuallyA benign, usually HSV-triggered hypersensitivity reaction. “Minor” EM has minimal-to-no mucosal involvement and does not carry SJS/TEN's mortality risk.
CourseLesions erupt over 3–5 days, favor the extremities (including palms), and each individual target lesion lasts about a week. The full episode resolves over 2–4 weeks without scarring. Because it's HSV-driven in most cases, it can recur with each HSV reactivation.
Pearl: Check mucosal surfaces specifically — the presence or absence of significant oral/ocular/genital erosions is what separates this reassuring course from the SJS/TEN entry in the main algorithm, not the dramatic appearance of the skin lesions themselves.

Acute urticaria (extensive)

+
Looks likeImpending anaphylaxis — rapidly spreading, widespread wheals understandably frighten patients into thinking a severe allergic reaction is about to compromise their airway.
ActuallyBenign, histamine-mediated, and self-limited as long as there are no respiratory, GI, or cardiovascular symptoms accompanying it.
CourseIndividual wheals are transient by definition, resolving within 24 hours each (though new ones can keep appearing elsewhere). The overall episode typically runs its course within days and, by definition, is called “acute” if it resolves within 6 weeks.
Pearl: Teach the pen-mark test: circle one wheal and recheck it in a few hours. If that specific lesion is gone or clearly changed, this is ordinary urticaria — and worth saying out loud to an anxious patient as objective reassurance.

Molluscum contagiosum with the “BOTE sign”

+
Looks likeA worsening skin infection needing antibiotics — when molluscum papules become red, crusted, and inflamed (the “Beginning Of The End”, or BOTE, sign), parents and patients read this as the infection spreading or turning septic.
ActuallyThe opposite is true: this inflammatory change is the immune system finally recognizing and clearing the virus. It is a reassuring, not alarming, sign.
CourseIndividual lesions typically last 2–4 months; because autoinoculation seeds new lesions in the meantime, the overall condition in a child can persist 6–18 months before full spontaneous clearance. The BOTE sign, when it appears, usually predicts resolution within weeks.
Pearl: This is one of the few times in dermatology where you can tell a worried parent that redness and crusting are good news — frame it that way explicitly, since the visual instinct is to assume the opposite.

Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease)

+
Looks likeAn alarming new facial rash — the abrupt, bright “slapped cheek” appearance in an otherwise playing, active child often prompts urgent concern from parents.
ActuallyA mild, self-limited parvovirus B19 infection in an immunocompetent child, with the child usually feeling better than the rash looks by the time it appears.
CourseSlapped-cheek erythema lasts 2–4 days, followed by a lacy, reticular rash on the trunk/extremities that lingers 1–3 weeks — and can transiently flare with heat, sunlight, or exercise even after the child seems to have recovered, which itself often re-alarms parents.
Pearl: Ask specifically about pregnant household contacts — the child's illness is mild, but a pregnant contact exposed during the child's contagious (pre-rash) period should follow up with obstetrics given the fetal hydrops risk.

Roseola infantum

+
Looks likeA concerning rash after several days of high fever — parents who were already anxious about the fever become newly alarmed when a rash appears just as the child seems to be improving.
ActuallyA benign HHV-6/7 viral illness. The classic “fever-then-rash” sequence is itself the reassuring pattern, not a sign of a new complication.
CourseHigh fever (often 39–40°C) persists for 3–5 days, frequently with a child who looks less ill than the fever number suggests. The fever resolves abruptly, and a pink macular/maculopapular rash appears within 12–24 hours of defervescence, fading on its own within 1–3 days.
Pearl: This sequence — rash appearing only after the fever breaks — is worth explaining to parents directly: it's the tail end of the illness, not the start of a new one.

Severe allergic contact dermatitis (e.g., facial poison ivy)

+
Looks likeCellulitis, angioedema, or a severe infectious/allergic emergency — dramatic facial/eyelid swelling and weeping blisters look infectious or anaphylactic, especially when the eyes swell shut.
ActuallyA benign (if uncomfortable) delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction, with no systemic threat unless there is true airway involvement, which is rare even with severe facial cases.
CourseOnset is delayed 1–3 days after exposure (this is a T-cell-mediated reaction, not immediate), peaking around days 3–7, then gradually resolving over 2–3 weeks. New patches can appear at unexposed sites days into the course (autoeczematization/“id” spread) — which understandably makes patients think it's contagious or spreading internally.
Pearl: Explain the delayed-onset mechanism up front — patients are often confused about why a rash “keeps spreading” days after they stopped touching anything, and the autoeczematization phenomenon is the answer, not worsening exposure.

Cutaneous larva migrans

+
Looks likeAn active parasite burrowing and spreading under the skin — which is, notably, literally true, making this one of the more legitimately unsettling-looking benign conditions in this list.
ActuallySelf-limited: the larva is trapped in human skin, cannot complete its lifecycle, and will eventually die on its own even without treatment.
CourseUntreated, the track continues migrating for several weeks and resolves spontaneously within 4–8 weeks as the larva dies. A short course of an antiparasitic (e.g., albendazole or ivermectin) reliably shortens this considerably.
Pearl: You can honestly tell a disturbed patient that the larva cannot survive long-term in human skin — humans are a dead-end host, which is exactly why this resolves rather than progressing to anything more invasive.

Mechanical (Valsalva-associated) petechiae

+
Looks likeMeningococcemia or another dangerous petechial process — in a well-appearing, afebrile patient, petechiae still reflexively trigger sepsis-level concern given everything else in this atlas that petechiae can mean.
ActuallyA benign, purely mechanical phenomenon from a transient spike in intrathoracic/venous pressure (forceful vomiting, coughing fits, prolonged crying, or the pushing phase of childbirth) rupturing superficial capillaries.
CoursePetechiae appear immediately after the inciting event, are classically confined above the nipple line (face, neck, upper chest, periorbital area), and fade like an ordinary bruise over 1–2 weeks with no new lesions appearing elsewhere.
Pearl: The distribution and the story do the differentiating work: a well-appearing, afebrile patient with petechiae confined to the face/neck after a clear forceful-Valsalva event does not need the same workup as the meningococcemia entry in the main algorithm — but any fever, toxicity, or petechiae below the chest should reopen that concern immediately.

Erythema toxicum neonatorum

+
Looks likeAn infected or allergic newborn rash — blotchy erythema studded with small pustules on a days-old infant is exactly the kind of finding that alarms new parents.
ActuallyOne of the most common benign newborn skin findings there is (affecting roughly half of full-term infants), with no infectious or allergic significance whatsoever.
CourseAppears within the first few days of life; individual lesions can come and go within hours, and the entire eruption resolves completely within 1–2 weeks without any treatment.
Pearl: This is a genuinely reassuring newborn-exam finding — naming it confidently for anxious new parents (rather than hedging) is itself part of good care here.

Miliaria (heat rash)

+
Looks likeA viral exanthem, allergic reaction, or infection — especially when it appears on a febrile infant, parents often assume the rash IS the illness rather than a separate, incidental heat-related finding.
ActuallyA benign, mechanical obstruction of sweat ducts from heat and occlusion (overbundling, fever, humid weather), with no association to the underlying illness causing the fever.
CourseAppears within hours to a few days of heat/occlusion exposure and resolves within hours to a couple of days once the skin is cooled and unoccluded — one of the fastest-resolving entries in this whole list.
Pearl: Worth explicitly decoupling from the fever in your explanation to parents — “this is from being overheated/bundled up, not part of what's making them sick” prevents an unnecessary secondary workup.

Livedo reticularis (physiologic / cutis marmorata)

+
Looks likeVascular occlusion, vasculitis, or poor perfusion — the lace-like, net-patterned mottling can look like an ischemic or inflammatory vascular process, especially to someone who hasn't seen the benign form before.
ActuallyIn infants (cutis marmorata) and many young women (primary/physiologic livedo reticularis), this is simply an accentuated normal cutaneous vascular response to cold.
CourseAppears with cold exposure and, critically, resolves completely with rewarming within minutes to about an hour. This rapid, complete reversibility with warming is the defining feature of the benign form.
Pearl: This is the cleanest bedside test in the whole list: rewarm the limb and recheck. Complete resolution confirms the benign physiologic pattern; persistence despite rewarming is what should redirect you toward a pathologic cause (vasculitis, antiphospholipid syndrome, cholesterol emboli) instead.

Course timelines are general teaching patterns, not guarantees — any lesion that deviates from its expected course (new systemic symptoms, mucosal involvement, failure to improve on schedule) should be re-evaluated rather than forced to fit the reassuring pattern.

⚠ 2nd disease is the exception that proves the rule

Scarlet fever is caused by a Group A streptococcal exotoxin, not a virus — it's grouped in this list purely for historical/teaching continuity. Worth saying out loud when teaching this framework so it doesn't get miscategorized as viral.

1st disease — Measles (rubeola)

+
Virus / etiologyMeasles virus (paramyxovirus)
Location & patternStarts on the face/hairline, spreads cephalocaudally (head → trunk → extremities) over 3 days; lesions coalesce as they spread.
PathophysiologyDirect cytopathic viral invasion of respiratory epithelium and skin capillary endothelium, with a T-cell-mediated immune response driving much of the rash itself.
Other presentationHigh fever, the “3 C's” (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis), and pathognomonic Koplik spots (buccal mucosa) 1–2 days before the rash.
Pearl: Koplik spots appear before the rash and are essentially diagnostic — look for them specifically in a compatible prodrome, and treat as a public-health-reportable, airborne-precautions case.

2nd disease — Scarlet fever

+
Virus / etiologyNot viral — Group A Streptococcus (bacterial exotoxin)
Location & patternDiffuse fine sandpaper-like papular rash starting on the trunk/neck, accentuated in flexural creases (Pastia's lines), with circumoral pallor.
PathophysiologyStreptococcal erythrogenic (pyrogenic) exotoxin-mediated, not a direct viral effect — the classic teaching exception in this numbered list.
Other presentationStrawberry tongue, sore throat, fever — essentially strep pharyngitis plus a toxin-mediated rash.
Pearl: Worth flagging explicitly: this is bacterial, not viral, despite its historical place in the “numbered exanthem” teaching list — treat the strep, and the rash resolves as the infection clears.

3rd disease — Rubella (German measles)

+
Virus / etiologyRubella virus (togavirus)
Location & patternStarts on the face, spreads cephalocaudally like measles but faster (rash fully out within ~24h) and clears face-first as it spreads, unlike measles.
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection with a milder immune/inflammatory response than measles, producing a milder, faster-resolving illness.
Other presentationMild fever, posterior auricular/suboccipital lymphadenopathy (a distinguishing feature from measles), and possible transient arthralgias in adults.
Pearl: The occipital/postauricular adenopathy is a useful discriminator from measles — and always ask about pregnancy exposure given the congenital rubella syndrome risk to a fetus.

4th disease — “Duke's disease” (historical)

+
Virus / etiologyContested / likely not a distinct entity
Location & patternHistorically described as a scarlatiniform rash distinct from scarlet fever and rubella.
PathophysiologyModern consensus is that this was likely not a genuinely distinct disease — probably a mix of mild scarlet fever, staphylococcal toxin-mediated rashes, or other exanthems misclassified at the time.
Other presentationNo reliable distinguishing features — this is largely a historical/teaching footnote rather than an actionable diagnosis today.
Pearl: Worth knowing it exists as trivia for the numbered-exanthem list, but don't chase it as an actual diagnosis — most modern texts consider it obsolete.

5th disease — Erythema infectiosum (fifth disease)

+
Virus / etiologyParvovirus B19
Location & pattern“Slapped cheek” facial erythema first, followed by a lacy, reticular (net-like) rash on the trunk and extremities.
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection of erythroid precursor cells; the rash itself is thought to be immune-complex mediated, appearing after viremia has already cleared — which is why the child is often no longer contagious by the time the rash appears.
Other presentationMild or absent fever, sometimes preceded by a nonspecific viral prodrome; can flare with heat/sun/exercise for weeks after the initial illness.
Pearl: By the time the rash appears, the child is generally past the contagious viremic phase — but a pregnant contact exposed during the earlier prodrome should still follow up given the fetal hydrops risk.

6th disease — Roseola infantum

+
Virus / etiologyHHV-6 (and sometimes HHV-7)
Location & patternPink macular/maculopapular rash on the trunk, spreading to the neck/face/extremities, appearing only after the fever breaks.
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection with viremia during the febrile phase; the rash appears as the immune response clears the virus from the blood, which is why it coincides with defervescence.
Other presentationHigh fever (39–40°C) for 3–5 days in an otherwise not-especially-ill-appearing infant/toddler, sometimes with febrile seizure due to the rapid temperature rise.
Pearl: The fever-then-rash sequence is the single most useful teaching point — a rash that appears as the fever resolves is reassuring, not a sign of a new complication.

Other High-Yield Viral Rashes11 entries

Not part of the historical numbered list, but every bit as relevant to an ED shift — several of these also appear in the main location-based algorithm or the scary-but-benign page; they're repeated here in full so this page works as a true standalone viral reference.

Varicella (chickenpox)

+
Virus / etiologyVaricella-zoster virus (VZV), primary infection
Location & patternCentripetal distribution (trunk and face first, then spreading outward to extremities, sparing palms/soles relatively).
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection of epidermal keratinocytes causing intraepidermal vesicle formation; crops appear in successive waves over several days.
Other presentationFever and malaise typically precede or accompany the rash; lesions appear in different stages simultaneously (papule, vesicle, crust) — the classic “dew drops on a rose petal” appearance.
Pearl: Contagious from ~48h before rash onset until all lesions are crusted — this window matters for isolation guidance and school/daycare return timing.

Hand-foot-and-mouth disease

+
Virus / etiologyCoxsackievirus A16 or Enterovirus 71 (classic); Coxsackievirus A6 (atypical, more severe/widespread variant)
Location & patternOval vesicles/papules on the palms, soles, and buttocks, plus painful oral ulcers/vesicles.
PathophysiologyDirect viral cytopathic effect on oral and acral epidermis; the A6 strain variant can produce a more widespread, bullous, and even perianal/extremity-predominant eruption than classic HFM.
Other presentationPainful mouth ulcers often drive poor oral intake and are frequently the chief complaint rather than the skin findings themselves; mild fever is common.
Pearl: The A6 atypical variant can look more like a severe bullous drug eruption or even eczema herpeticum — know that it exists so a genuinely more dramatic-looking HFM doesn't get over-worked-up.

Herpangina

+
Virus / etiologyCoxsackievirus A (various serotypes)
Location & patternSmall vesicles/ulcers confined to the posterior oropharynx (soft palate, tonsillar pillars, uvula) — no skin involvement, unlike hand-foot-mouth disease.
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection of posterior oropharyngeal mucosa; the posterior-only location (versus HFM's anterior mouth plus acral involvement) reflects a distinct site of viral tropism.
Other presentationHigh fever, sore throat, and odynophagia are often more prominent than in HFM disease.
Pearl: Posterior-only oral lesions without any skin/acral involvement is the key discriminator from hand-foot-mouth disease, even though both are Coxsackievirus-driven.

Infectious mononucleosis (EBV)

+
Virus / etiologyEpstein-Barr virus
Location & patternTrunk-predominant morbilliform or urticarial rash in a minority of patients — but appears in the large majority of cases if amoxicillin/ampicillin is given.
PathophysiologyThe classic rash is thought to reflect a transient drug-specific T-cell hypersensitivity reaction triggered by aminopenicillins during active EBV infection, not a direct viral exanthem in most cases.
Other presentationExudative pharyngitis, posterior cervical/diffuse lymphadenopathy, fatigue, and splenomegaly are the dominant features — the rash is usually incidental to the actual clinical picture.
Pearl: Avoid amoxicillin/ampicillin empirically in a patient with a mono-compatible presentation — the resulting rash is a classic, largely avoidable teaching-file finding.

Molluscum contagiosum

+
Virus / etiologyMolluscum contagiosum virus (a poxvirus)
Location & patternDome-shaped, flesh-colored, umbilicated papules anywhere on the body, often clustered; spread by direct contact and autoinoculation (e.g., scratching then touching another site).
PathophysiologyDirect epidermal infection causing characteristic Henderson-Patterson bodies (viral inclusions) on histology; the virus stays confined to the epidermis without systemic spread.
Other presentationUsually asymptomatic apart from occasional mild itching; surrounding eczematous dermatitis (“molluscum dermatitis”) can develop around individual lesions.
Pearl: See the Scary-Looking-but-Benign page for the “BOTE sign” — inflammation around lesions usually signals immune clearance, not worsening infection.

Primary HSV gingivostomatitis / genital HSV

+
Virus / etiologyHerpes simplex virus 1 (classically oral) or 2 (classically genital) — substantial overlap exists
Location & patternGrouped, painful vesicles on an erythematous base at the site of inoculation (oral mucosa/perioral skin, or genital/perigenital skin).
PathophysiologyDirect viral infection of epithelial cells with characteristic multinucleated giant cells and intranuclear inclusions; the virus then establishes latency in the regional sensory ganglion, enabling recurrence.
Other presentationPrimary infection can be dramatic — fever, malaise, and painful lesions severe enough to limit oral intake (gingivostomatitis) or urination (genital); recurrences are typically milder and preceded by a tingling/burning prodrome.
Pearl: Latency and recurrence at the same site is the defining feature that separates HSV from every other vesicular viral eruption in this list, all of which are one-time (or reinfection-based) illnesses.

Herpes zoster (shingles)

+
Virus / etiologyVaricella-zoster virus, reactivation of latent infection
Location & patternGrouped vesicles on an erythematous base in a strict, unilateral dermatomal distribution that does not cross the midline.
PathophysiologyReactivation of VZV that has remained latent in a dorsal root or cranial nerve ganglion since the primary varicella infection, traveling down the sensory nerve to the corresponding dermatome.
Other presentationPain, burning, or paresthesia in the dermatome often precedes the rash by 1–5 days; postherpetic neuralgia can persist long after the rash resolves, especially in older adults.
Pearl: V1 (ophthalmic) distribution with Hutchinson's sign (nasal tip vesicles) needs urgent ophthalmology involvement — covered in more depth on the main ER Differential Algorithm page.

Gianotti-Crosti syndrome (papular acrodermatitis of childhood)

+
Virus / etiologyHistorically Hepatitis B; now more often EBV, and a range of other viruses (Coxsackievirus, CMV, etc.) in vaccinated populations
Location & patternSymmetric, monomorphic (all lesions at the same stage) papules or papulovesicles on the face, buttocks, and extensor extremities, classically sparing the trunk.
PathophysiologyThought to represent an immune-complex-mediated reaction to an underlying viral infection rather than direct cutaneous viral invasion — the specific virus varies by region and era (hepatitis B historically, now more often EBV where HBV vaccination is widespread).
Other presentationUsually seen in young children, often with mild or no fever; can persist for several weeks, longer than most other viral exanthems on this list.
Pearl: The trunk-sparing, extensor-predominant, monomorphic pattern is the key recognition point — and it's a useful cue to consider checking hepatitis B and EBV serologies depending on regional prevalence and vaccination history.

Asymmetric periflexural exanthem of childhood

+
Virus / etiologyPresumed viral, no single pathogen consistently identified
Location & patternBegins unilaterally around one axilla or groin flexure, spreads to the same side of the trunk/extremity, and only later becomes more bilateral — the asymmetry is the defining feature.
PathophysiologyPresumed to be a viral exanthem based on its seasonal clustering and prodromal symptoms, though no single causative virus has been consistently isolated.
Other presentationMild or absent fever, sometimes mild GI or respiratory prodromal symptoms; the child is otherwise well.
Pearl: The one-sided-then-spreading pattern is genuinely distinctive — recognizing it as its own benign entity avoids an unnecessary workup for asymmetric rashes chasing a unifying diagnosis that doesn't exist here.

Papular-purpuric gloves and socks syndrome

+
Virus / etiologyParvovirus B19 (a distinct presentation from classic fifth disease)
Location & patternPainful edema and erythema of the hands and feet with a sharp, glove-and-sock-like demarcation at the wrists/ankles, evolving into petechiae/purpura, plus oral lesions in many cases.
PathophysiologyDirect parvovirus B19 infection with immune-complex deposition in the acral skin, distinct from the immune response pattern that produces classic fifth disease's facial/reticular rash.
Other presentationFever, malaise, and oral erosions/petechiae frequently accompany the acral findings.
Pearl: The sharp wrist/ankle demarcation plus the acral petechial/purpuric quality can look alarming (superficially resembling a vasculitis or even a coagulopathy) — but in a well-appearing child/young adult with this specific pattern, it's a recognized, self-limited parvovirus presentation.

Pityriasis rosea

+
Virus / etiologyPresumed HHV-6/7 reactivation (not fully established)
Location & patternHerald patch first, then a generalized eruption following skin cleavage lines in a “Christmas tree” pattern on the back.
PathophysiologyThought to represent a cutaneous response to HHV-6/7 reactivation, though the exact mechanism (direct infection vs. immune reaction to reactivation) remains debated.
Other presentationUsually minimal systemic symptoms; mild pruritus is common.
Pearl: See the Scary-Looking-but-Benign page for the full course timeline and reassurance framing — it's grouped there because its dramatic spread is what alarms patients, but it's included here for its viral etiology.

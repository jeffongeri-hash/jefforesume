Puberty & Development

Tanner staging, and the underlying hormonal cascade that actually drives it — two separate axes (adrenal and gonadal) that usually, but don't always, move together.

The Two Axes Behind Puberty

AxisDriven byProduces
Adrenarche (HPA axis)Rising adrenal androgen output (DHEA, DHEAS), typically mid-to-late childhoodPubarche — pubic/axillary hair. Independently is not true puberty.
Gonadarche (HPG axis)Disinhibition of pulsatile GnRH release from the hypothalamus, driving LH/FSH and ovarian maturationThelarche — breast budding (correlates with rising estradiol). This is the true marker of puberty onset.

These two axes mature independently — roughly 1 in 4–5 girls shows pubarche before thelarche, and for the rest, breast development leads or the two arrive together. Neither pathway is "abnormal"; they're simply different, individually variable entry points into the same overall process.

Typical Sequence & Timing

1
Thelarche (breast budding, Tanner breast stage 2) — first visible sign of puberty in 95% of girls, typically age 8–13.
2
Growth spurt begins shortly after — unlike boys, girls hit peak height velocity early in puberty, typically between Tanner breast stages 2 and 3.
3
Pubarche (pubic/axillary hair) — follows thelarche by roughly 1–1.5 years on average, though timing relative to thelarche varies as above.
4
Menarche (first period) — average age 12.5 years in the U.S., regardless of ethnicity, typically ~2–2.5 years after thelarche. Menarche itself is usually estrogen-withdrawal bleeding, not a sign of ovulation — regular ovulatory cycles often take another 1–2 years to establish.

Breast and pubic hair stages are frequently illustrated as moving in lockstep — in practice they should always be scored separately, since a meaningful proportion of girls progress through them at different rates or in a different order.

Precocious & Delayed Puberty — Definitions

  • Precocious puberty: secondary sexual characteristics before age 8 (some sources: 6 in Black girls, reflecting documented earlier average timing in this population) — warrants evaluation for central (GnRH-dependent) vs. peripheral (GnRH-independent) causes.
  • Delayed puberty: no breast development by age 13, or no menarche by age 15 with otherwise normal development (or by age 13 with no breast development at all) — Module 2 covers the leading functional causes.
02

What Suppresses & Promotes Development

The two directions this goes wrong — too early, or not at all — trace back to remarkably similar underlying levers: energy balance, inflammation, and environmental exposure.

What Can Delay or Suppress Development

Functional Hypothalamic Amenorrhea (FHA) — the central mechanism Tier A

Per the Endocrine Society's clinical practice guideline, three overlapping drivers suppress pulsatile GnRH release and can delay puberty or halt an already-established cycle: energy deficit (caloric restriction, disordered eating, or exercise output exceeding intake), excessive/high-intensity exercise specifically, and psychological stress. These activate the HPA axis in a way that directly suppresses the HPG axis — a genuine, adaptive-but-costly biological trade-off, not a vague "stress affects hormones" platitude.

  • A persistent myth worth correcting: FHA is not exclusive to underweight patients — it occurs across the weight spectrum whenever energy availability (intake relative to expenditure) is insufficient, including in athletes with normal or even high BMI who train intensely without matching caloric intake.
  • Consequences beyond delayed/absent menses: impaired peak bone mass accrual during a critical developmental window — a genuinely irreversible cost if it happens during adolescence, since peak bone mass is largely set by the end of the teens/early twenties.
  • Endocrine-disrupting chemicals — compounds like bisphenol A (BPA) and certain polychlorinated biphenyls — are proposed to interfere with GnRH neuron activity and kisspeptin signaling directly, though this research area is still developing relative to the well-established energy/stress mechanisms above.
  • Chronic illness (poorly controlled diabetes, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease) and genetic conditions (Turner syndrome, and others causing primary ovarian insufficiency) are organic causes of delayed puberty distinct from the functional causes above — worth distinguishing since the workup and management differ substantially.

What Can Accelerate or Trigger Early Development

Childhood obesity — a genuine, mechanistic driver, not just correlation

Current research points to hypothalamic inflammation as a real mechanistic link between childhood obesity and earlier puberty onset — a high-fat/high-glycemic diet and excess adiposity trigger microglial activation in the hypothalamus, which can prematurely activate GnRH neurons. This inflammatory change has been shown to precede significant weight gain in some studies, suggesting the relationship isn't simply "more body fat, earlier puberty" in a linear sense, but involves a genuine inflammatory pathway.

  • Higher childhood BMI is consistently associated with earlier thelarche in girls, and earlier puberty itself is associated with higher adult BMI — a bidirectional relationship worth explaining to families as a cycle rather than a one-way cause.
  • Central precocious puberty causes: most commonly idiopathic in girls, but CNS lesions (hypothalamic hamartoma, other tumors), prior CNS radiation, and hydrocephalus are organic causes that warrant imaging workup, particularly with rapid progression or other neurologic signs.
  • Peripheral (GnRH-independent) causes: ovarian or adrenal tumors secreting sex steroids, McCune-Albright syndrome, severe hypothyroidism (via TSH/FSH receptor cross-reactivity) — a distinct category requiring different evaluation (sex steroid levels without elevated gonadotropins) than central causes.
The practical takeaway for counseling families

Both directions of abnormal timing trace back to the same broad categories — energy balance, inflammation/metabolic status, and less commonly structural/genetic causes — which makes a thorough growth chart review, weight history, and menstrual/exercise history the highest-yield first step in either direction, before reaching for extensive lab or imaging workups.

Menstrual Pain

Affects between 45% and 95% of menstruating people depending on the study population — common enough that it's frequently under-treated as "just normal," even when genuinely disruptive.

Primary vs. Secondary — Why the Distinction Matters

Primary dysmenorrheaSecondary dysmenorrhea
CauseNo identifiable pelvic pathology — a prostaglandin-mediated processUnderlying pelvic pathology (endometriosis is the most common cause; also adenomyosis, fibroids, müllerian anomalies)
OnsetWithin ~1 year of menarche; occurs in ovulatory cyclesCan begin any time after menarche, sometimes years later
PatternPain concentrated in the first 1–3 days, cramping, may radiate to back/thighsOften worsens as the period progresses, or persists after it ends; accompanied by other symptoms (dyspareunia, heavy bleeding, infertility)
Response to first-line treatmentGenerally responds well to NSAIDs/hormonal therapyPoor or partial response to NSAIDs/hormonal contraceptives is itself a clue pointing toward a secondary cause

Mechanism — Why NSAIDs Are the Correct First-Line Choice

Primary dysmenorrhea is driven by overproduction of prostaglandins (particularly PGF2α) and leukotrienes in the secretory endometrium, causing myometrial hypercontractility, vasoconstriction, uterine ischemia/hypoxia, and heightened local nerve sensitivity — essentially the same prostaglandin-driven contractility seen in labor, just uterine-scale rather than delivering a fetus. This is precisely why NSAIDs (which inhibit prostaglandin synthesis via COX inhibition) are first-line and mechanistically targeted, rather than a generic analgesic choice.

Management Ladder

1
NSAIDs — first-line for both primary and secondary dysmenorrhea; most effective when started at the first sign of bleeding or cramping rather than waiting until pain is severe, since this gets ahead of prostaglandin accumulation.
2
Hormonal contraceptives (combined or progestin-only) — equally first-line, working by thinning the endometrium and reducing COX-2/prostaglandin production; can be used alone or combined with NSAIDs in refractory cases. Not FDA-approved specifically for this indication, but extensively used and effective off-label.
3
Acetaminophen — reasonable alternative when NSAIDs are contraindicated and hormonal therapy isn't wanted/appropriate, though generally less effective than either first-line option for moderate-severe pain.
4
Adjunctive/non-pharmacologic: topical heat (as effective as NSAIDs in some comparative trials for mild-moderate pain), regular exercise, adequate sleep. TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation) has reasonable supporting trial data as an adjunct.
5
Refractory to the combination of NSAIDs + hormonal therapy: this is the specific threshold that should prompt evaluation for a secondary cause (pelvic ultrasound at minimum) rather than continuing to escalate analgesics indefinitely.
Ultrasound isn't a routine part of a straightforward case

Routine pelvic ultrasound isn't indicated for classic, treatment-responsive primary dysmenorrhea — but it's genuinely useful for excluding secondary causes (endometriosis, adenomyosis, structural anomalies) when the pattern above is atypical or treatment-refractory.

The Four-Hormone Choreography, in Words

  • FSH rises first (days 1–4) to recruit a cohort of follicles; as one follicle becomes dominant and its estradiol output rises, FSH is suppressed via negative feedback — the "FSH window" that determines dominant-follicle selection.
  • Estradiol climbs through the follicular phase, and once it exceeds roughly 200 pg/mL for more than 50 hours, the feedback relationship flips from negative to positive — this sustained high estradiol is what triggers the LH surge.
  • LH surges sharply ~34–36 hours before ovulation, then falls just as sharply once ovulation occurs.
  • Progesterone stays low throughout the entire follicular phase, then rises steeply after ovulation as the corpus luteum forms — a reliable retrospective confirmation that ovulation occurred (a day-21 progesterone >3 ng/mL is a standard clinical check for this).

The Fertile Window — Why Timing Isn't Just "Ovulation Day"

Because ovulation and conception aren't the same moment, the window in which intercourse can actually result in pregnancy is longer than most people assume — and it's asymmetric, extending mostly before ovulation rather than after it.

A typical ~6-day fertile window on a day-14-ovulation cycle: roughly day 9 through day 15. Most of that window is driven by how long sperm survive waiting for the egg, not by the egg waiting for sperm.
The two numbers that explain everything Tier A

Per ASRM guidance on optimizing natural fertility: an ovulated egg is viable for only 12–24 hours — fertilization has to happen in that narrow window or the cycle is over. Sperm, by contrast, can survive for up to 5 days (typically averaging around 3) inside fertile cervical mucus, which appears in the days leading up to ovulation and specifically nourishes and protects sperm while filtering out abnormally-shaped or poorly motile ones. Combine those two numbers — up to 5 days of sperm survival plus roughly a day of egg viability — and the fertile window comes out to about 6 days: the 5 days before ovulation plus ovulation day itself.

Concretely: if ovulation happens on cycle day 14 and insemination occurs on day 12 (two days before ovulation), viable sperm can already be waiting in the fallopian tube when the egg is released on day 14 — resulting in a real, unremarkable pregnancy. This is exactly why "we only had sex a couple of days before ovulation, there's no way she's pregnant" is a common but incorrect assumption; in fact, data on conception timing show intercourse 1–2 days before ovulation carries the single highest per-cycle chance of conception, slightly ahead of ovulation day itself, precisely because it gives sperm time to reach and wait in the fallopian tube.

Myth: you can only get pregnant on ovulation day

This remains one of the most common fertility misconceptions on both sides — it leads some people trying to conceive to focus only on ovulation day itself (missing the more fertile days just before it), and leads others relying on withdrawal or poorly-timed "safe day" avoidance to underestimate their actual risk of pregnancy from intercourse well before ovulation.

How Contraceptives Change the Cycle

Combined hormonal contraception doesn't just prevent pregnancy — it fundamentally flattens the hormonal choreography from Module 4 into something much quieter.

Top: the natural estradiol peak that triggers the LH surge and ovulation. Bottom: exogenous estrogen/progestin on combined contraception, held low and flat via steady negative feedback — the peak that would trigger the LH surge never happens.

The Mechanism

Combined hormonal contraceptives (pill, patch, ring) supply a steady, exogenous dose of estrogen and progestin. Because the hypothalamus and pituitary sense these hormones are already present, they suppress endogenous GnRH, FSH, and LH release via negative feedback — the pituitary never sees the sustained high-estradiol signal (Module 4's >200 pg/mL for >50 hours) that normally flips feedback from negative to positive and triggers the LH surge. No LH surge means no ovulation, which is the primary mechanism of action. Secondary mechanisms (thickened cervical mucus, thinned endometrium) provide backup contraceptive effect and also explain the reduced menstrual flow and cramping many users experience.

What This Means Practically

  • "Withdrawal bleeding" on a traditional cyclic pill pack is not a real menstrual period in the physiologic sense — it's a response to the scheduled hormone-free interval, not to a natural corpus luteum regression. This is why extended-cycle or continuous regimens (skipping the placebo week) are physiologically reasonable, not just a convenience hack.
  • Progestin-only methods (POP, DMPA, implant, hormonal IUD) suppress ovulation to varying degrees depending on dose/method — the hormonal IUD, for example, works predominantly through local endometrial/cervical mucus effects with many users still ovulating, while DMPA reliably suppresses ovulation systemically.
  • Mood and libido effects some users report are biologically plausible given how much this flattens the natural estradiol/progesterone choreography described in Module 4 — though individual response varies widely, and for many the flatter hormonal profile is neutral or an improvement (particularly for those with significant PMS/PMDD or menstrual migraine tied to the natural hormone swings).
  • Fertility returns quickly after stopping most methods (pill, patch, ring, hormonal IUD) since the suppression is actively maintained rather than a lingering effect — DMPA is the notable exception, with return to ovulation sometimes delayed several months to a year after the last injection.

How Early Pregnancy Changes the Cycle

Pregnancy doesn't add new hormones so much as hijack the existing luteal-phase machinery — and then hands the job off to an entirely new organ a few weeks later.

hCG from the implanting embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, sustaining progesterone through roughly week 6–7. A brief progesterone dip appears around week 7 as the corpus luteum's output declines — right as the placenta is ramping up to take over. By roughly week 10, the placenta is the dominant progesterone source.

The Luteal-Placental Shift, Step by Step

1
Implantation (~6–10 days post-ovulation): the implanting embryo's syncytiotrophoblast begins secreting hCG, which is structurally similar enough to LH to bind LH receptors on the corpus luteum.
2
hCG rescues the corpus luteum — normally the corpus luteum regresses after ~14 days without a pregnancy signal (triggering menses); hCG prevents this regression and keeps it actively producing progesterone.
3
Corpus luteum carries the pregnancy through ~week 6–7 — removing the corpus luteum before this point reliably causes miscarriage, confirming it's the sole meaningful progesterone source this early.
4
The luteal-placental shift (~weeks 6–10, transient progesterone dip ~week 7): the developing placenta begins independent progesterone production, gradually overtaking the declining corpus luteum. The dip reflects the corpus luteum's output falling faster than the placenta's is ramping up — a normal, transient window, not itself a danger sign in an otherwise normal pregnancy.
5
By ~week 10, the placenta is fully in charge — progesterone climbs steadily for the rest of pregnancy, reaching roughly 6–8× luteal-phase levels by term, entirely independent of ovarian function from this point on.
Why this matters clinically Tier A

This is the biologic rationale for progesterone supplementation in early IVF pregnancies and in patients with a history of recurrent early loss — supplementing exogenous progesterone bridges the gap if corpus luteum function is inadequate (e.g., after egg retrieval disrupts normal luteal function), specifically covering the window before the placenta reliably takes over around week 10.

How Aging Changes the Cycle

The same four hormones from Module 4, but the underlying follicle supply that makes the whole system work is finite — and its decline, not any single hormone, is the real driver.

  • The core mechanism is follicle depletion, not hormone "wearing out" — a woman is born with her lifetime supply of oocytes; by the late 30s/early 40s, the remaining follicle pool is smaller and less responsive to FSH, so it takes progressively more FSH to recruit a dominant follicle each cycle.
  • Early sign: rising FSH, shortening follicular phase — cycles get shorter first (a compressed follicular phase), often years before periods become irregular in length or heavy/light pattern — AMH (reflecting the remaining follicle pool) declines earliest and most steadily of any hormone marker, which is why it's used clinically to estimate ovarian reserve.
  • Later: anovulatory cycles become common — without a dominant follicle producing the sustained high-estradiol signal, the LH surge doesn't reliably occur, so progesterone's characteristic post-ovulatory rise (Module 4) becomes inconsistent — this is the hormonal basis of perimenopausal irregular bleeding.
  • Estradiol itself becomes erratic, not just low — perimenopause is characterized by unpredictable swings, sometimes with higher peaks than a younger reproductive-age cycle, before the sustained decline of true menopause — which is part of why perimenopausal symptoms (mood, vasomotor) can be more volatile than postmenopausal ones.

Where Women Aren't Studied

Not an abstract grievance — a documented regulatory history with a specific start date, a specific correction, and measurable downstream consequences that are still being uncovered.

The Regulatory History

1
1977 — FDA guidance excludes women of "childbearing potential" from Phase I and early Phase II drug trials, a reaction to the thalidomide and DES birth-defect tragedies of the prior decade. The exclusion was broadly applied — sweeping in single women, women using contraception, and women whose partners had had a vasectomy, not just those actively trying to conceive.
2
1985–1990: an NIH advisory committee recommends including women in research (1986); the NIH establishes the Office of Research on Women's Health (1990); Bernadine Healy, MD becomes the NIH's first female director (1991) and launches the Women's Health Initiative.
3
1993 — the correction: the NIH Revitalization Act legally mandates inclusion of women and minorities in NIH-funded clinical research, explicitly stating cost is not an acceptable reason for exclusion. Weeks later, the FDA's own "Guideline for the Study and Evaluation of Gender Differences in the Clinical Evaluation of Drugs" reverses the 1977 policy and calls for routine sex-specific pharmacokinetic analysis.
4
The result: a roughly 16-year window (1977–1993) during which most drugs on the market today for chronic conditions were originally studied with women's participation actively restricted — a large share of the current pharmacopoeia predates the correction, which is exactly why sex-specific dosing/safety data remains incomplete for so many established drugs even now.

Case Study 1 — ADHD Diagnosis

The actual numbers

Childhood ADHD is diagnosed in boys at roughly a 3:1 to 4:1 ratio versus girls — but by adulthood that ratio closes to nearly 1:1, which is only mathematically possible if a large share of women were living with undiagnosed ADHD throughout childhood and adolescence. Survey-based research estimates 50–75% of women with ADHD remain undiagnosed. A 2025 mixed-methods study of women with late-diagnosed ADHD documented consistent themes of internalized shame, low self-esteem, and being dismissed by medical professionals prior to diagnosis — with diagnosis itself described by participants as revelatory to their sense of self.

Why the gap exists: diagnostic criteria were developed and validated predominantly in studies of boys, who more often present with the overt hyperactive/impulsive/externalizing symptoms clinicians are trained to recognize. Girls and women more often present with the inattentive subtype — internalized, less disruptive — and are more likely to actively mask/camouflage symptoms to meet social expectations, a compensatory strategy that itself contributes to burnout and delayed recognition.

Case Study 2 — Cardiovascular Disease Presentation

The actual numbers

A frequently cited symptom-recognition study found men were significantly more likely than women to report chest symptoms during a confirmed heart attack, while women more often reported nausea, palpitations, dyspnea, fainting, back pain, and pain between the shoulder blades instead. A qualitative study of women aged 30–55 hospitalized with acute MI found the presence of these atypical symptoms directly contributed to delayed care-seeking — some women delayed 3 hours to 5 days before seeking help, often attributing symptoms to non-cardiac causes and prioritizing family/household responsibilities over their own symptoms.

Why the gap exists: diagnostic criteria, public health messaging, and clinician pattern-recognition were built around the male-typical chest-pain-centric presentation for decades, in a research landscape where cardiovascular trials historically enrolled predominantly male participants — a direct downstream consequence of the pre-1993 exclusion described above.

Case Study 3 — Drug Dosing & Pharmacokinetics

The actual numbers

Zolpidem (Ambien) is the clearest, most consequential example: approved in 1992 — before the correction took full effect — with a single dose recommended for all adults. Post-marketing data eventually showed women have roughly 35% lower zolpidem clearance than men and 40–50% higher blood concentrations the morning after a standard dose, independent of body weight, driving next-day driving impairment risk. In 2013 — 21 years after approval — the FDA cut the recommended starting dose in half for women specifically. It remains one of the only widely used drugs with an explicit sex-based dosing difference on its label; most approved drugs still lack sex-specific pharmacokinetic dosing guidance despite known physiological differences in absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.

Why the gap exists: the original approval-era pharmacokinetic studies almost certainly underrepresented women, consistent with the era's broader research pattern — the difference wasn't caught until real-world adverse event surveillance accumulated over two decades of marketed use.

Case Study 4 — Autoimmune Disease

The actual numbers

Roughly 80% of autoimmune disease patients are women, yet the average time to an autoimmune diagnosis is 4.6 years, typically after seeing 4 or more physicians. For lupus specifically — a disease with a strong female predominance — approximately 50% of patients report an initial misdiagnosis, with an average 2-year diagnostic delay. Survey data from autoimmune patient advocacy groups found that nearly half of patients were told their symptoms were psychosomatic before receiving a correct diagnosis.

Why the gap exists: early autoimmune symptoms (fatigue, diffuse joint pain, brain fog) are nonspecific and overlap heavily with anxiety/depression presentations, and a 2024 Stanford study identified a specific X-chromosome-linked molecular mechanism (the Xist RNA molecule) as a plausible biological contributor to female-predominant autoimmunity — an active, still-developing research area that illustrates how much about sex-based immune differences remains to be worked out, decades after the exclusion era formally ended.

The throughline across all four examples

In each case, the gap isn't that women's bodies are inherently more "complicated" — it's that diagnostic criteria, drug dosing studies, and clinical pattern-recognition were built predominantly around male-typical data, and are only being corrected as researchers specifically go looking for the sex-based difference. The 1993 policy correction opened the door; closing the actual knowledge gap is still ongoing, case by case, disease by disease.

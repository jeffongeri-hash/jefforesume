Metabolic Counter-Regulation Matrix

Cortisol, GH, glucagon, and catecholamines all oppose insulin through different mechanisms — the same four-hormone team shows up together in nearly every stress response in this atlas.

The TeamFour Hormones, One Job

HormoneMechanism against insulinCross-reference
CortisolAntagonizes peripheral glucose uptake, drives hepatic gluconeogenesis, permissive for the other threeVolume 1 HPA · Volume 3a steroid-induced hyperglycemia
GlucagonDirect hepatic glycogenolysis/gluconeogenesis via Gs/cAMPVolume 1 pancreatic section
GHDirect, IGF-1-independent reduction in peripheral glucose uptake; lipolysis mobilizes competing fuelVolume 2 GH/IGF-1
Catecholaminesα2-mediated direct inhibition of insulin secretion + β-mediated glycogenolysis/lipolysisVolume 2 catecholamines

All four rise together during acute physiologic stress (illness, surgery, trauma, fasting) — this is a coordinated team, not four independent signals, and it's exactly why critically ill patients reliably become hyperglycemic regardless of baseline diabetes status ("stress hyperglycemia").

Clinical InteractionCortisol Excess + Pre-existing Insulin Resistance

This is the mechanistic core of Volume 3a's steroid-induced hyperglycemia section and Volume 6's Cushing's scenario card later in this file: a patient with baseline insulin resistance (obesity, prediabetes, PCOS) has far less physiologic reserve to absorb additional cortisol-driven antagonism — the same glucocorticoid dose that's metabolically trivial in an insulin-sensitive patient can unmask overt diabetes in one who was already compensating maximally.

Clinical InteractionGH Excess (Acromegaly) and Diabetes

Chronic GH excess produces clinically significant insulin resistance independent of cortisol — a meaningful minority of acromegaly patients develop overt diabetes, and glycemic control often improves measurably after biochemical remission (Volume 2/3c), directly demonstrating GH's causal contribution rather than mere association.

Clinical InteractionCatecholamine Excess (Pheochromocytoma) and Glucose

Pheochromocytoma can cause hyperglycemia or unmask latent diabetes via the same α2-mediated insulin suppression covered in Volume 2 — glucose often normalizes after tumor resection, another case where a "separate" endocrine diagnosis and glycemic control are mechanistically the same problem rather than two coincidental issues.

Exogenous Testosterone → InsulinThe Interaction Jeff Asked About First

Flagged back in Volume 1's HPG-male section and now the direct payoff: exogenous testosterone/anabolic steroid effects on insulin sensitivity are genuinely bidirectional and dose-dependent, not a simple "worse" or "better" answer.

ContextEffect on insulin sensitivityMechanism
Physiologic replacement in a truly hypogonadal, often obese manOften improves insulin sensitivityReduces visceral fat mass over months, raises SHBG back toward normal, reverses part of the closed loop above

Why both directions are true at once
Dose is the variable that flips the sign: replacement-dose testosterone in a deficient man corrects an existing metabolic deficit, while anabolic-dose testosterone in someone without deficiency pushes a different, supraphysiologic-exposure mechanism. This full dose-response picture is why "does testosterone help or hurt insulin resistance" doesn't have a single answer independent of dose and baseline status — exactly the nuance worth carrying into any patient conversation about exogenous androgen use (see the dedicated scenario card below for the fuller picture, including lipid and hematologic effects).

Leptin ↔ HPG Axis

As established in Volume 2: falling leptin (starvation, very low body fat, eating disorders) suppresses GnRH pulsatility, producing hypothalamic amenorrhea in women and reduced testosterone in men — a genuine bidirectional signal, since sex hormones also modestly influence leptin sensitivity. This is the mechanistic reason functional hypothalamic amenorrhea and low-testosterone-from-underfeeding both resolve with adequate energy availability restored, often before body weight normalizes completely — the leptin signal responds to energy availability, not just stored body fat.

Estrogen ↔ Insulin

Premenopausal women have a relative insulin-sensitivity advantage partly attributable to estrogen's direct favorable effects on adipocyte function and inflammatory tone — the menopausal transition's estrogen decline (Volume 3b) is part of why metabolic syndrome risk and central fat redistribution accelerate around this time, independent of chronological aging alone.

Thyroid × Everything Else

Thyroid hormone's permissive relationship with catecholamines and its direct receptor-mediated effect on lipid handling are the two interactions with the most bedside relevance.

Thyroid × CatecholaminesThe Permissive Relationship

As established in Volume 1: thyroid hormone upregulates β-adrenergic receptor density and post-receptor signaling — it doesn't produce catecholamines itself, but amplifies their effect. This is why hyperthyroid symptoms (tachycardia, tremor, anxiety, heat intolerance) overlap so heavily with sympathetic excess, why β-blockers are effective symptomatic therapy in hyperthyroidism/thyroid storm before definitive treatment takes effect (Volume 4), and why a pheochromocytoma and hyperthyroidism can look clinically similar at the bedside despite completely different underlying pathology — genuinely worth keeping on the differential for each other in an unexplained hyperadrenergic-appearing patient.

Thyroid × LipidsHypothyroidism as a Reversible Dyslipidemia Cause

As established in Volume 1: thyroid hormone upregulates hepatic LDL-receptor expression. Hypothyroidism — even subclinical disease (Volume 3c) — reduces LDL clearance, raising LDL cholesterol independent of diet or genetics. This is a genuinely high-yield, easily reversible secondary dyslipidemia cause: checking TSH is standard practice when working up new or worsening hyperlipidemia, and treating the hypothyroidism often improves the lipid panel without any lipid-directed therapy at all. The reverse direction matters too — hyperthyroidism lowers LDL, so a surprisingly low LDL in someone with thyrotoxicosis symptoms is a consistent, not paradoxical, finding.

Thyroid × Reproduction

Both hypo- and hyperthyroidism disrupt the HPG axis — hypothyroidism can raise prolactin via TRH cross-stimulation (Volume 1/2), suppressing GnRH pulsatility and causing menstrual irregularity/anovulation; severe hyperthyroidism can also disrupt cycles through excess SHBG production altering free sex hormone availability (Volume 1 SHBG regulation table). This is why thyroid function is checked early in any infertility or menstrual irregularity workup, alongside prolactin.

Thyroid × Bone

As established in Volume 1 and revisited in Volume 3c: thyroid hormone is permissive for bone turnover, and excess (whether endogenous hyperthyroidism or iatrogenic over-replacement/TSH-suppressive levothyroxine dosing) accelerates bone resorption, independently lowering bone mineral density — particularly relevant in postmenopausal women already losing estrogen's bone-protective effect (Volume 3b), where the two mechanisms compound rather than simply adding.

PCOS — The Full Mechanism

Every thread flagged across Volumes 1, 2, and 6 converges here — this is the payoff scenario for the two-cell ovarian theory, SHBG regulation, and the insulin-androgen loop all at once.

Diagnosis2023 International Guideline — Rotterdam Criteria

Requires 2 of 3 (after excluding other causes — thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, nonclassic CAH via 17-OHP, Volume 1):

Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism Ovulatory dysfunction (irregular cycles) Polycystic ovarian morphology — ≥20 follicles (2–9mm) on ultrasound, or elevated AMH as an alternative in adults (not adolescents)

If both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are present, imaging/AMH isn't required at all. In adolescents, ultrasound/AMH shouldn't be used to diagnose given low specificity so soon after menarche — both hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction are required instead.

MechanismThe Full Loop, Assembled

  1. Gonadotropin imbalance — relatively elevated LH:FSH ratio (Volume 1) over-drives theca cell androgen production while relatively lower FSH limits granulosa cell aromatization capacity to convert it all to estrogen.
  2. Insulin resistance (present in a majority of PCOS patients, independent of BMI) directly amplifies theca cell androgen synthesis via the insulin receptor's mitogenic (Ras/MAPK) signaling arm (Volume 1 pancreatic section) — theca cells retain robust mitogenic insulin signaling even when classic metabolic insulin signaling is impaired elsewhere.
  3. Hyperinsulinemia suppresses hepatic SHBG synthesis (Volume 1 SHBG table), raising free/bioactive androgen independent of any change in total testosterone — part of why total testosterone can look deceptively normal in a clearly hyperandrogenic patient.
  4. Peripheral consequences: hyperandrogenism → hirsutism, acne, androgenic alopecia; anovulation → irregular cycles, infertility; the closed metabolic loop (Section 2 of this volume) tends toward further weight gain and worsening insulin resistance over time without intervention.

Beyond ReproductionWhy PCOS Is a Metabolic Diagnosis, Not Just a Gynecologic One

The 2023 guideline explicitly strengthens recognition of PCOS's broader footprint: metabolic risk factors, cardiovascular disease risk, obstructive sleep apnea, and a markedly elevated prevalence of anxiety/depression are now considered core features requiring active screening — not incidental comorbidities. All patients with PCOS should be assessed for cardiovascular risk factors as a matter of routine care, not only when symptoms prompt it.

Exogenous Androgen & Anabolic Steroid Use

Every system this atlas has touched gets affected at once — HPG suppression, metabolic effects, lipids, hematology, and cardiac risk, assembled into one patient encounter.

HPG AxisThe Foundational Effect

As established in Volume 1: exogenous androgen suppresses GnRH/LH/FSH via the same negative feedback the axis uses endogenously — the axis cannot distinguish source. Endogenous testosterone production collapses (testicular atrophy from LH withdrawal), spermatogenesis is suppressed independently via FSH withdrawal and loss of the ~50–100x intratesticular testosterone gradient Sertoli cells require locally (Volume 1). This is the single most predictable, universal effect of any exogenous androgen exposure, dose and duration dependent but never fully avoidable.

Metabolic EffectsDose-Dependent, Not One-Directional

See this volume's Sex Hormones × Metabolism section for the full mechanism — supraphysiologic/anabolic dosing tends to worsen insulin sensitivity acutely, in contrast to the improvement typically seen with physiologic replacement dosing in genuinely deficient men.

LipidsA Consistently Adverse Effect

Unlike the metabolic/insulin effects above, the lipid effect of supraphysiologic androgen use is more consistently unfavorable: lowers HDL substantially (often 20–70% depending on dose/formulation) and can raise LDL — oral 17α-alkylated androgens (historically used for their oral bioavailability, see Volume 3b's discussion of why these were avoided) have the most pronounced hepatic lipid and hepatotoxicity effects of any androgen formulation.

HematologicErythrocytosis

As established in Volume 3b: androgens stimulate erythropoiesis (part of why TRT monitoring includes hematocrit) — supraphysiologic doses amplify this substantially, and secondary erythrocytosis from anabolic steroid use is a recognized, sometimes clinically significant finding warranting the same monitoring/consideration as any other secondary erythrocytosis cause (Volume 2 EPO section).

Cardiac

Chronic supraphysiologic androgen exposure is associated with left ventricular hypertrophy, impaired diastolic function, and accelerated atherosclerosis in observational data — distinct from and additive to the lipid-mediated risk above. This is a genuinely different risk profile from the TRAVERSE trial's reassuring physiologic-replacement-dose data (Volume 3b) — that trial's findings should never be extrapolated to supraphysiologic/anabolic use, a fundamentally different dose regime with different physiology.

Practical Approach to Disclosure

Patients using non-prescribed anabolic steroids often don't volunteer this history — clues include unexpectedly high hematocrit, suppressed LH/FSH with low-normal-to-high testosterone (paradoxical given the suppression, if using testosterone itself), significant HDL suppression out of proportion to other risk factors, or testicular atrophy on exam. A nonjudgmental, direct question about supplement and performance-enhancing drug use is more productive than inferring from labs alone.

Metabolic Syndrome — The Full Cascade

Not a single hormone disorder but the visible endpoint of insulin resistance pulling on nearly every axis in this atlas simultaneously.

Diagnostic Criteria3 of 5

Waist circumference — elevated (population/ethnicity-specific cutoffs) Triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL HDL <40 mg/dL (men) / <50 mg/dL (women) Blood pressure ≥130/85 Fasting glucose ≥100 mg/dL

Assembling the Cascade From This Atlas

  1. Visceral adiposity increases peripheral aromatization of androgens to estrogen and drives leptin resistance (Volume 2 adipokines) — the hypothalamus stops responding appropriately to elevated leptin signal.
  2. Insulin resistance develops, with compensatory hyperinsulinemia (Volume 1 pancreatic section) — the beta cell working harder to overcome peripheral resistance.
  3. Hyperinsulinemia suppresses hepatic SHBG synthesis (Volume 1 SHBG table) — raising free androgen in women (contributing to PCOS overlap, this volume's PCOS card) and, paradoxically alongside falling total testosterone from aromatization, disrupting the androgen picture in men (Volume 3b).
  4. Hyperinsulinemia + visceral fat-derived inflammatory cytokines promote hepatic triglyceride synthesis and reduce HDL — the dyslipidemia component of the syndrome.
  5. Insulin resistance activates the sympathetic nervous system and RAAS (via mechanisms including hyperinsulinemia's direct renal sodium-retaining effect) — contributing to the hypertension component (Volume 2 RAAS section).
  6. Cortisol's contribution: even without frank Cushing's syndrome, subtly elevated tissue-level cortisol activity (via 11β-HSD1 in visceral fat, Volume 1) is implicated in worsening the whole picture — part of why chronic stress and disrupted sleep are epidemiologically linked to metabolic syndrome risk.
Why this reframe matters clinically
Treating metabolic syndrome as five independent numbers to normalize misses that they share a common upstream driver — interventions that address insulin resistance directly (weight loss, exercise per Volume 1/2's lifestyle evidence, GLP-1 agonists) tend to improve multiple criteria simultaneously, while treating each number with a separate drug (a statin, an antihypertensive, a separate glucose-lowering agent) treats the downstream symptoms without touching the shared mechanism.

Where It Intersects the Rest of This Atlas

PCOS (this volume) — same insulin-SHBG-androgen loop in a reproductive-age woman Steroid-induced hyperglycemia (Volume 3a) — exogenous cortisol accelerates the same pathway pharmacologically Low testosterone with obesity (Volume 3b) — the aromatization/SHBG side of the same cascade in men Hypothyroidism and lipids (this volume, Thyroid × Everything) — a second, independent contributor to the same dyslipidemia that should be excluded before attributing dyslipidemia to metabolic syndrome alone

Polyglandular Autoimmune Syndromes

Why one autoimmune endocrine diagnosis should always prompt screening for others — the closing scenario, tying together every autoimmune mechanism scattered across this atlas.

Type 1 (APS-1 / APECED)

Autosomal recessive, caused by mutations in AIRE (autoimmune regulator gene) — AIRE normally drives expression of peripheral tissue antigens in the thymus, allowing developing T-cells to be screened against self-antigens and eliminated if self-reactive; loss of this function allows autoreactive T-cells targeting endocrine (and other) tissue to escape into circulation. Presents in childhood, diagnosed by ≥2 of the classic triad:

Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (often the first sign) Hypoparathyroidism (Volume 1 calcium axis) Primary adrenal insufficiency (Volume 1 HPA axis)

Also associated with autoimmune hepatitis, malabsorption, vitiligo, pernicious anemia, type 1 diabetes, primary hypogonadism, and primary hypothyroidism.

Type 2 (Schmidt Syndrome)

Polygenic (HLA-associated), adult-onset (typically ~30s), female-predominant. Defined by Addison's disease plus autoimmune thyroid disease and/or type 1 diabetes — this is the combination of autoimmune diagnoses from three different volumes of this atlas (Volume 1 HPA, Volume 1 HPT, Volume 1 pancreatic) occurring together in one patient more often than chance alone would predict.

CombinationFrequency
Addison's + Hashimoto's thyroiditisMost common presentation
Addison's + type 1 diabetesCommon
Addison's + Graves' diseaseLess common

Also associated with primary hypogonadism, myasthenia gravis, celiac disease, vitiligo, and pernicious anemia.

Type 3

Autoimmune thyroid disease plus another autoimmune condition excluding Addison's disease (the presence of Addison's reclassifies it as Type 2) — commonly paired with type 1 diabetes, but also reported with rheumatologic conditions (lupus, myasthenia gravis) and hematologic autoimmune disease (ITP).

Why This Matters at the Bedside

One autoimmune endocrine diagnosis raises the pretest probability of another
A patient newly diagnosed with Addison's disease warrants screening for thyroid autoimmunity (TSH, anti-TPO — Volume 1/3c) and type 1 diabetes risk; a patient with Hashimoto's and unexplained fatigue, hyperpigmentation, or hypotension should prompt consideration of coexisting adrenal insufficiency rather than attributing everything to the known thyroid diagnosis. This pattern-recognition habit — treating one confirmed autoimmune endocrinopathy as a flag to actively screen for others, not just a reason to watch passively — is the single most transferable lesson from this scenario card, and closes the loop on autoimmune mechanisms introduced independently across Volumes 1, 2, and 5 (ICI-induced endocrinopathies, which mimic this multi-gland pattern through a different, drug-induced mechanism rather than inherited autoimmune susceptibility).

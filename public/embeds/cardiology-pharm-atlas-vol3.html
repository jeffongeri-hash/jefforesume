02 / Antithrombotics & Remaining Agents

Unfractionated Heparin & Heparin Drips

The oldest anticoagulant still in daily use — and still the default choice whenever rapid reversibility or unpredictable renal function make it the safer bet over LMWH or a DOAC.

Mechanism Binds antithrombin III and accelerates its inhibition of thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa by roughly 1000-fold — unlike LMWH, UFH inhibits both thrombin and Xa in a roughly 1:1 ratio, which is part of why aPTT (thrombin-time-sensitive) is the classic monitoring test.

Weight-Based Dosing by Indication

Indication changes the target intensity — ACS dosing runs slightly lower than full-dose VTE treatment dosing in most protocols, though institutional nomograms vary.

aPTT-Based Titration

The classic nomogram approach: check aPTT 6 hours after the bolus/infusion start (and 6 hours after any rate change), then titrate based on where the result falls relative to the therapeutic range. Once two consecutive therapeutic levels are achieved, monitoring can space out to once daily.

Anti-Xa Monitoring — An Increasingly Preferred Alternative

Anti-factor Xa assays are less affected by baseline coagulopathy, lupus anticoagulant, or other conditions that can confound aPTT — many institutions now monitor heparin drips via anti-Xa level (target 0.3-0.7 IU/mL) instead of or alongside aPTT, particularly in patients where aPTT is known to be unreliable at baseline.

Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT)

A paradoxical PRO-thrombotic syndrome, not just thrombocytopenia

Immune-mediated HIT (antibodies against platelet factor 4-heparin complexes) typically develops 5-10 days after heparin exposure (or within hours in a patient with recent prior heparin exposure — "rapid-onset HIT") and paradoxically causes both thrombocytopenia AND a high risk of new arterial/venous thrombosis. The 4Ts score (degree of Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher explanation) helps estimate pretest probability. Any significant platelet drop (typically >50% from baseline) in a patient on heparin warrants HIT consideration — stop ALL heparin products (including heparin flushes and heparin-coated catheters) and switch to a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban or bivalirudin — see the next section) while confirmatory testing (PF4 antibody, serotonin release assay) is pending. Do not simply switch to LMWH — significant cross-reactivity exists.

Reversal

AgentDosingSpecial Considerations
Protamine sulfate 1mg IV per 100 units of heparin given in the preceding ~2-3 hours (max single dose typically 50mg), infused slowly over 10 min Forms an inactive complex directly with heparin — reliable, rapid reversal. Rapid infusion risks hypotension and anaphylactoid reactions (higher risk with fish allergy, prior protamine exposure, or NPH insulin use given cross-reactivity with protamine-containing insulin formulations); have resuscitation support available for the infusion.
Why heparin is still chosen over LMWH/DOACs in specific scenarios

Short half-life (~60-90 min) and complete reversibility with protamine make UFH the preferred anticoagulant whenever a procedure might be needed on short notice (active ACS heading toward possible urgent CABG, unstable patients, or significant renal impairment where LMWH/DOAC clearance becomes unpredictable) — this is precisely why it remains the default in critical care and periprocedural settings despite being older and requiring more intensive monitoring than the alternatives.

Who to call
Hematology for HIT workup and management; pharmacy for nomogram-based dosing questions or complex renal/hepatic dose adjustments.

LMWH, Fondaparinux & Parenteral Direct Thrombin Inhibitors

The subcutaneous alternatives to a heparin drip, plus the two agents that keep HIT patients anticoagulated without touching heparin at all.

Low Molecular Weight Heparin

Smaller heparin fragments with more Xa-selective activity than thrombin inhibition (roughly 3-4:1 anti-Xa:anti-IIa ratio, vs. UFH's roughly 1:1) — more predictable pharmacokinetics allow fixed or weight-based dosing without routine monitoring in most patients.

DrugTreatment DoseProphylactic DoseRenal AdjustmentSpecial Considerations
Enoxaparin 1mg/kg SC q12h, or 1.5mg/kg SC daily 30-40mg SC daily-BID depending on risk CrCl <30: reduce to 1mg/kg SC daily (treatment) or 30mg SC daily (prophylaxis) Preferred anticoagulant for VTE in pregnancy (see Vol. 6) and cancer-associated thrombosis; anti-Xa monitoring (target range varies by indication/timing of level) reserved for extremes of weight, renal impairment, or pregnancy rather than routine use
Dalteparin 200 units/kg SC daily (first month), then 150 units/kg SC daily 2500-5000 units SC daily Use with caution/reduce dose in significant renal impairment Historically the preferred LMWH specifically for cancer-associated thrombosis (CLOT trial) — though enoxaparin and DOACs have both since shown comparable efficacy in this population in more recent trials
No reliable reversal for LMWH

Protamine only partially reverses LMWH's anti-Xa activity (it fully neutralizes the anti-IIa component but only partially the anti-Xa component) — this incomplete reversibility is one of the practical reasons UFH remains preferred over LMWH whenever rapid, complete reversal might be needed.

Fondaparinux

MechanismDosingSpecial Considerations

Parenteral Direct Thrombin Inhibitors (HIT-Safe Anticoagulation)

Both agents directly inhibit thrombin without going through antithrombin — this is exactly why they don't cross-react with HIT antibodies (which target platelet factor 4-heparin complexes, not thrombin itself) and are first-line for anticoagulation during active HIT.

DrugDosingSpecial Considerations
Argatroban 2mcg/kg/min IV infusion (start LOWER, ~0.5-1.2mcg/kg/min, in hepatic impairment or critical illness), titrate to aPTT 1.5-3x baseline Hepatically cleared — the preferred parenteral DTI in significant renal impairment. Falsely elevates INR, complicating the transition to warfarin (see the Warfarin section for the specific bridging technique needed).
Bivalirudin HIT/general anticoagulation: 0.15-0.2mg/kg/hr IV infusion, titrate to aPTT; PCI-specific dosing differs (bolus 0.75mg/kg, infusion 1.75mg/kg/hr) — see Vol. 4 for the PCI-specific context Renally cleared — the preferred parenteral DTI in significant hepatic impairment (opposite preference pattern from argatroban). Very short half-life (~25 min) allows relatively quick offset if needed.
Argatroban vs. bivalirudin — remember the clearance organs are swapped

Argatroban is hepatically cleared (use in renal failure, avoid/reduce in liver failure); bivalirudin is renally cleared (use in liver failure, adjust in renal failure) — this single fact drives most of the selection logic between the two in HIT management, and is worth memorizing as a pair rather than separately.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Hematology for any HIT diagnosis requiring argatroban/bivalirudin selection and the eventual warfarin transition strategy.
Urgent evaluation
Significant renal impairment discovered in a patient on fondaparinux (no reversal exists) or LMWH at treatment dose — reassess anticoagulant choice.
Who to call
Hematology for HIT management; pharmacy for renal/hepatic dose adjustment across this entire drug category.

Warfarin

Still the only oral anticoagulant proven safe for mechanical heart valves — and still the one with the most genuinely counterintuitive early-therapy physiology of any drug in this atlas.

Mechanism Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1), blocking the recycling of vitamin K needed to gamma-carboxylate (activate) clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X — as well as the anticoagulant proteins C and S.
The early-therapy procoagulant paradox

Protein C has a short half-life (~8 hours) — shorter than factors II, IX, and X (which range from ~24 hours to several days). In the first days of warfarin therapy, protein C activity drops faster than the procoagulant factors, creating a transient relatively procoagulant state. This is exactly why warfarin is always started with bridging heparin/LMWH coverage in patients needing immediate anticoagulation, and why warfarin-induced skin necrosis (a rare but serious complication) occurs specifically in patients with underlying protein C deficiency during this early window.

Genetic variability is real and substantial

CYP2C9 (the enzyme that metabolizes warfarin) and VKORC1 (warfarin's target enzyme) polymorphisms together account for a large proportion of the variability in warfarin dose requirements between individuals — some patients are genetically "sensitive" and need a fraction of the standard dose, others are "resistant" and need considerably more. Pharmacogenomic testing exists but isn't routine practice everywhere; the practical takeaway is that slow, careful titration based on actual INR response matters more than any starting-dose formula.

IV vitamin K — infuse slowly

Rapid IV administration of vitamin K carries a rare but real anaphylactoid reaction risk — infuse slowly (over at least 20-30 minutes) and have supportive care available, rather than pushing it rapidly even in an urgent bleeding scenario.

Drug & Dietary Interactions — A Genuinely Long List

Potentiators (raise INR)

Amiodarone (see Vol. 2 — reduce warfarin dose by ~30-50% when starting), many antibiotics (fluoroquinolones, macrolides, metronidazole, TMP-SMX), azole antifungals, and acute illness/reduced oral intake (reduced dietary vitamin K plus altered metabolism) are among the most common culprits — any new antibiotic course in a warfarin patient warrants closer INR monitoring.

Antagonists (lower INR)

Consistent (not necessarily low) dietary vitamin K intake matters more than avoiding vitamin-K-rich foods entirely — sudden large changes in leafy green vegetable intake in either direction destabilize INR control more than steady, moderate intake. Rifampin and certain anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin) are notable pharmacologic antagonists via enzyme induction.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Anticoagulation clinic/pharmacy for complex dose titration or an unstable INR pattern despite consistent dosing; hematology for suspected warfarin-induced skin necrosis.
Urgent evaluation
Any significant bleeding on warfarin regardless of INR value, or any INR >10 even without bleeding.
Who to call
Hematology or the managing anticoagulation clinic for reversal-agent dosing and post-reversal anticoagulation restart planning; cardiac surgery specifically for mechanical valve patients needing perioperative bridging around a procedure.

The 5-10 day lead-in requirement — an easy detail to miss

Unlike apixaban and rivaroxaban (which can be started directly as monotherapy for VTE treatment), both edoxaban and dabigatran specifically require 5-10 days of initial parenteral anticoagulation (typically LMWH) before transitioning — this reflects how each drug was actually studied in its pivotal trials, not an arbitrary rule, and missing this step is a genuine prescribing error.

Reversal Agents — Current US Status

Andexanet alfa was withdrawn from the US market — December 22, 2025

Andexanet alfa (Andexxa), the specific reversal agent developed for factor Xa inhibitors, was voluntarily withdrawn from the US market after the ANNEXA-I trial showed a doubling of thrombotic events and thrombosis-related deaths compared to usual care (4F-PCC) in intracranial hemorrhage. This is a genuinely significant, recent change to standard practice — if your training predates this, update your mental model. 4-factor PCC (Kcentra) is now the de facto US standard for reversing apixaban/rivaroxaban/edoxaban-associated life-threatening bleeding. Andexanet alfa remains available in the EU, UK (marketed as Ondexxya), and some other countries — always verify current national/institutional guidance rather than assuming US status applies elsewhere.

Thrombolytics (Fibrinolytics)

All three agents convert plasminogen to plasmin, which degrades fibrin — they differ in fibrin specificity, half-life, and dosing convenience rather than fundamental mechanism.

Phenylephrine Pure alpha-1 agonist — vasoconstriction with NO inotropic or chronotropic effect IV infusion 0.5-5mcg/kg/min (dosing varies substantially by clinical context/protocol) Can reflexively slow heart rate (baroreceptor response to the pure vasoconstriction) — occasionally used deliberately for this rate-slowing property in specific tachyarrhythmia scenarios, but generally a less common choice than norepinephrine for routine shock management given the lack of inotropic support
Matching the agent to the physiology, not just reaching for "the pressor"

Cardiogenic shock with preserved blood pressure but poor perfusion favors an inotrope (dobutamine or milrinone) alone. Cardiogenic shock with hypotension favors norepinephrine (with an inotrope added if perfusion remains poor despite adequate MAP). Mixed cardiogenic-vasodilatory shock (common in sepsis-complicated cardiac patients, or late cardiogenic shock with vasoplegia) often needs both a vasopressor and an inotrope simultaneously — this is where combination therapy and sometimes mechanical circulatory support (see the Cardiothoracic Surgery Atlas's LVAD/MCS section) enter the picture.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/critical care for any patient requiring inotrope or vasopressor support in the setting of cardiogenic shock (see Vol. 5's SCAI staging framework) — this represents at least SCAI Stage C and warrants shock-team-level involvement.
ICU admission
Any patient on a continuous inotrope or vasopressor infusion requires ICU-level monitoring, full stop.
Who to call
Cardiology/critical care jointly for hemodynamic support titration and escalation planning, including mechanical circulatory support consideration when pharmacologic support proves inadequate.

PDE5 inhibitors + nitrates = potentially fatal hypotension

Sildenafil, tadalafil, and vardenafil all potentiate nitrate-induced vasodilation dramatically — concurrent use can cause severe, refractory hypotension. Always ask specifically about PDE5 inhibitor use (including recreational/undisclosed use) before administering any nitrate, and observe the drug-specific washout window (generally 24 hours for sildenafil/vardenafil, 48 hours for tadalafil given its longer half-life) before nitrate administration.

Why this pipeline matters even before approval

Recognizing an elevated Lp(a) today — even with no specific therapy yet available — still changes risk stratification and the intensity of managing every other modifiable risk factor (see Vol. 1a's lipid content) in that patient. CKD Dosing Considerations

Renal function silently changes the risk-benefit calculation for a huge share of this atlas — this section pulls every significant CKD-related dosing consideration from all three volumes into one cross-reference.

"Renally cleared" and "needs dose adjustment" are not the same question

Some drugs are renally cleared but don't need dose adjustment because their therapeutic index is wide (many statins). Others need adjustment specifically because reduced clearance raises drug levels into a narrow toxic range (digoxin, dofetilide). And some drugs need adjustment not because of drug accumulation but because CKD itself changes the drug's effect (loop diuretics needing HIGHER doses to reach the site of action). Treat each entry below on its own terms rather than assuming a single pattern.

Heart Failure Contraindications & Cautions

A patient's HF status should change your prescribing reflexes for drugs that have nothing to do with the heart on the surface — this section exists specifically because a drug being "for PAD" or "for pain" doesn't mean it's safe in a patient with reduced EF.

The example that prompted this section: cilostazol

Cilostazol is a phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor used for claudication symptom improvement in PAD (see Vascular Vol. 1) — but PDE-3 inhibition is also the mechanism behind milrinone's inotropic effect, and that same mechanism carries a class-wide mortality signal in heart failure. Cilostazol is contraindicated in heart failure of any severity, not just advanced/decompensated disease. A patient with both PAD and HF — a genuinely common combination given shared atherosclerotic risk factors — needs an alternative claudication strategy (supervised exercise, cilostazol alternatives, revascularization) rather than this drug.

The general pattern worth internalizing

Most HF-unsafe drugs fall into one of two mechanistic buckets: direct negative inotropy (non-DHP CCBs, disopyramide, Class Ic antiarrhythmics in scarred myocardium) or sodium/fluid retention (NSAIDs, thiazolidinediones, minoxidil, high-dose steroids). Once you recognize which bucket a new or unfamiliar drug falls into, you can reason about HF safety even for an agent not explicitly listed here — ask "does this reduce contractility?" or "does this retain sodium/water?" as your first-pass screen.

Consult now
Cardiology/heart failure specialist before starting any drug on the caution list in a patient with established HF, particularly reduced EF — a brief consult often identifies a safer alternative already familiar to the HF team.
Urgent evaluation
Any new or worsening HF symptoms temporally associated with starting a new medication — actively screen the medication list (including OTC NSAIDs) before assuming spontaneous disease progression.
Who to call
Heart failure cardiology for medication reconciliation and safer-alternative selection; pharmacy for systematic medication safety review in any complex HF patient with multiple comorbidities and prescribers.

01 / Acute MI Complications & Cardiogenic Shock

Cardiogenic Shock & SCAI Staging

Cardiogenic shock is a spectrum, not a binary — the SCAI SHOCK classification (2019, refined 2022) gives that spectrum a common language across every team involved in a patient's care.

The Complication Timeline — Where Shock Fits

Before diving into shock staging specifically, it's worth seeing the full picture: cardiogenic shock can result from the infarct itself, or from any of the mechanical complications on this timeline.

The "day 3-5 triad" (free wall rupture, papillary muscle rupture, VSD) represents the highest-density window for mechanical complications — heightened vigilance in this window is warranted for any patient with a large infarct or delayed reperfusion.

SCAI SHOCK Stages A–E

Most patients at risk never progress beyond Stage A; progressively fewer reach the more severe stages — but progression can happen rapidly, and staging should be reassessed continuously, not just at initial presentation.

Why this classification caught on

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology/critical care for any Stage B or higher — Stage B in particular is a "nebulous" category in the literature that can deteriorate rapidly and deserves proactive attention rather than a wait-and-watch approach.
Activate shock team
Stage C or higher — this is the threshold at which multidisciplinary shock team involvement (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, critical care) meaningfully changes outcomes per shock-team-model literature.
Transfer if no shock team/MCS capability on-site
Any Stage C or higher patient at a facility without mechanical circulatory support capability — early transfer before further deterioration is far preferable to transferring a Stage D/E patient who has already exhausted local resources.
Transport considerations
Vasoactive infusions must continue uninterrupted during transport with a critical-care-capable transport team; any patient already on MCS requires a transport team specifically trained in that device's management, and the receiving facility must have confirmed capacity before departure.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology and cardiac surgery jointly for shock complicating a mechanical complication (see following sections); critical care/cardiology for shock without a mechanical complication driving it.
02 / Acute MI Complications & Cardiogenic Shock

Mechanical Circulatory Support

Four devices, four different mechanisms — and one trap (ECMO's afterload problem) that catches even experienced teams.

Device Comparison

Each device unloads the heart (or bypasses it) through a fundamentally different mechanism — understanding how each works predicts both its benefits and its specific complications.

Interactive: Device Selection Walkthrough

Step 1 — Is this isolated LV failure, or biventricular/respiratory failure too?

Isolated LV failure with preserved oxygenation → IABP or Impella are reasonable starting points depending on support level needed. Biventricular failure or concurrent respiratory failure (severe hypoxemia, ARDS-like picture) → VA-ECMO provides both circulatory and oxygenation support that isolated LV devices cannot.

Step 2 — How much hemodynamic support is actually needed?

Mild-moderate shock, some native cardiac output preserved → IABP may suffice and is the fastest/simplest to insert. Moderate-severe shock needing several liters per minute of support → Impella (device size selected based on required flow) or TandemHeart. Refractory shock or shock with arrest → VA-ECMO for maximal support.

Step 3 — Is there a rhythm to trigger counterpulsation?

IABP requires a reasonably organized rhythm with a discernible pulse to trigger inflation/deflation timing — it performs poorly or not at all during VF/pulseless VT/severe arrhythmia. Impella and ECMO are continuous-flow and don't depend on a triggering rhythm, making them the better choice when malignant arrhythmia is part of the picture.

Step 4 — If using VA-ECMO, is the LV being adequately unloaded?

Watch for signs of LV distension (worsening pulmonary edema, lack of aortic valve opening on echo, rising PCWP) — this indicates the LV isn't being adequately decompressed against ECMO's retrograde aortic flow. Add a concurrent unloading strategy (Impella, IABP, atrial septostomy, or surgical vent) rather than accepting a distended, non-recovering LV.

The ECMO afterload trap, restated

This is worth repeating because it's genuinely counterintuitive: a device designed to support the failing heart can make LV function worse if used alone, because retrograde aortic flow increases the pressure the native LV must overcome. Any team initiating VA-ECMO for cardiogenic shock should have an explicit plan for LV unloading, not an implicit assumption that ECMO alone is sufficient.

2025 guideline context

The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI ACS guideline explicitly addresses cardiogenic shock management, including consideration of microaxial flow pumps (Impella-class devices) as part of a patient-specific risk-benefit approach — reflecting how central device selection has become to modern shock management rather than a last-resort afterthought.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Interventional cardiology and cardiac surgery jointly for any patient being considered for MCS — device selection benefits from both perspectives, particularly when surgical MCS or eventual durable support/transplant might be on the table.
Activate shock team protocol
Any SCAI Stage C or higher patient not responding to initial pharmacologic support — this is the threshold where MCS discussion should begin, not after prolonged escalating pressor doses.
Transfer if no MCS program on-site
Any patient likely to need MCS at a facility without that capability — transfer earlier rather than later; a patient who arrests during a delayed transfer decision represents a preventable systems failure.
Transport considerations
A patient already on IABP, Impella, or ECMO requires a specialized transport team credentialed in that specific device — this is not a standard critical care transport scenario. Confirm the receiving facility has confirmed bed and team availability for the specific device in place before departure.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for Impella/IABP; cardiac surgery and/or a dedicated ECMO team for VA-ECMO cannulation and management; the receiving shock program's on-call attending directly when arranging inter-facility transfer of a device-supported patient.
03 / Acute MI Complications & Cardiogenic Shock

Papillary Muscle Rupture

A new murmur and cardiogenic shock in the days after an inferior MI — one of the most time-critical surgical emergencies in all of cardiology.

Why the Posteromedial Muscle Fails

Blood supply asymmetry is the whole story here: the anterolateral papillary muscle receives dual supply from both the LAD (diagonal branches) and LCx (obtuse marginal branches), while the posteromedial papillary muscle depends solely on the posterior descending artery — typically a branch of the RCA. A single-vessel occlusion can infarct and rupture the posteromedial muscle; the anterolateral muscle is protected by its redundant supply.

Papillary muscle rupture (left) and ventricular septal rupture (right) present similarly — new murmur plus shock in the day 3-5 window — but differ in murmur location, echo findings, and the diagnostic oxygen step-up.
The murmur can be deceptively quiet

Counterintuitively, a very severe, acute mitral regurgitation from complete papillary muscle rupture can produce a surprisingly soft or even inaudible murmur — the massive regurgitant volume rapidly equalizes left atrial and left ventricular pressure, eliminating the pressure gradient that generates the murmur's intensity. Don't let a soft or absent murmur falsely reassure you in a patient with new cardiogenic shock after MI; the absence of a loud murmur does not rule this out.

Diagnostic Approach

Echocardiography

Transthoracic echo may show a flail mitral leaflet and severe MR on color Doppler; transesophageal echo offers substantially better visualization of the ruptured muscle/chordal apparatus if TTE is equivocal and the patient is stable enough for the study.

Right heart catheterization

A prominent large V-wave on the pulmonary capillary wedge pressure tracing reflects the regurgitant volume slamming into a non-compliant left atrium — a useful confirmatory finding, and notably there is NO oxygen step-up between the right atrium and right ventricle (distinguishing it from VSD).

Management — Time Is Everything

Definitive treatment is emergent mitral valve surgery (repair when feasible, replacement when not) — medical therapy alone carries extremely high mortality. Temporizing measures while arranging surgery include afterload reduction (nitroprusside, if BP tolerates) to reduce the regurgitant fraction, IABP to reduce afterload and augment coronary perfusion, and inotropic support as needed — but these are bridges, not destinations. Delay to surgery is one of the strongest predictors of mortality in this condition.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery emergently the moment papillary muscle rupture is suspected — do not wait for definitive echo confirmation if clinical suspicion is high and the patient is unstable; involve surgery in parallel with the diagnostic workup, not after it.
ICU admission
Immediately for any suspected or confirmed case — these patients need continuous hemodynamic monitoring and rapid escalation capability.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
Emergent transfer to a cardiac-surgery-capable center is required for essentially all cases — this diagnosis should trigger the same urgency as any other true surgical emergency, with pre-arrival notification to the receiving surgical team.
Transport considerations
IABP placement prior to transport if available and feasible, to stabilize hemodynamics for the transfer itself; continuous hemodynamic monitoring; a critical care transport team given the risk of sudden decompensation en route; avoid excessive afterload (hypertension) during transport if tolerated by BP.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery directly and urgently — this is not a case for routing through general cardiology first if surgical capability is available; parallel notification to interventional cardiology for IABP placement and hemodynamic bridging.

Ventricular Septal Rupture

Same day 3-5 window as papillary muscle rupture, same "new murmur plus shock" presentation — but a distinctly different lesion, mechanism, and catheterization finding (see the comparison diagram in the previous section).

The oxygen step-up, explained

A left-to-right shunt through the septal defect delivers oxygenated LV blood directly into the right ventricle — sampling blood at serial points during right heart catheterization (RA → RV → PA) reveals a measurable jump in oxygen saturation at the level of the shunt, confirming the diagnosis and helping estimate shunt fraction (Qp:Qs). This step-up is absent in papillary muscle rupture, where the abnormal flow (MR) stays entirely on the left side of the heart.

Hemodynamic Consequences

Unlike papillary muscle rupture's pure left-sided volume problem, VSR creates a combined burden: the left-to-right shunt volume-loads the right ventricle and pulmonary circulation while simultaneously reducing effective forward (systemic) cardiac output, since a portion of LV ejection recirculates through the shunt rather than reaching the periphery. This dual burden is why VSR patients often show more pronounced right heart strain and a more complex shock picture than an equivalently-sized regurgitant lesion would produce.

Surgical timing is genuinely difficult

Immediate surgical repair carries the best chance of survival in an unstable patient, but operating on freshly infarcted, friable septal tissue is technically challenging and carries its own mortality risk — patch dehiscence and residual shunt are recognized complications of early repair. In a more stable patient, some surgeons favor a brief delay (days) to allow the tissue to become more fibrotic and surgically manageable, temporizing with MCS in the interim. This tension between "operate now on friable tissue" versus "wait and risk further deterioration" is one of the genuinely difficult judgment calls in cardiac surgery, and is made jointly by the surgical and shock team rather than by a fixed protocol.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery emergently upon suspicion, in parallel with echocardiographic confirmation — identical urgency to papillary muscle rupture.
ICU admission
Immediately for any suspected or confirmed case, with continuous hemodynamic monitoring given the combined shunt/pump-failure physiology.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
Emergent transfer to a cardiac-surgery-capable center with experience in mechanical complication repair — VSR repair in particular benefits from surgeons with specific experience in this less-common operation.
Transport considerations
MCS (IABP or Impella) placement prior to transport if feasible and available, to stabilize the combined shunt/shock physiology; continuous monitoring; critical care transport team given the complexity and instability risk.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery directly and urgently; interventional cardiology in parallel for MCS bridging; consider early discussion with a surgical program specifically experienced in VSR repair if your local surgical team has limited experience with this specific complication.

Free Wall Rupture & Tamponade

The mechanical complication most likely to kill before anyone has a chance to intervene — with a biphasic timing pattern and a survivable subacute form worth actively looking for.

Acute vs. Subacute Presentation

The same underlying lesion (LV free wall rupture) produces two dramatically different clinical pictures depending on whether the rupture is complete and sudden, or small and at least partially contained.
PEA arrest after MI — think free wall rupture

Sudden pulseless electrical activity in a patient days into their MI course, particularly with a persisting organized rhythm on the monitor despite no palpable pulse, should immediately raise suspicion for free wall rupture. Bedside echo (if time allows during resuscitation) looking for pericardial effusion/tamponade physiology can confirm the diagnosis and redirect resuscitation toward emergent pericardiocentesis as a bridge to surgery, rather than continuing standard ACLS algorithms that won't address the underlying mechanical problem.

Risk Factors Worth Knowing

Patient factors

First MI (no prior collateral circulation or ischemic preconditioning), older age, female sex, and hypertension all independently increase risk — the mechanism isn't fully understood but likely relates to less-developed collateral vessels and different infarct healing dynamics in these groups.

Treatment-related factors

Delayed or absent reperfusion increases risk substantially — timely PCI/fibrinolysis doesn't just save myocardium, it reduces the transmural extent of necrosis that predisposes to rupture. Late fibrinolytic administration (beyond the optimal window) has specifically been associated with increased rupture risk in some analyses, adding another argument for timely reperfusion strategy selection (see Vol. 4).

Management of the Subacute Form

Emergent pericardiocentesis serves as a temporizing bridge to stabilize hemodynamics — but definitive management is surgical repair, since the underlying defect in the ventricular wall persists regardless of how effectively the pericardial fluid is drained. Some small, stable, well-contained leaks have been managed with a more conservative watch-and-wait approach in carefully selected patients, but this requires close surgical involvement in the decision and continuous readiness to intervene if the situation changes.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiac surgery emergently for any suspected free wall rupture, acute or subacute — this is one of the most time-critical surgical emergencies in cardiology, full stop.
ICU admission / resuscitation bay
Immediate, with cardiac surgery and cardiology present or immediately available — acute rupture may require emergent bedside pericardiocentesis before any transport is even feasible.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
For the subacute/contained form only, once initially stabilized (pericardiocentesis performed, hemodynamics temporarily improved) — the acute/catastrophic form typically doesn't survive long enough for inter-facility transfer to be a realistic option, underscoring the importance of recognizing and treating it at the presenting facility if at all possible.
Transport considerations
A physician (not just paramedic-level) transport team is strongly preferred given the risk of sudden decompensation; pericardiocentesis equipment and a clinician capable of repeating the procedure should accompany the patient; continuous hemodynamic monitoring throughout.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery immediately and directly — call the surgeon, not just the general cardiology consult line, given how quickly clinical status can change with this diagnosis.

LV Aneurysm & Mural Thrombus

A weeks-later complication with a deceptively similar name to pseudoaneurysm — but a completely different wall composition, risk profile, and urgency.

True Aneurysm vs. Pseudoaneurysm

The critical distinguishing question: does the wall contain any actual myocardium (true aneurysm, low rupture risk) or is it purely pericardium/thrombus (pseudoaneurysm, essentially a stabilized free wall rupture with high rupture risk)?
Don't let the similar names cause a management error

A true aneurysm and a pseudoaneurysm sound like variations on the same finding, but they carry almost opposite management urgency — a true aneurysm is often managed medically for years, while a pseudoaneurysm generally warrants surgical repair once identified given its substantial rupture risk. Getting the neck-width assessment right (narrow neck favors pseudoaneurysm) on imaging is a genuinely consequential distinction, not an academic one.

Persistent ST-Elevation — A Clinical Clue

ST-elevation that persists on ECG weeks after the index MI, in the absence of ongoing chest pain or a new event, is a recognized (though not universal) marker of true LV aneurysm formation — sometimes called a "frozen" or persistent injury pattern. It reflects the abnormal electrical behavior of the thinned, akinetic/dyskinetic aneurysmal segment rather than ongoing ischemia, and shouldn't automatically trigger a repeat ACS workup if the clinical picture and timeline fit aneurysm formation instead.

Mural Thrombus & Anticoagulation

Why it forms

The akinetic or dyskinetic aneurysm segment creates an area of stagnant blood flow along the endocardial surface — Virchow's triad in miniature — providing ideal conditions for thrombus formation independent of any systemic hypercoagulable state.

Anticoagulation duration

There isn't a single universal fixed-duration guideline the way there is for, say, post-cardioversion AFib anticoagulation — duration is individualized based on thrombus characteristics (mobile/protruding thrombus generally warrants longer treatment than a flat, laminated one), aneurysm size and persistence, and the patient's bleeding risk profile. This is a case where cardiology input on the specific imaging characteristics meaningfully changes the anticoagulation plan.

Large anterior MI is the setup

True LV aneurysms form almost exclusively after large, transmural, typically anterior/apical infarcts with extensive myocardial loss — a patient with a large anterior STEMI and delayed or unsuccessful reperfusion is the population to watch for this complication in the weeks following discharge, making follow-up echocardiography a reasonable part of routine post-MI care in this higher-risk group.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology for any newly identified LV aneurysm to establish anticoagulation plan and surveillance imaging schedule; cardiac surgery specifically for any pseudoaneurysm given the generally recommended surgical approach.
Urgent (not necessarily emergent) evaluation
Incidentally discovered pseudoaneurysm — while not typically presenting as an acute crisis the way free wall rupture does, it still warrants prompt surgical evaluation given ongoing rupture risk rather than routine outpatient follow-up timelines.
Transfer if no cardiac surgery on-site
Confirmed pseudoaneurysm requiring surgical repair, or true aneurysm with a complication (refractory arrhythmia, heart failure, or large mobile thrombus) requiring specialized management.
Transport considerations
Generally more flexible timing than the acute mechanical complications above — standard inter-facility transfer protocols are usually adequate for a hemodynamically stable patient with either finding, unless a specific complication (arrhythmia, embolic event) changes the acuity.
Who to call
Cardiac surgery for pseudoaneurysm or complicated true aneurysm; general cardiology for straightforward true aneurysm management and anticoagulation planning.

Post-MI Arrhythmias & Dressler's Syndrome

Rounding out the timeline from Section 1 — the arrhythmic and inflammatory complications that span from the first hour to months after the index event.

Timing-Based Framework

Hours: peak ventricular arrhythmia risk

VF and polymorphic VT risk peaks in the earliest hours after infarction — this is the leading driver of pre-hospital and early in-hospital mortality, and the core rationale for continuous monitoring and immediate defibrillator availability in every ACS patient regardless of apparent stability. See Vol. 3's Ventricular Arrhythmias section for full VT/VF/Torsades management detail.

Hours to days: AV block (especially inferior MI)

RCA territory infarction frequently produces transient AV block via ischemia to the AV nodal artery — this typically improves with reperfusion and rarely requires permanent pacing (see Vol. 3's Bradyarrhythmias section and Vol. 4's RV MI section, since RV infarction and AV block frequently coexist given their shared RCA blood supply).

Days: primary prevention ICD timing — why you wait

LVEF measured in the acute post-MI period frequently improves substantially over the following weeks as stunned (but viable) myocardium recovers function — implanting a primary-prevention ICD based on an acutely depressed EF risks implanting a device in a patient whose EF will recover above the treatment threshold. Guidelines generally recommend waiting at least 40 days post-MI (and re-assessing EF at that point) before primary-prevention ICD implantation for ischemic cardiomyopathy, using a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator as a bridge in appropriately selected high-risk patients during this waiting period.

Weeks to months: Dressler's syndrome

An autoimmune pericarditis (sometimes with pleural and pulmonary involvement) thought to result from an immune response against exposed myocardial/pericardial antigens after infarction. Presents weeks to months after the index MI with pleuritic chest pain, fever, and pericardial/pleural friction rub — distinct from the early post-MI pericarditis that can occur in the first few days from direct pericardial inflammation adjacent to the infarct (a different, more mechanical phenomenon).

Early pericarditis vs. Dressler's — don't conflate them

Pericarditis in the first few days after MI is usually a direct inflammatory reaction to the adjacent necrotic myocardium (sometimes called peri-infarction pericarditis) and is generally self-limited. Dressler's syndrome is a distinct, later, immune-mediated phenomenon — the distinction matters because Dressler's is more prone to recurrence and may warrant a longer treatment course or closer follow-up for relapse.

Dressler's Management

NSAIDs plus colchicine follow the same general approach as pericarditis elsewhere (see Vol. 1b) — colchicine in particular reduces recurrence risk, which is a more prominent concern with Dressler's than with typical viral pericarditis. Corticosteroids are generally reserved for refractory cases, since early steroid use in the broader pericarditis literature is associated with higher recurrence rates, though this must be weighed against the specific clinical picture in a post-surgical or post-MI patient.

Tying the whole volume together

The through-line across this entire volume: recognizing that a "routine" post-MI course can pivot to genuine emergency at multiple distinct time points — arrhythmia in the first hours, mechanical rupture in the day 3-5 window, and inflammatory/immune complications weeks later. Maintaining situational awareness of exactly how many days out from the index event a patient is should actively shape your differential for any new symptom during recovery.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Electrophysiology for any sustained post-MI ventricular arrhythmia (see Vol. 3 for full criteria) and for primary-prevention ICD planning at the appropriate post-MI interval; cardiology for any suspected Dressler's syndrome, particularly if recurrent or associated with a significant effusion.
ICU admission
Any sustained ventricular arrhythmia in the post-MI period, or Dressler's syndrome complicated by a large pericardial effusion with any hint of tamponade physiology.
Transfer if no EP/cardiology on-site
Recurrent post-MI ventricular arrhythmia needing EP evaluation, or a wearable defibrillator candidate who needs specialized fitting/follow-up not available locally.
Transport considerations
Continuous cardiac monitoring for any patient with recent ventricular arrhythmia; standard transport is generally adequate for stable Dressler's syndrome without effusion complications.
Who to call
Electrophysiology for arrhythmia and device timing questions; general cardiology for Dressler's syndrome management and monitoring.

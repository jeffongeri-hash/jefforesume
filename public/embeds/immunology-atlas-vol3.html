01 · Foundations

Why Checkpoint Inhibitors Cause This

The toxicity is the mechanism working correctly, just aimed too broadly — not an off-target side effect.

“This patient started pembrolizumab three months ago and now has new joint pain and diarrhea — is that even related, this late?”

PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 are checkpoint molecules — physiologic “brakes” that prevent T cells from over-responding and attacking the body's own tissue, part of normal peripheral tolerance. Checkpoint inhibitors work by blocking these brakes, which unleashes T cells against tumor antigens — but the same released T cells lose some of their restraint against normal tissue too. An irAE isn't a drug side effect in the traditional pharmacologic sense; it's the intended mechanism of action generalizing beyond the tumor.

Figure 1. Checkpoint blockade releases T-cell activity against the tumor — and, less selectively, against healthy tissue.
Combination therapy (e.g., ipilimumab + nivolumab) substantially raises both the incidence and severity of irAEs compared with either agent alone — anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 act at different points in T-cell regulation, so blocking both removes more than one layer of restraint at once.

The General Management Ladder

The specifics differ by organ, but the shape of the response is the same across nearly every irAE.

Figure 3. Generic CTCAE-anchored grading and escalation ladder — organ-specific detail follows in each section below.
The single most useful cross-organ rule: a corticosteroid-refractory irAE is generally defined as failure to improve by at least one grade within 48–72 hours of adequate steroid dosing — that's the trigger point for escalating to a second-line immunosuppressive agent (infliximab, vedolizumab, mycophenolate, IVIG, etc., chosen by organ) rather than simply continuing to wait.
Verify before use: this section reflects the ASCO irAE management guideline framework (Schneider et al., J Clin Oncol, 2021 update) — confirm current version and institutional oncology protocol before applying specific dosing.
02 · Gastrointestinal

Colitis & Immune-Mediated Diarrhea

The most common severe irAE — and the one where the evidence for which second-line agent to reach for is genuinely still moving.

Grading follows stool frequency above the patient's baseline: Grade 1 <4 stools/day above baseline; Grade 2 4–6 stools/day above baseline; Grade 3 ≥7 stools/day above baseline, incontinence, or hospitalization indicated; Grade 4 life-threatening, perforation, or hemodynamic collapse.

GradeManagement
1Supportive care, continue ICI, close follow-up; rule out infection before attributing to the drug
2Hold ICI, oral or IV corticosteroids (~1 mg/kg prednisone-equivalent)
3–4Hold/discontinue ICI, IV methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg; if no ≥1-grade improvement by 48–72 hours, add infliximab or vedolizumab; imaging (CT abdomen/pelvis) to exclude perforation or typhlitis before escalating workup further
Screen for HIV, hepatitis A/B, and TB before starting infliximab or vedolizumab, and repeat annually if a patient requires ongoing biologic treatment for recurrent irAEs — easy to forget in an acute presentation, but standard of care before immunosuppressive biologic initiation.
Genuinely unsettled, not a clean answer: which second-line agent to reach for first is actively debated. Some 2025 cohort data found higher colitis recurrence with infliximab than vedolizumab (28.7% vs 12.9%); other 2025 data found the opposite — higher recurrence with vedolizumab. A 2024 randomized trial found the two comparably effective at symptom control within two weeks. Combination infliximab + vedolizumab shows higher remission than infliximab alone in refractory cases. Don't treat either agent as the clearly superior default — this is genuinely evolving literature, and institutional/oncology preference reasonably varies.

For colitis refractory to both steroids and a first-line biologic, third-line options with smaller evidence bases include tofacitinib, ustekinumab, and fecal microbiota transplantation (reported ~75% efficacy in a small refractory case series).

02 · Gastrointestinal

Hepatitis

Usually asymptomatic transaminitis caught on routine labs before it's caught on exam.

Reported in roughly 2–10% of patients on monotherapy, rising to 25–30% (all-grade) with combination ipilimumab + nivolumab, with grade 3 hepatotoxicity in roughly 15% of combination-therapy patients. Onset is typically 6–12 weeks after initiation. Grading follows AST/ALT and total bilirubin elevation relative to the upper limit of normal.

GradeManagement
1Continue ICI, monitor LFTs more frequently
2Hold ICI, consider corticosteroids if not improving; rule out viral hepatitis, alcohol, other drugs, and disease progression
3–4Hold/discontinue ICI, IV methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg; if refractory, mycophenolate mofetil is preferred second-line — not infliximab, which carries its own hepatotoxicity risk and is avoided specifically for this irAE
Easy mistake: infliximab is the reflexive second-line agent for several other irAEs, but hepatitis is a specific exception — anti-TNF therapy carries its own risk of drug-induced liver injury, so mycophenolate is favored instead for steroid-refractory immune-mediated hepatitis.
Verify before use: specific transaminase/bilirubin grading cutoffs and second-line dosing should be confirmed against current ASCO/institutional oncology guidance.
03 · Pulmonary

Pneumonitis

More common with combination therapy and in patients with pre-existing lung disease — and a mimicker of the malignancy itself.

Grading is based on symptom severity and functional limitation rather than imaging extent alone: Grade 1 asymptomatic, radiographic changes only; Grade 2 symptomatic, limiting instrumental activities of daily living; Grade 3 severe symptoms, limiting self-care, oxygen indicated; Grade 4 life-threatening, respiratory failure.

GradeManagement
1Continue ICI with close imaging/symptom follow-up
2Hold ICI, corticosteroids (~1 mg/kg); bronchoscopy with BAL ± transbronchial biopsy if the patient can tolerate it, to help exclude infection
3–4Discontinue ICI, IV methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg, pulmonary and infectious disease consultation; if no improvement at 48 hours, add a second agent — infliximab, mycophenolate, IVIG, or cyclophosphamide depending on the case
The differential that has to stay open: new pulmonary infiltrates in a cancer patient on immunotherapy are not automatically pneumonitis — infection and disease progression/lymphangitic spread have to be actively excluded, not assumed away, before committing to high-dose steroids that could worsen an unrecognized infection.
04 · Cardiac

Myocarditis

Rare, and the one irAE where “wait and see” is the wrong instinct — by far the highest mortality of any irAE.

“This patient on nivolumab has new mild dyspnea and a slightly elevated troponin — how worried should I actually be?”

Very. Reported incidence is low (roughly 0.04–1.14%, higher with combination therapy), but mortality is reported at 25–50%, and a 2024 cohort of severe (Grade 3–4) cases found mortality as high as 54.5%. Median time to onset is around 45 days, but presentations within the first few doses are well described, and the clinical spectrum ranges from an isolated asymptomatic troponin elevation to fulminant cardiogenic shock, life-threatening arrhythmia, and complete heart block — the last of which is an independently strong predictor of death.

Figure 4. Suspected ICI myocarditis — workup and management pathway.
The overlap syndrome to actively screen for

Myocarditis, myositis, and a myasthenia gravis-like syndrome can occur together as a recognized overlap — a patient with new proximal weakness, ptosis, or dysphagia on a checkpoint inhibitor should prompt cardiac workup even without cardiac symptoms, and vice versa. This overlap is disproportionately represented in fatal cases.

StepDetail
Hold the drugImmediately, on clinical suspicion — do not wait for confirmatory testing to complete
WorkupTroponin, ECG, BNP/NT-proBNP, echocardiogram; cardiac MRI and/or biopsy as available, but treatment should not be delayed waiting for these
First-line treatmentHigh-dose pulse corticosteroids (methylprednisolone ~1 g/day) — associated with lower MACE than lower-dose regimens in recent case series/systematic review data
Refractory diseaseCase-report-level evidence for anti-thymocyte globulin, plasmapheresis, abatacept, or ruxolitinib — this is specialist (cardiology/oncology) territory, not an ED-driven decision
RechallengeGenerally avoided after myocarditis of any grade given the mortality profile — an oncology/cardio-oncology decision, not a default
Myth: “a normal ejection fraction on echo makes this unlikely to be significant.” ICI myocarditis frequently presents with a preserved ejection fraction even in fatal cases — the injury is often more about conduction system involvement and arrhythmia than pump failure, so a normal EF should not be reassuring on its own.
Verify before use: mortality figures are drawn from recent (2024–2025) retrospective cohorts and vary by cohort and severity mix — treat as an order-of-magnitude estimate; escalation therapy beyond high-dose steroids is guided by specialist consultation given the sparse evidence base.
05 · Endocrine

Endocrinopathies

The irAE category most likely to be permanent — and the one where a normal-looking A1c can be dangerously reassuring.

Most common endocrine irAE
Thyroiditis

Destructive thyroiditis often runs a biphasic course — a transient thyrotoxic phase from release of preformed hormone, followed by hypothyroidism as the gland is depleted. Usually doesn't require holding the ICI; managed symptomatically (beta-blocker in the thyrotoxic phase) and with levothyroxine replacement once hypothyroid.

Check TSH/free T4 · Often permanent hypothyroidism
More common with anti-CTLA-4
Hypophysitis

Pituitary inflammation causing headache and fatigue, with secondary adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and/or hypogonadism from downstream hormone loss. MRI may show pituitary enlargement early. Hormone deficits are frequently permanent even after the acute inflammation resolves.

Check morning cortisol, TSH/free T4, LH/FSH · MRI pituitary
Rarer, more acutely dangerous
Primary adrenal insufficiency

Can precipitate a true adrenal crisis — hypotension and shock refractory to fluids. If clinically suspected in an unstable patient, treat with empiric stress-dose hydrocortisone immediately; do not wait for a cosyntropin stimulation test to result.

Fatigue, hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia
Rare, classically missed
Fulminant Type 1 diabetes

Can present as new DKA with rapid beta-cell destruction — and because onset is fast, the A1c can be near-normal despite the patient being critically ill, since there hasn't been time for chronic hyperglycemia to accumulate. A reassuring A1c does not rule this out.

Check A1c and glucose/ketones/gap — don't anchor on A1c alone
General endocrine irAE rule: unlike most other irAEs, most endocrinopathies do not require holding the checkpoint inhibitor once the patient is on appropriate hormone replacement — the exception is an acute adrenal or thyroid storm-level presentation requiring stabilization first.
Verify before use: hormone replacement dosing and specific ICI-hold decisions should be made with endocrinology/oncology input — this section is oriented toward ED recognition and initial stabilization, not long-term hormone management.
06 · Practical Synthesis

The ED Approach

One question to add to every relevant history, and one number to keep in your head.

Practical synthesis

Ask about checkpoint inhibitor exposure ever, not just current treatment — irAEs can present months after the drug was stopped. Any new or unexplained symptom in that patient deserves irAE on the differential, and more than one organ can be involved at once (screen broadly, especially for the myocarditis-myositis-myasthenia overlap once any one component is found). Hold the checkpoint inhibitor and start corticosteroids for anything Grade 2 or higher pending oncology involvement. For myocarditis specifically, treat clinical suspicion as sufficient to act — don't wait for confirmatory testing to start steroids or to hold the drug. Involve oncology early in every case; irAE management decisions (rechallenge, choice of second-line agent) are collaborative, but initial recognition and stabilization in the ED shouldn't wait for that conversation to happen.

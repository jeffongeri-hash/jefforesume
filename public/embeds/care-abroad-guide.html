Care abroad is a route, not a package.

A screening price buys one stop on a five-stop route. This guide maps the whole route for yourself, a partner and older parents: what to buy where, what has to stay close to home, and what it adds up to.

Builds on a 22-page research brief (included below as a PDF) and adds two more leads: Memorial in Istanbul and Meinian OneHealth in China. Advertised prices, not quotes. Checked 20 Sep 2026.

Not medical advice. This guide is general information for planning and comparison. It does not diagnose or treat any condition, and it does not endorse any provider. It is no substitute for a licensed clinician who knows your history. Prices are advertised, not quotes, and they change. Anyone with a known or suspected cancer, or any urgent symptom, should talk with a treating physician before booking travel. In an emergency, call your local emergency number.

The route

No hospital sells one all-inclusive stay from labs to chemotherapy, because treatment depends on the diagnosis. What exists is a route with five stops, priced differently at each. Pick where you are entering; tap a stop to open it.

See when each step happens

    Under every stop: a home base. Someone who holds your records, an emergency plan, coverage that works outside the U.S., and a way home if something goes wrong. The safety net section below turns this into scenarios.

    What travels well

    This is editorial clinical-logistics judgment, not sourced data. The pattern: planned, protocol-driven, single-episode care travels. Anything urgent, repetitive or dependent on continuity does not.

    Plans well abroad

    • Baseline labs and imaging
    • Endoscopy with same-visit results
    • Second opinions and tumor-board review
    • Single-episode procedures with short, predictable recovery

    Needs a stay or repeat trips

    • Cancer surgery: stay through recovery and final pathology
    • Radiation: usually daily sessions over weeks
    • Chemotherapy and immunotherapy: cycles over months
    • Biopsy with histology you need before deciding

    Keep close to home

    • Emergencies, including after you return
    • Chronic disease titration and refills
    • Pregnancy and delivery
    • Children's acute illness
    • Long-term surveillance

    So "never deal with U.S. healthcare" is a goal you can approach for planned and expensive care, not reach. The realistic version is a small, deliberate U.S. footprint: one clinician who owns your records, plus coverage and an emergency plan.

    Why the chemo blocks pin you down

    For many cytotoxic regimens, blood counts bottom out roughly a week or two after the infusion. That window varies by drug, and it is when a fever becomes an emergency.

    Meinian OneHealth (the reel's "Meinuan"), China Social video lead

    Not in the source brief. It is a screening chain, not a treatment hospital.

    • What it is: China's largest private preventive-checkup operator, Shenzhen-listed. A secondary source counts 600+ centers in 300+ cities and roughly 96.7% of revenue from exams. Coordinator pages give inconsistent counts (500+, 600+, 700+).
    • What it is not: a place for chemotherapy or surgery. It is a cheap baseline layer, with abnormal findings handed to a real hospital.
    • Foreigners: passports are accepted, but booking, payment and messaging are built around Chinese apps and phone numbers. Every foreign-facing source found is a coordinator, mainly China Care (already in the brief as a source).
    • Not established: conventional colonoscopy, biopsy and pathology, whole-body MRI. A bilingual coordinator handles registration and results review; one coordinator page says reports are issued in Chinese with English translation on request, another says bilingual support. Confirm which.

      The safety net, as scenarios

      Ask what has to already exist when each of these happens. Insurance points are general and vary by plan: verify with your insurer or a broker.

      See when to build the safety net

      Fever or bleeding after a biopsy or during chemo abroad

      Who acts
      The treating hospital's 24-hour unit.
      What must exist
      A named responsible clinician, a written after-hours number, a stay window agreed before you leave the hospital's reach, and terms for extra admissions, since those change the total cost.

      A complication after you fly home

      Who acts
      A U.S. emergency department first. EDs must screen and stabilize you regardless of ability to pay (EMTALA), but follow-up is not guaranteed.
      What must exist
      English procedure and pathology reports, DICOM images, a medication list, and a home clinician who has agreed to take the handoff.

      A parent is on Medicare

      What to know
      Original Medicare generally does not pay for care outside the U.S., with narrow exceptions, so planned care abroad is self-pay. Some Medigap plans add limited foreign-travel emergency cover: emergencies only, not planned care.
      What to ask
      Fitness to fly, how long to stay after sedation, biopsy or surgery, and whether long flights after a procedure raise clot risk for them.

      Coverage that follows you

      Options that exist
      International private medical insurance can cover planned treatment abroad, and evacuation memberships cover transport. Both typically underwrite or exclude pre-existing conditions and may have waiting periods.
      What to do
      Get the exclusions in writing before the trip. This guide cannot recommend a policy.

      Something goes wrong and you want recourse

      What to know
      Legal protections and malpractice routes differ abroad and can be harder to pursue. Check the contract's jurisdiction, cancellation and complication terms before paying a deposit.

      Results need a U.S. second look

      What must exist
      Released slides and blocks, DICOM discs or links, and English reports. Expect that a U.S. treating team may still repeat tests. The CDC also cautions that unproven interventions abroad can delay effective treatment.

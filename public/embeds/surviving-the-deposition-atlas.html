Emergency Medicine · The Deposition

Surviving the Deposition

What happens when your chart meets opposing counsel — how the note gets used against you, and how to testify truthfully, consistently, and without handing the plaintiff their case.

Premise: the deposition is where the chart becomes testimony Core skill: answer the question asked — nothing more Companion to: The Bulletproof Note
Domain 01

What a deposition actually is

Sworn testimony taken out of court, transcribed word-for-word, with no judge present to referee. For the plaintiff's attorney it is the single most valuable event in the case — the place they lock in your story and lay the groundwork for cross-examination at trial. For you, it is the moment your chart stops being a medical record and becomes evidence you must defend under oath.

§
The one fact that governs everything else: once you testify to something in deposition, it is very difficult to testify differently at trial. If your trial testimony contradicts your deposition, opposing counsel will read the transcript aloud to impeach you and damage your credibility with the jury. The deposition is not a dress rehearsal — it is the record your trial self is chained to.
AThe purpose — theirs and yours

What the plaintiff's attorney wants

  • Pin down your story so it can't move at trial.
  • Find contradictions — between your testimony and your chart, the nurse's chart, or your co-defendants.
  • Extract admissions — any concession about what you should have done.
  • Assess you as a witness — will you fluster, ramble, or concede under pressure in front of a jury?

What you want

  • Tell the truth — concisely, consistently with your chart.
  • Answer only the question asked — give no more.
  • Avoid speculation and avoid teaching the plaintiff their case.
  • Preserve your credibility intact for trial.
i
No one “wins” the deposition. It is not a public proceeding — no judge, no jury, no audience, just a court reporter and the lawyers. You cannot win your case that day, but you can lose it. The goal is not to be brilliant; it is to be truthful, brief, and consistent.
BWhy physicians struggle in the chair

The instincts that make you a good clinician work against you in a deposition:

  • You explain. Clinicians teach — you want to walk through your reasoning. In deposition, unrequested explanation hands the plaintiff material. You do not need to explain your thinking unless directly asked.
  • You fill silence. Opposing counsel leaves a pause; you rush to fill it and volunteer something harmful. Silence is their tool, not a problem you must solve.
  • You want to appear certain. The deposition feels like a test of competence, so you resist saying “I don't recall” — and instead guess, which is far more dangerous.
  • You take it personally. The case has your name on it and a bad outcome behind it; the emotional weight pushes you to argue or defend when you should simply answer.
✓
The reframe: a deposition rewards the opposite of your clinical instincts. Be the witness who listens, pauses, answers the narrow question truthfully, and stops talking. The less surface area you offer, the less there is to attack at trial.
Domain 02

The cardinal rules of testimony

Distilled from defense-counsel deposition-preparation practice. None of these ask you to be evasive or untruthful — they ask you to be precise, brief, and disciplined. Truth delivered with discipline is the entire craft.

AThe five preferred answers

When truthful, the great majority of good answers are one of five. The discipline is to not volunteer beyond them:

  • “Yes.”
  • “No.”
  • “I don't know.”
  • “I don't recall.”
  • “I don't understand the question.”

A question that can be truthfully answered “yes” or “no” should be answered “yes” or “no” — not “yes, because…” The “because” is where cases are lost.

!
One caution on overuse: don't hide behind these mechanically. If you truthfully do recall something, saying “I don't recall” and then “remembering” it at trial lets opposing counsel impeach you with your own transcript. The answers are shields for genuine limits of knowledge — not tactics.
BThe rules, in order of how often they're broken
Answer only the question asked. Do not volunteer. The most damaging testimony is almost always the sentence that came after the answer. If asked the time, don't explain how the watch works.
Never guess or speculate. Testify only from personal knowledge. Know the difference between an estimate (a bounded, honest approximation you can give) and a guess (a fabrication you cannot). A deposition is not a memory test.
Pause before every answer. The pause lets you (a) make sure you understood the question, (b) let your attorney object if needed, and (c) avoid the reflexive fill. Silence is free; a blurted answer is permanent.
If you don't understand, say so. Never answer a question you didn't fully parse — compound questions, double negatives, and vague pronouns are built to trap you. Make them rephrase.
Don't adopt the questioner's words. If they characterize your care with a loaded term, don't repeat it back. Use your own words. “Would you agree you failed to…” does not require you to accept “failed.”
Don't argue or get angry. The plaintiff's attorney may provoke you to see how you'll look to a jury. Stay level. A composed “I disagree” beats an indignant speech every time.
Correct your own errors immediately. If you misspeak, fix it on the record then and there. A videotaped “perhaps I did perforate that…” will be replayed at trial — catch it in the moment.
It's fine to say you'd need to see the record. You are not required to have the chart memorized. “I'd have to review the record to answer that accurately” is a legitimate, safe answer.
✓
The meta-rule: truthful, brief, and consistent. Every cardinal rule serves those three words. You are not there to win, teach, or impress — you are there to answer the narrow question honestly and stop.
Domain 03

Your chart on the stand

This is the bridge from the Bulletproof Note atlas: everything you charted is now an exhibit, read back to you line by line. The method of attack is always the same — hand you your own words and pin you to them. The defense is a chart that says exactly what you can stand behind, and testimony that stays consistent with it.

AHow the chart gets used
  • The whole record comes in. Plaintiff attorneys routinely enter the entire chart into evidence — every line, every timestamp, the audit trail, the nurse's notes, and yours, side by side.
  • Consistency is the test. Your testimony is measured against what you wrote. A conclusory phrase you can't support (“ruled out dissection”) becomes the question you can't answer.
  • Silence is cross-examined too. What you didn't chart is used against you: “There's nothing in your note about considering this, is there?”
  • The audit trail can contradict your words. If you testify you reviewed the prior ECG but the metadata shows you never opened it, your credibility collapses.
BThe impeachment mechanic — watch it happen

This is the classic sequence defense-prep literature warns about — a physician who answered loosely, then got trapped by the gap between memory and chart:

How it goes wrongQ: Doctor, did you personally check the cast that day after it was split? A: I don't recall. Q: Did you chart anywhere that you did? A: No. Q: So you did not personally check the cast that day? A: That is not true — I don't recall doing it, but I normally would. — The witness has now given two inconsistent answers under oath. At trial, both get read back, and the “I normally would” looks like an after-the-fact save.
The disciplined versionQ: Doctor, did you personally check the cast that day after it was split? A: I don't have a specific recollection of this patient. My routine practice after splitting a cast is to check distal circulation, sensation, and movement, and I would document abnormal findings. — Truthful, consistent, and grounded in habit-and-practice without pretending to a memory you don't have or conceding a fact not in evidence. Note: only invoke “routine practice” if it is genuinely your consistent habit.
CHabit & routine practice — the legitimate bridge

You will not remember one patient among thousands years later — and you're not expected to. When you have no specific recollection, testimony about your consistent routine practice is a recognized, legitimate way to answer, and it is exactly why the maxim “if it wasn't written it didn't happen” is not the ironclad rule plaintiffs pretend. Physicians do things they don't chart; a credible, genuinely consistent routine can establish that a step was taken.

  • It must be a true, consistent habit — not a convenient story. If it isn't actually your invariable practice, don't claim it.
  • It supplements, never contradicts, the chart. Use it to fill a silence, not to override something you affirmatively wrote.
  • The stronger your contemporaneous chart, the less you need it. Good documentation is what keeps you off this bridge in the first place.
i
The connection to your charting: every habit in the Bulletproof Note atlas — naming the differential, charting pertinent negatives, using probabilistic language — exists so that at deposition you're reading from a record that already made your case, instead of reconstructing it from memory under hostile questioning.
Domain 04

The question traps

The recurring structures opposing counsel uses to manufacture a harmful admission. Each has a name, a mechanism, and a disciplined response. Tap to open.

TrapThe compound / "isn't it true" question▸
The question
“Isn't it true that you were busy that night, and you didn't spend enough time with my client, and that's why you missed the diagnosis?”
The mechanism
Three assertions bundled into one so that a single “yes” concedes all three. You can truthfully agree with one part and be trapped into the others.
The response
“That's several questions. I can't answer them as one.” Make them break it apart. Answer each narrow piece truthfully and separately.
TrapThe hypothetical / "would you agree"▸
The question
“Would you agree that a reasonable physician always orders a CT in a patient like this?”
The mechanism
An abstract absolute (“always”) that, once agreed to, becomes the standard of care you're then accused of violating. Real medicine is rarely “always.”
The response
Decline the absolute: “It depends on the specific clinical circumstances,” then, only if asked, the factors. Don't ratify a blanket rule that erases clinical judgment.
TrapThe silence / expectant pause▸
The tactic
You answer. The attorney says nothing, holds eye contact, lets the silence stretch — and you, uncomfortable, keep talking and volunteer something harmful.
The mechanism
Humans reflexively fill silence. Clinicians, trained to educate, fill it with explanation — exactly the surplus material the plaintiff is fishing for.
The response
Answer, then stop. The silence is not your problem to solve. If they want more, they will ask another question. Let it hang.
TrapThe loaded characterization▸
The question
“When you rushed this patient out of the ED without bothering to order the test, were you aware of the risk?”
The mechanism
Prejudicial verbs (“rushed,” “bothering,” “failed,” “ignored”) smuggled into the question so your answer adopts them and the transcript reads as an admission.
The response
Reject the framing, keep your own words: “I discharged the patient after determining [X]. I wouldn't characterize it as rushing.” Never echo the loaded term.
TrapThe "you'd agree the textbook says" (learned treatise)▸
The question
“You'd agree that Tintinalli is an authoritative text, correct? And it says X. So you deviated from it, didn't you?”
The mechanism
Getting you to endorse a text as “authoritative” can open the door to that text being read to the jury as the standard — then any daylight between it and your care becomes a deviation.
The response
Don't reflexively anoint any text as the single authority: “It's a respected reference, but no single text defines the standard of care, and guidelines are applied to the individual patient.” (See Domain 06.)
TrapThe retrospective / "knowing what you know now"▸
The question
“Knowing what you know now, you'd have done things differently, wouldn't you?”
The mechanism
Hindsight framing invites a concession that sounds like an admission of error, when the standard of care is judged on what was reasonable at the time with the information then available.
The response
Anchor to the moment of care: “My decisions have to be judged on the information available at the time. Based on that, my assessment and plan were reasonable.”
!
The unifying thread: every trap tries to get you to say more than the question requires, adopt language that isn't yours, or ratify an absolute. Slow down, answer the narrow truthful core, keep your own words, and refuse the blanket rule.
Domain 05

Memory & the honest “I don't recall”

The most misunderstood tool in the chair. Used honestly, “I don't recall” is a complete, truthful, and safe answer. Used as a dodge, it becomes an impeachment waiting to happen at trial. The distinction is everything.

A“I don't know” vs. “I don't recall”

“I don't know”

You never had the information — it's outside your knowledge. Example: a lab value that was never reported to you, or what a consultant was thinking. This answer says the knowledge was never mine.

“I don't recall”

You may have known it once but don't remember now. This is legitimate and expected when you see dozens of patients a day and the encounter was years ago. It says I can't reliably retrieve this specific memory.

i
Why the difference matters: if you say “I don't recall” and later your memory is genuinely refreshed by a document, you can testify to it — the earlier answer was true when given. But if you say “I don't know” and then produce the fact, you've contradicted yourself. Match the answer to the actual state of your knowledge.
BThe honest use — and the abuse
✓ Honest & safe
“I don't recall this specific patient; I interpret dozens of studies a day and this was several years ago.”
Truthful context for why memory is limited. Credible.
“I'd need to review the record to answer that accurately.”
You're not required to have the chart memorized.
“I don't recall the exact time, but the record would reflect it.”
Defers to the contemporaneous document.
✕ The abuse (impeachment bait)
“I don't recall” — to something you clearly do remember.
If your memory “returns” at trial, the transcript makes you look evasive or dishonest.
Using it for everything, reflexively.
A wall of “I don't recall” reads to a jury as a witness with something to hide.
“I don't recall” then volunteering a favorable detail.
Selective memory — forgetting the bad, recalling the good — destroys credibility.
CThe memory-and-blame effect

Be aware of a documented asymmetry: memory can be distorted when blame is present. After a bad outcome, your recollection of the encounter may shift — often toward what you wish you'd done. This is exactly why contemporaneous documentation outranks later memory, and why speculation is so dangerous: the plaintiff's client remembers every detail of what was, for them, a life-altering event, while you are reconstructing one shift among thousands. Anchor to the record, not to a reconstructed memory.

✓
Bottom line: say “I don't recall” when it's true, say it without embarrassment, and don't let it become either a crutch or a tactic. Truthful memory limits are expected; manufactured ones are fatal.
Domain 06

Standard of care & the learned-treatise trap

The whole case turns on the standard of care — what a reasonably prudent provider would do in the same circumstances. Opposing counsel will try to define it narrowly, using a textbook or a guideline, and then measure you against that definition. Know how to talk about it.

AHow to speak about the standard of care
  • It's what a reasonably prudent provider would do in the same or similar circumstances — not perfection, not the single best retrospective choice, and not a guarantee of outcome.
  • It is applied to the individual patient. Guidelines and decision rules inform judgment; they don't replace it. A rule's low-risk criteria don't forbid testing, and clinical judgment can justify more or less workup.
  • Reasonable providers can disagree. The existence of another reasonable approach doesn't make yours a breach. Say so plainly when pressed.
  • It's judged at the time of care. Return to this whenever hindsight framing appears.
BThe learned-treatise doctrine — why “authoritative” is a trap word

Under the learned-treatise approach, if you concede that a text is “authoritative,” passages from it can be put to you and read to the jury as if they define the standard — and any gap between the text and your care is framed as a deviation. The move is to get you to crown a single source as the authority.

  • Don't anoint a single text. Tintinalli, Rosen's, UpToDate, a specific guideline — each is respected, none is the sole definition of the standard of care.
  • Acknowledge respect without surrendering authority: “It's a well-regarded reference” ≠ “it authoritatively defines what I was required to do.”
  • Return to the individual patient — texts describe populations; you treated a person with specific findings.
Model exchangeQ: You'd agree Tintinalli is authoritative in emergency medicine? A: It's a well-respected reference. But no single text defines the standard of care, and its guidance has to be applied to the individual patient in front of me. Q: But it says a patient like this should get a CT. A: General guidance informs my judgment. My decision was based on this patient's specific findings, which I documented.
CExplaining the bad outcome

Every malpractice case has a poor outcome behind it, so you must be prepared to explain why the patient didn't fare well in a way that is sincere, reasonable, and consistent with your defense and your expert's testimony. A known complication, an atypical presentation, an unavoidable risk, or a disease that was genuinely occult at the time are all legitimate explanations — delivered without defensiveness and without blaming the patient gratuitously.

i
Consistency across the defense: your explanation should align with what your defense expert will testify and with your own chart. A sincere, reasonable account of why a bad outcome occurred — grounded in the record — is one of the most persuasive things a defendant can offer.
Domain 07

The pre-deposition checklist

Tap to check. Work through this with your attorney — not alone. Nothing here substitutes for your defense counsel's specific guidance.

1Preparation
Reviewed the entire record your attorney directed — your notes, nursing notes, consults, imaging, and the audit trail.
Met with defense counsel to prepare; know the issues and the defense theory of the case.
Know your standard of care and how you'll articulate it in plain language.
Prepared a sincere, reasonable explanation for the bad outcome, consistent with your expert.
2In the chair — every question
Pause before answering — understand it, let your attorney object.
Answer only what's asked; do not volunteer; stop when done.
Never guess or speculate — “I don't know” / “I don't recall” when true.
Don't adopt loaded words; use your own language.
Break apart compound questions; make vague ones be rephrased.
Decline absolutes (“always,” “never”) and single-text “authority.”
Anchor to the time of care against hindsight framing.
Correct any misstatement immediately on the record.
Stay composed — don't argue, don't get angry, take breaks as needed.
3Consistency with the chart
My testimony matches what I documented — no contradictions to be impeached with at trial.
Where I lack memory, I rely on the record or genuine routine practice — never invention.
I do not claim to have reviewed anything the audit trail shows I didn't open.
I understand this transcript binds my trial testimony — I'll treat every answer as final.
✓
The whole atlas in one sentence: tell the truth, briefly, from personal knowledge or the record, in your own words, consistent with your chart — pause, answer the narrow question, and stop. The disciplined witness with a bulletproof chart is the one the plaintiff's attorney can't move.

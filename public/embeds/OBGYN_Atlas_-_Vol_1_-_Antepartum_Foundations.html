Trimesters & Fetal Development

Every downstream module in this atlas assumes fluency here. Gestational age (GA) — not chronological time — is the unit obstetrics is built on, dated from the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP), and reconciled against first-trimester ultrasound when the two disagree by more than the ACOG-defined thresholds.

Dating rule

If LMP and first-trimester crown-rump length (CRL) disagree by >5 days (≤8w6d) or >7 days (9w0d–13w6d), ultrasound dating overrides LMP dating (ACOG Committee Opinion 700). A first-trimester ultrasound is the single most accurate dating tool available — more accurate than LMP even when the patient is certain of her dates.

First Trimester (0w0d–13w6d)

This is the window of organogenesis and the period of greatest teratogenic vulnerability — most major structural anomalies trace back to insults between weeks 3 and 8.

~wk 4
Implantation complete; hCG detectable in maternal serum/urine. A normally rising hCG in an early viable IUP increases by ≥53% every 48 hours (Barnhart criterion) — the single most useful number for triaging a first-trimester bleed or pain presentation before a gestational sac is visible.
wk 4–5
Neural tube closes by approximately day 28 post-conception — before most patients know they're pregnant, which is the entire rationale for pre-conception folic acid rather than starting it at the first prenatal visit.
wk 5–6
Cardiac activity typically visible on transvaginal ultrasound once mean sac diameter reaches ~25mm or CRL ~7mm; absence of a heartbeat above these thresholds is a criterion for pregnancy loss, not a reason to panic before them.
wk 6–10
Limb buds, primitive organ systems, and the face form. This is why first-trimester nausea and vomiting (NVP, affecting 70–80% of pregnancies) is worst here — the leading physiologic theory ties it to rising hCG and estrogen coinciding with organogenesis, possibly functioning as an evolved deterrent to ingesting teratogens/pathogens.
wk 11–13w6d
Nuchal translucency + first-trimester serum screen (PAPP-A, free β-hCG) window; also the window for combined preeclampsia risk screening (see Module 2).
Know the difference

NVP is nausea/vomiting without dehydration, ketonuria, or significant weight loss. Hyperemesis gravidarum (0.3–3% of pregnancies) is NVP plus dehydration, electrolyte derangement, ketonuria, and >5% pre-pregnancy weight loss — it needs IV fluids, antiemetics, and thiamine repletion before dextrose, not reassurance. Full escalation criteria in Module 6.

Second Trimester (14w0d–27w6d)

  • Quickening (first maternal perception of fetal movement) — typically 18–20 weeks in nulliparas, often 2 weeks earlier in multiparas who recognize the sensation.
  • Fundal height ≈ GA in cm (McDonald's rule) from ~20–36 weeks in a singleton with a normal BMI; a lag or surge of >3cm from expected warrants growth ultrasound rather than reflexive reassurance.
  • Anatomy scan, 18–22 weeks — the detailed fetal survey (cardiac 4-chamber/outflow tracts, cranium/spine, abdominal wall, limbs, placental location). This is also the practical window for placenta previa follow-up planning, since a low-lying placenta seen here often resolves by term as the lower uterine segment develops.

Placental Angiogenesis & the sFlt-1/PlGF Ratio

Normal placentation is an angiogenic balancing act. PlGF (placental growth factor) and VEGF drive the trophoblast invasion and spiral-artery remodeling that build a low-resistance placental circulation. sFlt-1 (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1) is an antiangiogenic decoy receptor that binds and neutralizes both — in preeclampsia, placental sFlt-1 production surges, PlGF and free VEGF collapse, and the resulting endothelial dysfunction produces the hypertension, proteinuria, and end-organ injury that define the syndrome clinically. This is the biologic explanation for why preeclampsia is fundamentally a placental disease that happens to present as a maternal vascular one.

PROGNOSIS trial (Zeisler et al., NEJM 2016): ratio ≤38 carried a 99.3% negative predictive value for ruling out preeclampsia within 1 week in women with suspected disease. Risk-tier time-to-delivery figures from Hypertension 2021 (Klein et al.) real-world cohort data.

Verify before relying on this

sFlt-1/PlGF ratio testing is well established in Europe and Asia but adoption in U.S. clinical practice has lagged, largely due to platform availability rather than the underlying evidence. Confirm your institution's assay availability and reference cutoffs before counseling on it — this is an adjunct to, not a replacement for, standard blood pressure and proteinuria assessment.

First-Trimester Preeclampsia Risk Screening & Aspirin Prophylaxis

Combined screening (maternal risk factors + mean arterial pressure + uterine artery Doppler pulsatility index + PlGF) at 11–14 weeks identifies women who benefit most from prophylaxis. In the ASPRE trial (Rolnik et al., NEJM 2017), screen-positive high-risk women randomized to low-dose aspirin 150mg nightly from 11–14 weeks through 36 weeks had a 62% reduction in preterm preeclampsia versus placebo.

Practical ACOG approach Tier A

Rather than requiring formal combined screening, ACOG recommends low-dose aspirin (81mg/day in U.S. practice) starting between 12–28 weeks (ideally before 16 weeks) for anyone with one high-risk factor (prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, autoimmune disease) or ≥2 moderate risk factors (nulliparity, obesity, family history, maternal age ≥35, prior adverse pregnancy outcome).

Fetal Growth & Antenatal Surveillance

How growth is measured, how "small" and "large" are defined, and the surveillance tools used once a pregnancy is flagged high-risk.

Growth Assessment

  • Estimated fetal weight (EFW) is calculated from ultrasound biometry (biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference, femur length) using standard formulas (Hadlock is the most widely used).
  • Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is defined as EFW or abdominal circumference <10th percentile for gestational age (ACOG/SMFM). Not every small fetus is pathologic — constitutionally small, growth-appropriate fetuses exist — which is why serial growth trajectory and umbilical artery Doppler matter more than a single percentile.
  • Macrosomia is generally defined as EFW >4,000–4,500g depending on the guideline referenced, with GDM and obesity as the leading modifiable risk factors (Module 7).

Evidence-Based Birth Preparation

What actually changes labor and delivery outcomes — separated from what's culturally assumed to.

Exercise Tier A

ACOG Committee Opinion 804 is unambiguous: physical activity in pregnancy is safe and desirable for the great majority of women, and providers should actively encourage it rather than defaulting to caution. Women who exercised habitually before pregnancy, including at vigorous intensity, can generally continue.

150 min/wk

Observational data cited in ACOG 804 associate regular exercise with decreased gestational diabetes, decreased cesarean and operative vaginal delivery rates, and faster postpartum recovery.

Stop exercising and call the provider if:
  • Vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage
  • Regular, painful contractions
  • Dyspnea before exertion, chest pain, or dizziness/headache
  • Calf pain or swelling (VTE concern)
  • Muscle weakness affecting balance

Pelvic Floor & Perineal Preparation Tier A

Antenatal perineal massage, self- or partner-administered for a few minutes several times a week starting around 35 weeks, has the strongest and most consistent evidence base here.

RR 0.91
Perineal trauma requiring suturing, nulliparas (Cochrane, Beckmann & Stock)
RR 0.50
Overall laceration risk with antenatal pelvic floor exercise (network meta-analysis)

The benefit is clearest in women having their first vaginal birth — the effect is less consistent in multiparas, whose perineal tissue has already been stretched once. Pelvic floor muscle training (Kegel exercises) throughout pregnancy shows the strongest overall signal for laceration prevention across interventions compared in recent Bayesian network meta-analyses, though certainty of evidence for individual outcomes ranges from low to moderate — this is a "recommend broadly, don't oversell precision" evidence base.

Continuous Labor Support & Childbirth Education Tier B

Continuous one-on-one labor support (doula or consistently present support person, as distinct from intermittent nursing checks) is consistently associated with shorter labors, reduced need for pharmacologic pain management, and higher patient-reported satisfaction across the Cochrane evidence base on this topic. Structured prenatal childbirth education classes show more mixed, harder-to-isolate effects on hard outcomes like cesarean rate, but consistently improve patient-reported preparedness and anxiety.

Verify before quoting precise numbers

Effect sizes for continuous labor support vary meaningfully across the Cochrane review's included trials; if you're citing a specific relative-risk figure to a patient or in a talk, pull the current Cochrane review directly rather than relying on older secondary summaries.

Nutrition & Weight Gain

  • Folic acid, 400–800 mcg/day, ideally starting before conception — neural tube closure is complete by ~day 28, so first-visit initiation is too late to prevent NTDs (landmark MRC Vitamin Study). Higher doses (4mg) are recommended for those with a prior NTD-affected pregnancy.
  • Iron per screening; Vitamin D and DHA have plausible but less definitively outcome-proven benefit and are reasonable rather than mandatory.
  • Avoid high-mercury fish (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish); limit unpasteurized dairy and deli meats (listeria); no safe alcohol threshold established.

Imaging Safety in Pregnancy

The single most persistent source of defensive, harmful under-imaging in pregnant patients is an inflated sense of fetal radiation risk. The numbers below are the corrective.

The governing principle — ACOG Committee Opinion 723

With few exceptions, radiologic imaging is at a dose far below the threshold associated with fetal harm — a clinically indicated study should not be withheld from a pregnant patient. Ultrasound and MRI (without gadolinium, when avoidable) remain first-line whenever they can answer the question, but they should never be substituted for CT when CT is genuinely the better or only diagnostic tool.

Radiation Dose Thresholds That Actually Matter

<50 mGy
Cumulative fetal dose with no demonstrated increase in fetal anomalies or loss (ACOG 723)
>100 mGy
Threshold generally associated with organogenesis risk in the 1st trimester — rarely reached by diagnostic imaging
10–20 mGy
Associated with childhood leukemia risk rising from a baseline of ~1/3,000 by a factor of 1.5–2.0×

Virtually no single diagnostic study — even a full abdomen/pelvis CT — approaches the 50 mGy threshold when appropriately protocoled. If a patient requires multiple ionizing-radiation studies in one pregnancy, that's the specific scenario where consulting a radiation physicist to sum cumulative fetal dose is genuinely warranted (ACOG 723) — not a reason to withhold any single indicated study.

Contrast Agents

  • Iodinated (CT) contrast — not shown to cause fetal harm; use when clinically indicated. Theoretical concern for neonatal thyroid suppression has not been borne out as a reason to withhold it.
  • Gadolinium (MRI) contrast — crosses the placenta and recirculates in amniotic fluid with unknown long-term fetal effects; ACOG recommends using it only when the expected diagnostic benefit clearly outweighs the theoretical risk, and use the lowest effective dose.
  • Breastfeeding — should not be interrupted after either iodinated or gadolinium contrast; the amount transferred into breast milk and absorbed by the infant is negligible.

When CT Is the Right Call

Don't reflexively downgrade to MRI or ultrasound when:
  • Trauma with concern for significant intra-abdominal or intracranial injury — MRI is too slow for an unstable patient, and a missed injury is a far larger fetal and maternal risk than the radiation dose.
  • Suspected PE when V/Q is unavailable, non-diagnostic, or the chest X-ray is abnormal — CT-PE protocol is often the lower fetal-dose option compared with V/Q, not the higher-risk one.
  • Suspected appendicitis when ultrasound and MRI are non-diagnostic or unavailable — delayed diagnosis of appendicitis carries a well-documented higher risk of perforation and fetal loss than the imaging itself.
  • Stroke or acute intracranial process — non-contrast CT head remains first-line given negligible fetal dose (fetus is outside the primary beam) and speed.

Pregnancy Emergencies

Signature findings, prevention strategies, and escalation triggers for the nine presentations most likely to define a bad outcome if missed. Tap each to expand.

Preeclampsia / Eclampsia / HELLP ▸
Signature findings

New hypertension (≥140/90) after 20 weeks plus proteinuria, OR new hypertension plus any sign of end-organ dysfunction even without proteinuria. Severe features: BP ≥160/110, platelets <100,000, creatinine >1.1 mg/dL or doubled from baseline, LFTs ≥2× normal, pulmonary edema, new-onset headache unresponsive to acetaminophen, or visual disturbances. HELLP = Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets — a preeclampsia variant that can occur with normal or only mildly elevated blood pressure.

Prevention

Low-dose aspirin 81mg/day from 12–16 weeks in at-risk patients (62% relative reduction in preterm preeclampsia, ASPRE trial). Calcium supplementation in low-dietary-calcium populations has WHO-level support internationally.

Escalate when

Any severe feature is present, or symptoms (headache, visual changes, RUQ pain) accompany a new hypertensive reading — magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis and delivery planning become the immediate priorities, not further outpatient monitoring.

Placental Abruption ▸
Signature findings

Painful vaginal bleeding with a firm, tender, hypertonic uterus and high-frequency, low-amplitude contractions on tocometry. Roughly 1 in 5 abruptions is concealed — no external bleeding at all, with blood trapped behind the placenta — so absence of visible bleeding never excludes it in a patient with a tender, contracting uterus and fetal distress.

Prevention

Blood pressure control (chronic hypertension is a major risk factor), trauma precautions/seatbelt counseling, smoking and cocaine cessation counseling — all major modifiable risk factors.

Escalate when

Any painful third-trimester bleeding with uterine tenderness — this goes to labor and delivery for continuous monitoring, not to a routine outpatient ultrasound slot.

Placenta Previa ▸
Signature findings

Classically painless, bright red vaginal bleeding, often in the third trimester. Diagnosed by transvaginal ultrasound, which — despite intuition — is safe and more accurate than transabdominal imaging for placental edge location.

Prevention

Not preventable in the primary sense; risk rises with prior cesarean, prior previa, and multiparity, which informs counseling around elective cesarean indications in future pregnancies.

Escalate when

Never perform a digital cervical exam with a known or suspected previa — it can precipitate catastrophic hemorrhage.

Preterm Labor & PPROM ▸
Signature findings

Regular contractions with cervical change before 37 weeks (preterm labor), or rupture of membranes before labor onset and before 37 weeks (PPROM). Fetal fibronectin and cervical length ultrasound help risk-stratify equivocal presentations.

Prevention

Vaginal progesterone for a short cervix, cerclage for select cervical-insufficiency histories, smoking cessation, and treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria (Module 7) all reduce preterm birth risk.

Escalate when

Antenatal corticosteroids for anticipated delivery 24w0d–33w6d (and selectively 34w0d–36w6d); latency antibiotics for PPROM to prolong pregnancy and reduce chorioamnionitis/neonatal sepsis; magnesium sulfate for fetal neuroprotection when delivery is anticipated before 32 weeks.

Ectopic Pregnancy ▸
Signature findings

First-trimester pain and/or bleeding with an hCG that fails to rise appropriately (<53% in 48 hours) or that has plateaued, especially once hCG exceeds the discriminatory zone (commonly ~3,500 mIU/mL, though this varies by institution and equipment) without a visualized intrauterine gestational sac. This remains a leading cause of first-trimester maternal mortality when missed.

Prevention

Not preventable per se; risk factors (prior ectopic, tubal surgery, PID, IUD in place, smoking) inform which patients need earlier, closer hCG/ultrasound follow-up.

Escalate when

Hemodynamic instability, peritoneal signs, or free fluid on ultrasound in a patient with a positive pregnancy test and no confirmed intrauterine pregnancy — this is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise.

Hyperemesis Gravidarum ▸
Signature findings

Persistent vomiting with ketonuria, >5% pre-pregnancy weight loss, and dehydration/electrolyte derangement — distinguishing it from the far more common physiologic NVP of Module 1.

Prevention

Early, proactive antiemetic use in patients with a prior HG history or early severe NVP, rather than waiting for dehydration to develop.

Escalate when

IV fluids and antiemetics are needed; give thiamine before dextrose-containing fluids in any prolonged-vomiting patient to avoid precipitating Wernicke encephalopathy.

Venous Thromboembolism / Pulmonary Embolism ▸
Signature findings

Pregnancy is a 4–5× baseline hypercoagulable state, with risk highest in the postpartum period. Leg swelling, calf pain, dyspnea, or pleuritic chest pain warrant a genuine workup rather than attribution to "normal pregnancy discomfort."

Prevention

Risk-based pharmacologic prophylaxis in patients with prior VTE or strong thrombophilia history; mechanical prophylaxis and early ambulation postpartum, especially after cesarean.

Escalate when

D-dimer is not a useful rule-out test in pregnancy — it's physiologically elevated at baseline and climbs further as pregnancy progresses. Go straight to imaging (Module 5) when clinical suspicion is real; don't let radiation-dose anxiety delay a CT-PE protocol or lower-extremity Doppler.

Trauma in Pregnancy ▸
Signature findings

Trauma is the leading nonobstetric cause of maternal death. Even seemingly minor trauma (a low-speed MVC, a fall) can cause occult placental abruption without any external sign.

Prevention

Seatbelt counseling (lap belt under the abdomen, across the hips; shoulder belt between the breasts, not under the arm) at every prenatal visit — a frequently skipped but genuinely protective piece of counseling.

Escalate when

ACOG supports a minimum of 4 hours of continuous fetal monitoring after any trauma ≥20 weeks, extended to 24 hours if contractions, abdominal pain, bleeding, ruptured membranes, or a non-reassuring tracing are present. Obtain a Kleihauer-Betke test in Rh-negative patients to guide Rhogam dosing beyond the standard dose if significant fetomaternal hemorrhage is suspected.

Sepsis in Pregnancy ▸
Signature findings

Pregnancy's physiologic tachycardia and mild leukocytosis (Module 1) mask early sepsis on standard adult criteria — a pregnant patient can be genuinely septic while looking "not that sick" by non-pregnancy-adjusted vital sign thresholds. Maternal early warning criteria (obstetric-specific modified qSOFA/MEWS tools) exist precisely because standard sepsis scoring under-triggers in this population.

Prevention

Prompt treatment of the common antecedents — chorioamnionitis, pyelonephritis (Module 7), and postpartum endometritis — before they progress.

Escalate when

Any fever with tachycardia or tachypnea in pregnancy deserves a lower threshold for source workup and empiric antibiotics than the same numbers would in a non-pregnant patient.

GDM, Obesity & UTI — Screening & Prevention Basics

These three conditions get a full clinical deep dive — pathophysiology, management algorithms, and outcome studies — in Volume 3 (Complications). Here, only what belongs at the antepartum-screening level.

Gestational Diabetes

ACOG's standard is a two-step approach at 24–28 weeks: a 50g, 1-hour glucose challenge test (no fasting required) followed by a 100g, 3-hour oral glucose tolerance test if the screen is positive, with 2 or more abnormal values required for diagnosis.

ApproachTestDiagnostic threshold
ACOG two-step50g GCT screen → 100g 3h OGTT if positiveScreen cutoff institution-dependent (commonly 130–140 mg/dL); ≥2 abnormal OGTT values (Carpenter-Coustan criteria)
IADPSG one-step75g, 2h OGTT, no prior screenA single abnormal value is diagnostic
Why this matters practically

The one-step approach increases diagnosed GDM prevalence by roughly 1.5–3× compared with the two-step approach — the same patient can be "diabetic" under one protocol and "normal" under the other. Know which protocol your institution uses before counseling a patient on her result.

Prevention: regular exercise reduces GDM incidence (Module 4); weight optimization pre-conception when feasible.

Obesity in Pregnancy

Pre-pregnancy BMI ≥30 is an independent risk factor for GDM, preeclampsia, cesarean delivery, macrosomia, and — less intuitively — a modestly increased risk of neural tube defects (thought to relate partly to reduced folate bioavailability and partly to detection: ultrasound anatomic survey sensitivity decreases with increasing maternal body habitus, an imaging limitation worth disclosing to patients, not just a screening-result caveat).

Practical imaging note tying back to Module 5: fetal radiation dose from a given CT protocol increases with patient thickness — obesity is one of the few variables that meaningfully raises the actual numbers in the Module 5 dose tables.

UTI & Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

This is the one screening test in obstetrics that's genuinely counter-intuitive to non-OB providers: pregnant patients get a urine culture at the first visit, not just a dipstick or symptom-triggered UA — because untreated asymptomatic bacteriuria progresses to pyelonephritis in roughly 20–40% of pregnant patients, versus only 1–2% in non-pregnant women with the same finding.

Why pregnancy is different

Progesterone-mediated ureteral smooth muscle relaxation plus mechanical compression of the ureters by the enlarging uterus (right > left, from dextrorotation of the uterus) causes physiologic hydronephrosis and urinary stasis — a set-up for ascending infection that doesn't exist outside pregnancy. This is why asymptomatic bacteriuria is treated in pregnancy when it would typically be left alone otherwise.

Your Own Wellbeing Matters, Too

Perinatal mood and anxiety disorders aren't exclusive to birthing parents — partners experience elevated rates of depression and anxiety across pregnancy and the postpartum period as well, often going unscreened because clinical attention is (appropriately) focused on the pregnant patient. If you notice persistent low mood, irritability, or anxiety in yourself, that's worth naming to your own primary care provider, not something to push through silently for the sake of "being the strong one."

Prenatal Bonding: What's Real vs. What's Folklore

What the evidence actually supports Tier A

The fetal auditory system is functional by roughly 25–26 weeks. Classic work by DeCasper & Fifer demonstrated that newborns show a measurable preference for their mother's voice over an unfamiliar female voice within hours of birth — evidence of genuine in-utero auditory learning. Talking to the baby, reading aloud, or simply the mother's own voice during daily life all plausibly contribute to this recognition; there's no evidence a father's or partner's voice needs to be louder or more frequent to matter — ordinary presence and conversation are enough.

Myth-bust: the "Mozart effect"

The original 1993 Rauscher study that spawned this idea tested college students' spatial-reasoning task performance immediately after listening to Mozart — it said nothing about fetuses, infants, or IQ, and even that narrow finding was small in magnitude, temporary (minutes), and has largely failed rigorous replication. There is no credible evidence that playing classical music to a pregnant belly raises a baby's intelligence. If you enjoy playing music for the baby, do it — it's a pleasant bonding ritual and almost certainly harmless — but frame it honestly to patients as a nice thing to do, not a proven cognitive intervention.

What the evidence does tie to better neurodevelopmental outcomes is less romantic but more actionable: adequate nutrition, avoidance of known teratogens and heavy alcohol/substance exposure, and — the piece most relevant to epigenetics — reducing chronic maternal stress, since sustained high maternal cortisol is one of the more consistently studied prenatal programming pathways (explored in depth in Volume 4). A calm, supported pregnancy is a genuinely evidence-based gift a partner can give; a Spotify playlist aimed at the belly is a nice-to-have, not a substitute.

Without Insurance

Uninsured patients face the full, un-negotiated cost of care — commonly cited around $18,000–$19,000+ for an uncomplicated delivery, and substantially more for a cesarean or complicated course, since they're billed at or near hospital chargemaster rates rather than the discounted rates insurers negotiate. Most hospitals offer financial-assistance or self-pay discount programs that aren't advertised — worth flagging to any uninsured patient, since the difference between the chargemaster rate and a negotiated self-pay rate can be substantial.

Practical options worth knowing
  • Medicaid covers a large share of U.S. births (commonly cited around four in ten nationally, higher in many states) — pregnancy itself is a qualifying event in most states even for those who don't otherwise qualify.
  • Under the ACA, maternity and newborn care is an essential health benefit — marketplace plans cannot deny coverage or charge more for a pre-existing pregnancy.
  • 2025 individual out-of-pocket maximums on marketplace plans are capped (~$9,200 in-network) — a hard ceiling on exposure even for a complicated, high-cost delivery.
  • Community health centers and midwifery/birth-center care are typically substantially lower-cost for genuinely low-risk pregnancies.
  • The federal No Surprises Act (since 2022) prohibits most surprise out-of-network billing when a patient is admitted to an in-network hospital.

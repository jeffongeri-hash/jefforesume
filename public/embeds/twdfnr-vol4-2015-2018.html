Series Origin

Before the journal series: the founding talk

Leading up to the 2012 Society of Hospital Medicine (SHM) annual meeting, Leonard S. Feldman, MD, was asked to give a talk — he called it "Things We Do For No Reason." He gave a version of that talk annually for over a decade before the concept became a recurring Journal of Hospital Medicine feature in October 2015 (Feldman, J Hosp Med. 2015;10(10):696, doi: 10.1002/jhm.2425). His original talk highlighted four practices — the conceptual seeds of the entire series. Source: retrospective interview with Dr. Feldman, "JHM's Things We Do For No Reason is a Hit," The Hospitalist, Oct 2025.

Founding Topic 1

Transfusing at a hemoglobin threshold above 7 g/dL in stable, non-actively-bleeding patients.

Largely resolved
Founding Topic 2

The daily chest X-ray as a routine, non-indication-driven ICU/ward habit.

Still persists
Founding Topic 3

Fractional excretion of sodium (FENa) used to distinguish prerenal azotemia from acute tubular necrosis.

Still persists
Founding Topic 4

NG lavage to determine whether a patient has an upper GI bleed.

Resolved — practice abandoned

Per Dr. Feldman and series co-editor Tony Breu, MD, in the same retrospective: NG lavage has genuinely gone away in practice; the daily chest X-ray and FENa continue to be ordered by habit; restrictive transfusion thresholds are "a mixed bag" — widely accepted in guidelines but inconsistently followed at the bedside. This mirrors the series' broader point: publishing the evidence rarely changes the culture on its own.

Confirmed Formal Entries

01 · Nursing / Vascular Access

Routine Replacement of Peripheral Intravenous Catheters

Patel SA, Alebich MM, Feldman LS. Things We Do for No Reason: Routine replacement of peripheral intravenous catheters. J Hosp Med. 2017;12(1):42–45. doi: 10.1002/jhm.2676

Original rationale

Standard nursing protocol replaced peripheral IV catheters (PIVCs) every 72–96 hours on a fixed schedule, regardless of the site's clinical appearance, to prevent phlebitis and catheter-related bloodstream infection.

Why it persists

Hardwired into nursing policy and hospital accreditation checklists for decades; routine replacement feels like a straightforward, low-risk precaution.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Randomized trials comparing routine (scheduled) PIVC replacement against clinically-indicated replacement (only when the site shows signs of a problem) found no significant difference in rates of phlebitis or catheter-related bloodstream infection between the two strategies.
  • Routine replacement adds unnecessary needle sticks, patient discomfort, and nursing time without a demonstrated infection-prevention benefit over as-needed replacement.
  • This finding directly foreshadows the series' 2026 entry on idle PIVCs (Volume 1, #1) — the two entries pair naturally: replace/remove based on clinical status and ongoing need, not a fixed calendar.

When scheduled replacement still makes sense

Sites with any sign of phlebitis, infiltration, or malfunction should be replaced immediately regardless of dwell time — the point is clinically-indicated replacement, not "never replace."

Bottom line

Replace PIVCs based on clinical indication (signs of phlebitis, infiltration, or malfunction), not automatically on a fixed 72–96 hour schedule.

02 · Pediatrics / Fluids

The Default Use of Hypotonic Maintenance Intravenous Fluids in Pediatrics

Hall AM, Ayus JC, Moritz ML. Things We Do For No Reason: The default use of hypotonic maintenance intravenous fluids in pediatrics. J Hosp Med. 2018;13:637–640. doi: 10.12788/jhm.3040

Original rationale

Hypotonic IV fluids were the traditional default maintenance fluid for hospitalized children, based on mid-20th-century (1957, Holliday & Segar) calculations of pediatric free-water requirements.

Why it persists

Decades of institutional default order sets and teaching; the original Holliday-Segar formula is still taught essentially unchanged in many training programs.

What the evidence actually shows

  • Hospitalized children have multiple nonosmotic stimuli for ADH release (pain, nausea, stress, illness itself) that impair free-water excretion — meaning hypotonic fluids significantly increase the risk of iatrogenic hyponatremia in this population.
  • The PIMS randomized controlled trial (McNab et al., Lancet 2015) directly compared 140 mmol/L sodium (isotonic) against 77 mmol/L sodium (hypotonic) maintenance fluid in hospitalized children and found significantly less hyponatremia in the isotonic arm.
  • A Cochrane review (McNab et al., 2014) reached the same conclusion across the pooled pediatric trial literature: isotonic fluids reduce the risk of hyponatremia compared with hypotonic fluids, without increasing the risk of hypernatremia.

Bottom line

Use isotonic (not hypotonic) maintenance IV fluids as the default for most hospitalized children — the older Holliday-Segar-based hypotonic default is no longer supported by randomized evidence.

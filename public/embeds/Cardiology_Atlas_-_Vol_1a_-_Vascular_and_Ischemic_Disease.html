01 / Vascular & Ischemic Disease

Hypertension

The most prevalent, modifiable driver of cardiovascular and renal disease worldwide — mechanism, staging, and the 2025 ACC/AHA reframing of who gets treated and how aggressively.

Burden

Hypertension affects roughly 46–50% of US adults, yet only about 23% of those with hypertension are controlled to goal. Left unaddressed, projections estimate 162 million US adults will have hypertension by 2060. verify before citing exactly

Pathophysiology: RAAS & Sympathetic Drive

Chronic hypertension is rarely a single-pathway problem — it's the net result of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) activation, sympathetic overactivity, and volume handling converging on the same endpoint: increased peripheral resistance and/or intravascular volume.

RAAS cascade from renal hypoperfusion through angiotensin II's four downstream effects, with ANP/BNP shown as the physiologic counter-regulatory brake.
Pearl

Angiotensin II does double duty: it's both an acute vasoconstrictor and a chronic growth factor. That's why ACEi/ARBs don't just lower pressure acutely — they also blunt the vascular and myocardial remodeling that drives long-term target-organ damage, which is part of why they're preferred in diabetic nephropathy and HFrEF independent of their BP-lowering effect.

Primary vs. Secondary Hypertension

~90–95% of hypertension is primary (essential) — polygenic, related to age, salt sensitivity, obesity, and sympathetic/RAAS tone. Suspect secondary causes with: onset before age 30 or after 55, resistant hypertension (uncontrolled on 3+ agents including a diuretic), abrupt worsening of previously controlled BP, or specific clinical clues below.

What's new in 2025

The Pooled Cohort Equations are out; the PREVENT equations are in for estimating 10-year CVD risk, and the treatment-initiation threshold moved from ≥10% (PCE) to ≥7.5% (PREVENT) — reflecting evidence that PCE meaningfully overestimated risk in contemporary cohorts. Practically: Stage 1 hypertension (130–139/80–89) now gets pharmacologic therapy if PREVENT 10-year risk is ≥7.5%, or if the patient already has clinical CVD, diabetes, or CKD — otherwise a 3–6 month lifestyle trial comes first. Stage 2 hypertension gets medication regardless of calculated risk. The SBP goal remains <130 mmHg, with new encouragement (based on additional trial evidence since SPRINT) to push toward <120 mmHg where tolerated. confirm current version before teaching

Out-of-office confirmation

The 2025 guideline leans further into home BP monitoring (HBPM) over ambulatory monitoring (ABPM) for diagnosis and titration — HBPM correlates with outcomes, is more reproducible, and is far easier to implement in practice. Don't anchor to a single elevated office reading.

Progression & End-Organ Damage Algorithm

Two very different clinical problems both sit under "severe hypertension" — the chronic, cumulative organ damage of sustained stage 2 disease, and the acute decompensation of a hypertensive crisis. They are staged and managed completely differently.

Left: the four-tier staging framework. Right: acute severe elevation (>180/120) branches into urgency vs. emergency based on acute target-organ damage. Bottom: the chronic remodeling pathway across five organ systems.
Urgency vs. emergency — don't blur this line

Hypertensive urgency: severe BP elevation, no acute end-organ damage. Oral agents, BP lowered gradually over 24–48 hours, often manageable as an outpatient. Rapid ED-based IV correction is not indicated and can itself cause harm (watershed ischemia).

Hypertensive emergency: severe BP elevation with acute damage — encephalopathy, ICH, acute pulmonary edema, ACS, aortic dissection, AKI, or eclampsia. IV titratable agents, ICU-level monitoring. General target is ~10–20% MAP reduction in the first hour (not to normal) — the major exceptions are aortic dissection (aggressive, rapid HR <60 and SBP 100–120 within ~20 minutes) and acute ischemic stroke (permissive, more conservative thresholds to protect penumbral perfusion).

First-Line Pharmacotherapy

Thiazide-type diuretics

Chlorthalidone favored over HCTZ in most outcome comparisons given longer half-life and better 24-hour coverage.

Long-acting dihydropyridine CCB

Amlodipine — well tolerated, effective across most demographics including Black patients and older adults.

ACE inhibitor / ARB

Preferred with diabetes, CKD with proteinuria, or HFrEF — added RAAS-remodeling benefit beyond BP control.

Combination first

Stage 2 disease often warrants starting two first-line agents simultaneously rather than sequential titration, given the larger gap to goal.

02 / Vascular & Ischemic Disease

Hyperlipidemia

Lipoprotein trafficking, where it goes wrong, and the newly restored LDL-C treatment goals from the 2026 ACC/AHA/Multisociety Dyslipidemia Guideline.

Lipoprotein Pathways

Two parallel systems handle dietary vs. hepatically-synthesized lipid, converging on LDL as the final atherogenic particle — with HDL running a separate, protective reverse-transport circuit.

Exogenous (dietary/chylomicron) and endogenous (hepatic/VLDL) pathways both terminate in LDL particle formation; statins act by upregulating hepatic LDL receptors. HDL reverse cholesterol transport runs in parallel as the protective counterweight.
Pearl

LDL-C is a calculated (Friedewald) or directly measured surrogate for the number of atherogenic particles, not a direct count. In patients with high triglycerides, diabetes, or low achieved LDL-C, ApoB (one molecule per atherogenic particle) is a more accurate risk marker than LDL-C alone — this is why the 2026 guideline elevates ApoB testing once LDL-C/non-HDL-C goals are met.

2026 LDL-C Treatment Goals

The 2018 cholesterol guideline moved away from fixed LDL-C targets toward percent-reduction-only thinking. The 2026 update reverses that — absolute LDL-C goals are back, layered on top of percent-reduction guidance, using an "earlier and lower for longer" framework.

Secondary & Genetic Causes Worth Screening For

Familial hypercholesterolemia clues

LDL-C ≥190 mg/dL, tendon xanthomas, corneal arcus before age 45, or a family history of premature ASCVD (men <55, women <65) — think heterozygous FH and consider cascade family screening.

Secondary dyslipidemia

Hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, poorly controlled diabetes, chronic alcohol use, and certain medications (protease inhibitors, retinoids, older beta-blockers, high-dose thiazides) can all drive acquired dyslipidemia — worth a basic metabolic/TSH check before assuming primary hyperlipidemia.

Safety: statin-associated muscle symptoms

Clinically meaningful myopathy is uncommon and rhabdomyolysis is rare with appropriately dosed statin monotherapy; most reported muscle symptoms in trials occur at similar rates to placebo (nocebo effect is substantial). True risk rises with drug-drug interactions (strong CYP3A4 inhibitors with simvastatin/lovastatin), hypothyroidism, and advanced age/frailty. Don't abandon statin therapy on symptoms alone without considering a rechallenge or alternate agent — undertreated ASCVD risk is the bigger safety problem in most of these conversations. verify current incidence figures before quoting to patients

03 / Vascular & Ischemic Disease

CAD → ACS Spectrum

From endothelial injury to plaque rupture to the reperfusion decision tree — reframed around the 2025 unified ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI Acute Coronary Syndromes Guideline.

Atherogenesis

The same five-step process underlies both the fixed, flow-limiting lesion of stable angina and the disrupted, thrombogenic lesion of ACS. What diverges is plaque composition, not just size.

Endothelial injury → lipid infiltration → foam cell formation → fatty streak → fibrous plaque. Downstream, a stable fibrous plaque produces demand-driven stable angina; a thin-cap fibroatheroma is primed to rupture or erode into an acute thrombus.
Pearl

Angiographic stenosis severity correlates poorly with rupture risk. Many MIs occur at plaques that were only mildly-to-moderately stenotic on prior imaging — it's the thin cap and lipid-rich core, not the luminal narrowing, that predicts an acute event. This is why aggressive lipid-lowering (cap stabilization) matters even for "insignificant" lesions.

Progression Algorithm & the 2025 Guideline

The 2025 guideline is the first to unify what used to be separate STEMI (2013) and NSTE-ACS (2014) documents into one framework — reflecting that the underlying decision points (reperfusion urgency, revascularization completeness, antithrombotic strategy) are more alike than different across the spectrum.

ECG bifurcates STEMI (immediate reperfusion) from NSTE-ACS (troponin-guided risk stratification into four invasive-timing tiers). Both pathways converge on revascularization and a shared secondary-prevention bundle — except cardiogenic shock, which gets culprit-only PCI first.
Complete revascularization

Now favored over culprit-only for most STEMI and NSTEMI patients with multivessel disease — nonculprit lesions can be treated at the index procedure or staged later, based on recent trial evidence. The exception is ACS complicated by cardiogenic shock, where culprit-only PCI remains preferred.

Intracoronary imaging

Elevated from a 2021 class 2a to a class 1 recommendation for guiding PCI in complex lesions — reflecting how routine IVUS/OCT use has become.

DAPT duration

Aspirin plus an oral P2Y12 inhibitor for at least 12 months remains the default for patients not at high bleeding risk; individualize shorter courses for those with elevated bleeding risk.

Transfusion threshold

In ACS patients with acute or chronic anemia who are not actively bleeding, it may be reasonable to transfuse to maintain hemoglobin around 10 g/dL — a more liberal threshold than general ICU anemia management.

Presentation pearls

Women, older adults, and patients with diabetes disproportionately present with atypical or absent chest pain — dyspnea, fatigue, epigastric discomfort, or "silent" ischemia detected only on ECG/troponin. Maintain a low threshold for ECG and troponin in these groups even with vague symptoms. Cocaine-associated chest pain is its own trap: avoid unopposed beta-blockade (unopposed alpha-mediated vasospasm risk) — benzodiazepines plus nitrates plus alpha-blockade or calcium channel blockade are preferred first-line.

04 / Vascular & Ischemic Disease

PAD & Aortic Aneurysm/Dissection

The same atherosclerotic biology that produces CAD, applied to the limbs and the aortic wall — where the failure modes are ischemia and mechanical rupture rather than acute thrombosis.

Peripheral Artery Disease

PAD is atherosclerosis by another name, staged by symptom severity and confirmed with the ankle-brachial index (ABI) — the resting ratio of ankle to brachial systolic pressure.

Left: symptomatic progression from asymptomatic stenosis through claudication, rest pain, and chronic limb-threatening ischemia — with acute limb ischemia as a separate, time-critical branch. Right: ABI interpretation bands.
Acute limb ischemia — the 6 P's

Pain, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, Poikilothermia, Paralysis. Paresthesia and paralysis are the late, ominous findings signaling nerve and muscle ischemia — this is a surgical emergency with a limb-viability clock measured in hours, not days. Don't wait for imaging to call vascular surgery if the exam is convincing.

Medical management

High-intensity statin and antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) for all symptomatic PAD, regardless of revascularization plans — this is secondary ASCVD prevention, not just a limb issue. Cilostazol can improve walking distance in claudication (contraindicated in heart failure). Supervised exercise therapy has outcomes data comparable to revascularization for claudication alone.

ABI caveat

Diabetic and ESRD patients frequently have medial arterial calcification producing falsely elevated, noncompressible ABIs (>1.40) — a normal-appearing ABI in a symptomatic diabetic patient should prompt toe-brachial index or other imaging rather than reassurance.

Aortic Aneurysm & Dissection

Two distinct mechanical failure modes of the aortic wall: aneurysm is a chronic dilation problem governed by Laplace's law; dissection is an acute intimal tear creating a false lumen — and the two frequently intersect, since a dissected or aneurysmal aorta is more prone to further complications.

Left: rupture risk rises steeply with diameter, driving the ~5.5 cm elective repair threshold. Right: Stanford/DeBakey classification and the intimal-tear-to-malperfusion mechanism.
AAA screening (USPSTF)

One-time ultrasound screening: men aged 65–75 who have ever smoked (grade B, recommended). Selective screening based on shared decision-making for men 65–75 who never smoked (grade C). Screening women who never smoked and have no family history is not recommended (grade D) — the harms of screening in this group outweigh the benefits. Evidence is insufficient for women 65–75 who have ever smoked or have a family history (grade I). confirm no interim USPSTF update

Documentation trap, revisited

As in the trauma literature: a normal or "unremarkable" CXR does not exclude dissection. A widened mediastinum is neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule in or out on its own. CT angiography (or bedside TEE in the unstable patient) is the standard — document the objective findings and imaging plan rather than a conclusory "dissection ruled out by CXR."

Rupture mortality

Ruptured AAA carries very high overall mortality — commonly cited figures put death before hospital arrival or during emergency repair at roughly 80% of ruptures, versus well under 5% mortality for elective repair of an unruptured aneurysm. This gap is the entire argument for screening and elective repair at threshold size. verify exact figures against current source before quoting

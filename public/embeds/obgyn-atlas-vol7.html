Contraceptive Method Comparison

Typical-use failure rates — not perfect-use — are what should drive most counseling conversations, since perfect use is the exception in real life, not the rule.

The counseling point that matters more than any single number

LARC methods (implant, both IUD types) have no meaningful gap between perfect-use and typical-use failure rates — once placed, there's nothing left for the patient to remember to do. Every other method's real-world effectiveness depends heavily on adherence, which is exactly why LARC methods outperform user-dependent methods so substantially in actual population data despite pills/injections having reasonable perfect-use efficacy on paper.

The takeaway for migraine specifically

Migraine with aura is a category 4 at any age — this is one of the few absolute contraindications that doesn't scale with additional risk factors the way smoking does. Progestin-only methods (POP, DMPA, implant, hormonal IUD) remain category 1–2 for migraine with aura, making them the go-to alternative rather than trying to risk-stratify around the estrogen component.

2024 Update Highlights

  • New conditions added: chronic kidney disease (including dialysis-specific guidance) and antiphospholipid syndrome, each with method-specific categorization for the first time.
  • Revised guidance on breastfeeding/postpartum windows, obesity, surgery with immobilization, VTE with/without anticoagulation, thrombophilia, valvular heart disease, peripartum cardiomyopathy, SLE, HIV risk, cirrhosis/liver tumors, sickle cell disease, and solid organ transplantation.
  • New method-specific guidance added for newer COC formulations (estetrol-containing), patches, rings, POPs, and LNG-IUD systems.

CDC MMWR, August 2024 (Vol. 73, No. 4) — the U.S. MEC app and summary chart are worth having on your own phone for quick point-of-care reference.

Emergency Contraception

Per ACOG — the copper IUD wins on every axis except convenience, and weight genuinely changes which pill to recommend.

MethodWindowPregnancy rateWeight/BMI effect
Copper IUDUp to 5 days~0.3%None — efficacy unaffected by weight
Ulipristal acetateUp to 5 days, no loss of efficacy across the window~1.5%Reduced efficacy at BMI ≥30, but retains meaningfully better performance than levonorgestrel up to BMI ~35
LevonorgestrelLabeled 72h; some efficacy retained to 120h~1.5–2.5%, rising with time elapsedLoses efficacy at a lower BMI threshold than ulipristal — least reliable option in patients with obesity
ACOG's practical ranking

Regardless of body weight, the copper IUD is the most effective EC method, followed by ulipristal acetate. ACOG specifically encourages same-day IUD placement capability for this reason. When ulipristal isn't accessible/acceptable, levonorgestrel remains reasonable — never refuse or discourage EC based on weight; counsel on the efficacy difference and let the patient decide.

  • Timing: effectiveness is highest in the first 24 hours for any oral method — encourage same-day dosing rather than waiting.
  • Mechanism: all EC methods work by delaying or preventing ovulation (or, for copper IUD, primarily by a spermicidal/toxic effect on sperm and ova) — none of them disrupt an existing implanted pregnancy, which is worth stating plainly given persistent public confusion on this point.
  • Starting ongoing contraception after EC: any regular method can start immediately after levonorgestrel or combined EC (with 7 days of backup/abstinence). After ulipristal acetate, wait at least 5 days before starting a progestin-containing method, since progestins can blunt ulipristal's antiprogestin mechanism.

IUD Complications

The ER-relevant IUD presentations — most are more reassuring than they feel to the patient in the moment.

Perforation

Rare — roughly 1 in 1,000 insertions — and occurs almost exclusively at the time of insertion, not spontaneously afterward. Risk is higher with insertion during breastfeeding/postpartum (altered uterine wall tone) and with an inexperienced inserter. A perforated IUD that has migrated into the peritoneal cavity or embedded in bowel/bladder requires surgical (usually laparoscopic) removal — it should not be left in place.

Expulsion

Occurs in roughly 2–10% of users, most commonly in the first year, especially the first 3 months. Risk factors: nulliparity, insertion immediately postpartum or post-abortion, and younger age. A patient who can no longer feel her strings, feels the device at or below the cervical os, or has a partner who feels hard plastic during intercourse should be evaluated for partial/complete expulsion with ultrasound.

"Lost Strings" — A Stepwise Approach

  • First, consider whether strings have simply retracted into the cervical canal (common, benign) versus true expulsion or perforation.
  • Pregnancy test — always, regardless of how unlikely it seems.
  • Pelvic ultrasound to confirm intrauterine location.
  • If not visualized on ultrasound and not expelled, an abdominal X-ray is the next step to rule out a perforated, extrauterine device before assuming it was expelled unnoticed.

Actinomyces-Like Organisms on Pap Smear

Don't reflexively remove the IUD

Incidental detection of actinomyces-like organisms on a routine Pap smear in an asymptomatic IUD user does not require IUD removal or antibiotic treatment — this is a common source of unnecessary device removal. Removal and treatment are reserved for a patient with actual signs/symptoms of pelvic infection.

Pain/Cramping at Removal or Insertion

Genuinely common and generally self-limited; severe pain out of proportion to a routine insertion, especially with heavy bleeding or peritoneal signs, should raise concern for perforation and prompt the workup above rather than reassurance alone.

Permanent Contraception

Brief by design — vasectomy is urologic territory, but both deserve a place in a contraception counseling conversation.

Tubal Ligation vs. Salpingectomy

Salpingectomy has displaced tubal ligation as the preferred approach Tier A

Complete bilateral salpingectomy (full removal of the fallopian tubes, rather than ligation/partial removal) is now favored by ACOG and increasingly standard practice — it provides equivalent or better contraceptive efficacy while also reducing lifetime ovarian cancer risk, since a meaningful proportion of what's classified as "ovarian" high-grade serous cancer actually originates in the fallopian tube epithelium. This makes salpingectomy a reasonable default recommendation over traditional tubal ligation when a patient has decided on permanent sterilization, including as an opportunistic procedure at the time of an already-planned cesarean delivery or other pelvic surgery.

  • Failure rate is low (roughly <1%) but not zero — a pregnancy after tubal sterilization carries a substantially elevated relative risk of being ectopic and should be evaluated accordingly.
  • Should be counseled as permanent — reversal is a complex, not-always-successful surgical undertaking, and patients should make this decision assuming it's final.

Vasectomy — Brief Overview

  • Outpatient urologic procedure, local anesthesia, meaningfully lower complication rate and cost than female sterilization procedures — worth mentioning as the more favorable option on a pure risk/efficacy/cost basis when a couple is deciding between the two.
  • Not immediately effective — requires confirmatory semen analysis showing azoospermia (typically ~3 months/20 ejaculations later); backup contraception is required until confirmed.
  • Also intended as permanent; reversal (vasovasostomy) is possible but success declines with time since the original procedure.

Cervical Cancer Screening

A genuinely useful example of two major guideline bodies actively disagreeing — worth knowing both positions rather than picking one and forgetting the other exists.

ACS (2020)USPSTF
Starting age2521
Ages 21–24No screeningCytology alone, every 3 years
Ages 25–65Primary HPV testing every 5 years (preferred)Cytology every 3 years, or co-testing/primary HPV every 5 years starting at 30
If primary HPV unavailableCo-testing q5yr or cytology alone q3yr—
Why ACS moved the starting age to 25

Fewer than 1% of cervical cancers occur before age 25, most HPV infections in the 21–24 age group are transient and clear spontaneously, and treating the resulting transient abnormalities carries its own downstream risk — including an association with increased preterm birth risk in future pregnancies. Modeling behind the 2020 update projected that starting primary HPV testing at 25 would prevent 13% more cervical cancers and 7% more cervical cancer deaths than the older cytology-first approach, while requiring 45% fewer total tests.

Stopping Screening

Both bodies support discontinuing screening around age 65 in patients with adequate prior negative screening history and no history of CIN2+ in the past 25 years — screening a patient with a long, well-documented negative history well past this point adds little benefit and isn't necessary.

Self-Collection

Self-collected HPV samples are now an acceptable alternative when provider-collected samples aren't feasible or acceptable to the patient — a meaningful access tool for patients who decline or can't tolerate a speculum exam, though provider collection remains preferred when available.

Breast Cancer Screening & Risk Stratification

2024 brought the first real USPSTF change on this topic in years — and there's still no full consensus on frequency.

Average-Risk Screening — Where Bodies Now Agree (Mostly)

USPSTF (2024)ACS / ACR
Starting age4040 (opportunity to start); 45 (regular start, ACS)
IntervalEvery 2 yearsEvery year (ACS/ACR)
Stopping ageNo recommendation ≥75 (insufficient evidence)Continue as long as in good health
The headline change

USPSTF lowering the start age from 50 to 40 (2024) is estimated to prevent 19% more breast cancer deaths than the prior recommendation, and specifically addresses that Black women are 40% more likely to die of breast cancer than white women, often from more aggressive disease diagnosed at younger ages. The remaining disagreement — annual vs. biennial — is a real, unresolved difference between USPSTF and ACS/ACR, not a settled point.

High-Risk Screening & Risk-Assessment Tools

  • Tyrer-Cuzick (IBIS) model and similar risk calculators incorporate family history, breast density, reproductive history, and other factors to estimate lifetime and 10-year risk — used to identify patients who qualify for supplemental MRI screening (generally offered at a calculated lifetime risk ≥20%).
  • Annual breast MRI in addition to mammography is recommended for BRCA1/2 carriers and other confirmed high-lifetime-risk patients, typically starting around age 25–30 — earlier than average-risk mammography alone would start.
  • Dense breast notification laws now require disclosure of breast density on mammogram reports in essentially all U.S. states — relevant because density itself is an independent risk factor and reduces mammographic sensitivity, which is part of the rationale for supplemental imaging discussions in dense-breasted patients.

Chemoprevention for High-Risk Patients

Tamoxifen or raloxifene reduces breast cancer incidence in high-risk patients (5-year risk generally ≥1.67% by risk-model calculation, or known high-risk genetic/histologic factors) by a meaningful margin in trial data — a genuine, underused option worth raising specifically in patients who screen as high-risk but decline or aren't candidates for prophylactic surgery.

Ovarian, Endometrial & Genetic Risk

The module where "there's no screening test" is the correct, evidence-based answer — and where genetic testing changes the entire management plan instead.

Ovarian Cancer — No Effective Average-Risk Screening Tier A evidence, for a negative finding

This is a genuinely settled question, despite ongoing patient demand for "the test"

The PLCO trial (>68,000 women) and the UKCTOCS trial (together >270,000 women across both trials) both found no mortality reduction from annual CA-125 and transvaginal ultrasound screening in average-risk women. Worse, PLCO found 72% of cancers detected through screening were already advanced stage (III/IV) despite the screening effort, and 15% of patients who underwent surgery for a false-positive finding had a serious surgical complication with no cancer found. The FDA issued a 2016 safety communication specifically recommending against marketed "ovarian cancer screening tests" for this reason. Both CDC and USPSTF are explicit: there is no recommended ovarian cancer screening test for average-risk, asymptomatic women.

What this means practically: don't order CA-125/TVUS as a "screening" test for an average-risk asymptomatic patient who requests it — redirect the conversation to symptom awareness (persistent bloating, early satiety, pelvic/abdominal pain, urinary urgency lasting most days for several weeks is the pattern worth acting on) and, if there's a concerning family history, to genetic risk assessment instead (below).

High-Risk Ovarian Cancer Surveillance — Still Imperfect

Even in confirmed BRCA1/2 carriers, semi-annual CA-125/TVUS surveillance has not been shown to reduce mortality — it's offered as an imperfect bridge for patients delaying risk-reducing surgery, not because it's been proven effective. This is a meaningful nuance: the same tests that don't work for screening in average-risk women also don't have great evidence in high-risk women — the real risk-reduction tool is surgery, not surveillance.

Ovarian cancer risk reduction from RRSO

Risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO) also reduces breast cancer risk substantially (37% in BRCA1, 64% in BRCA2 carriers in pooled data) — a dual benefit that factors into timing discussions alongside fertility planning. The tradeoff: RRSO before natural menopause causes surgical menopause, with real downstream effects on bone density, cardiovascular risk, and cognitive/quality-of-life measures — hormone replacement therapy after RRSO does not appear to negate the cancer-risk benefit and is generally appropriate for symptom management unless otherwise contraindicated, a nuance worth knowing since patients (and some providers) sometimes assume HRT is off the table after a BRCA-related surgery.

Lynch Syndrome — Endometrial & Ovarian Risk

  • Lynch syndrome (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, or EPCAM mutations) carries substantially elevated lifetime risk of both endometrial and ovarian cancer, in addition to the better-known colorectal risk.
  • No proven effective screening exists for either endometrial or ovarian cancer in this population either — annual endometrial biopsy/TVUS starting around age 30–35 is commonly offered, but (as with BRCA/ovarian surveillance above) the evidence for mortality benefit is limited rather than well-established.
  • Risk-reducing hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy after completion of childbearing is the recommended definitive risk-reduction strategy — directly analogous to BRCA/RRSO, and the conversation to prioritize over surveillance alone.
  • Postmenopausal bleeding functions as the practical "screening trigger" for endometrial cancer in the general population — any postmenopausal bleeding warrants prompt endometrial evaluation (biopsy ± TVUS) regardless of Lynch status, since there's no routine screening test for endometrial cancer in average-risk women either.

When to Refer for Genetic Counseling

Family history red flags worth a referral
  • Breast cancer diagnosed before age 50, or triple-negative breast cancer at any age
  • Ovarian, fallopian tube, or primary peritoneal cancer at any age, in the patient or a close relative
  • Male breast cancer in the family
  • Multiple relatives on the same side with breast and/or ovarian cancer
  • Known family BRCA or Lynch mutation
  • Personal or family history of colorectal or endometrial cancer before age 50 (Lynch pattern)
  • Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry with any personal or family breast/ovarian cancer history — founder mutation prevalence is substantially higher in this population

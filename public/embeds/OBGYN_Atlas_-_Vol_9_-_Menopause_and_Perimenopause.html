Perimenopause & the Menopausal Transition

STRAW+10 is the gold-standard staging system — worth knowing not for its own sake, but because it clarifies that "perimenopause" is a multi-year process with genuinely distinct phases, not a vague catch-all.

Key Definitions

  • Menopause is diagnosed retrospectively — 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea following the final menstrual period (FMP), with no other cause. Average age in the U.S. is 51.
  • Perimenopause (menopausal transition) spans roughly 4–7 years, beginning with menstrual cycle irregularity and ending 12 months after the FMP.
  • Early menopause: before age 45. Premature ovarian insufficiency: before age 40 (Module 7 — a distinct clinical entity, not just "early menopause turned up sooner").
  • Iatrogenic menopause: from bilateral oophorectomy, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy — abrupt rather than gradual, which matters for symptom severity and management urgency.

Practical point: the late transition stage (60+ day amenorrhea intervals) is where most patients first present asking "is this menopause?" — and it's also the stage where vasomotor symptoms (Module 2) are typically most intense, since it coincides with the most volatile estradiol fluctuations of the entire transition, not simply its lowest point.

Diagnosis Is Clinical, Not Lab-Based

For a woman in the typical age range with classic symptoms and irregular cycles, FSH testing is not required to diagnose the menopausal transition — hormone levels fluctuate too much during this phase to be reliably diagnostic on a single draw. Lab testing becomes more useful specifically when the presentation is atypical (unusually young age, or to help clarify Module 7's premature ovarian insufficiency).

02

Vasomotor Symptoms

Hot flashes and night sweats affect 60–80% of women during the transition — and 2023–2026 brought the first genuinely new drug class in decades for patients who can't or don't want to use hormones.

Mechanism

Declining estrogen removes negative feedback on KNDy neurons (kisspeptin/neurokinin B/dynorphin co-expressing neurons) in the hypothalamus. Increased neurokinin B signaling at these neurons disrupts the hypothalamic thermoregulatory center, narrowing the "thermoneutral zone" and triggering inappropriate heat-dissipation responses (flushing, sweating) — this specific mechanism is what the newest drug class targets directly.

Hormone Therapy — The Most Effective Option

Systemic estrogen (± progestogen) remains the single most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms — full dosing and risk/benefit detail in Module 3. This module focuses on the alternatives, for patients with contraindications or preference against hormones.

NK3/NK1 Receptor Antagonists — The New Class Tier A

Two approvals, back to back

Fezolinetant (Veozah, 45mg once daily) — FDA-approved May 2023, the first selective NK3 receptor antagonist, blocking neurokinin B binding at KNDy neurons directly at the mechanistic root of the hot flash. Demonstrated efficacy across the SKYLIGHT 1/2/4 trials. Requires baseline and periodic liver function monitoring given rare reports of transaminase elevation/liver injury. Elinzanetant (Lynkuet) — a newer dual NK1/NK3 antagonist, FDA-approved more recently, adding NK1 blockade that may extend benefit to sleep disturbance alongside vasomotor symptoms. Both represent a genuinely novel, non-hormonal mechanism — worth knowing as a first-line discussion point for any patient who wants effective VMS treatment without hormones, not just a fallback after SSRIs fail.

03

Hormone Therapy — The WHI Story & Current Guidance

Full dosing depth as requested — but the framing matters as much as the numbers here, since this is the single most publicly misunderstood topic in menopause medicine.

What the WHI Actually Found — and What Got Lost in Translation

The Women's Health Initiative (early 2000s) was a landmark RCT that led to a dramatic, decades-long drop in HRT prescribing after early results showed increased breast cancer, stroke, and VTE risk. What's less publicly known: the average WHI participant was in her early-to-mid 60s, many more than a decade past menopause onset, and the trial wasn't designed around the question most clinicians actually face — a symptomatic woman in her early 50s deciding whether to start HRT near menopause onset.

The "timing hypothesis" — extensively supported by subsequent analysis

Reappraisals of WHI data by age subgroup show a materially different risk profile for women who start HRT within 10 years of menopause onset or before age 60 — including a statistically significant reduction in coronary events with estrogen-alone therapy in the 50–59 age group in long-term follow-up. NAMS/IMS guidance now explicitly endorses this window as the favorable one, with a less favorable benefit-risk ratio for initiation ≥10–20 years past menopause or at age ≥60, where absolute risks of coronary disease, stroke, VTE, and dementia are higher at baseline.

Very recent regulatory development — worth double-checking against current labeling

The FDA has moved to revise systemic hormone therapy product labeling to remove the boxed warning language referencing cardiovascular disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia risk, based on evidence supporting risk reduction for all-cause mortality, fractures, and — in the appropriate age window — cardiac events and cognitive decline. The boxed warning for endometrial cancer risk with unopposed estrogen (in a patient with a uterus) remains. This is recent enough that it's worth confirming current product labeling directly rather than relying on older patient-facing materials that still carry the old warning language.

Compounded "Bioidentical" Hormones — A Genuine Myth to Correct

"Bioidentical" is a marketing term, not a clinical distinction

Both FDA-approved hormone products (estradiol patches/gels/pills, micronized progesterone) and unregulated compounded preparations can be molecularly "bioidentical" to endogenous hormones — the meaningful difference isn't bioidentical-vs-synthetic, it's regulated-vs-unregulated. ACOG's 2023 clinical consensus and the Endocrine Society are both explicit: compounded bioidentical preparations lack the manufacturing oversight, quality control, and proven pharmacokinetics of FDA-approved products, leading to variable absorption/potency, and there is no evidence-based need for compounded therapy when an FDA-approved formulation covering the same hormone and route already exists — which is nearly always the case. Marketing claims that compounded preparations are safer or more "natural" are not supported by evidence.

Duration & Discontinuation

There is no longer a blanket "5-year maximum" rule — current guidance frames duration as an individualized decision between patient and prescriber, continuing as long as benefits outweigh risks for that specific patient, with periodic reassessment rather than an arbitrary stop date.

Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause

Per the 2025 AUA/SUFU/AUGS joint guideline — the most current consensus, and a condition that's genuinely underdiagnosed because patients often don't volunteer these symptoms unprompted.

GSM affects more than half of postmenopausal women — vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, burning, and genitourinary symptoms including recurrent UTI and overactive bladder, from chronic hypoestrogenism of the vulvovaginal and lower urinary tract tissue. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, GSM is progressive and does not resolve on its own over time — a key point for counseling patients who assume it'll "pass" the way hot flashes often do.

Treatment Ladder

1
Over-the-counter vaginal moisturizers and lubricants — sufficient for many women with mild symptoms; reasonable first recommendation regardless of severity given essentially no risk.
2
Low-dose vaginal estrogen — cream, tablet, insert, or ring; effective for moderate-severe symptoms with minimal systemic absorption, and a progestogen is not needed alongside it given the low systemic exposure.
3
Vaginal DHEA (prasterone) — nightly vaginal insert, converted locally to estrogen (and androgen) within vaginal tissue; effective for dryness and dyspareunia, moderate-strength recommendation in the 2025 guideline.
4
Oral ospemifene — a SERM taken as a daily tablet, effective specifically for dyspareunia/dryness; carries an FDA boxed warning for endometrial effects and thrombotic/stroke risk, and can paradoxically worsen vasomotor symptoms in some patients — a genuine tradeoff to discuss, not a risk-free oral alternative to vaginal therapy.

Special Populations

  • On systemic estrogen already but still symptomatic: adding low-dose vaginal estrogen or DHEA on top of systemic therapy is appropriate — systemic HRT alone often under-treats GSM specifically.
  • History of breast or gynecologic cancer: low-dose vaginal estrogen or DHEA may still be considered through shared decision-making with the patient's oncology team — this is not an automatic contraindication, though it requires that explicit conversation rather than reflexive avoidance. Long-term endometrial safety data for vaginal estrogen/DHEA/ospemifene beyond about a year is more limited than for systemic therapy, worth factoring into extended-use conversations.
  • What doesn't have supporting evidence: vaginal/systemic testosterone, vaginal oxytocin, and energy-based therapies (laser, radiofrequency) for GSM lack demonstrated effectiveness per current systematic review — reasonable to steer patients away from these as a primary treatment strategy.

Bone Health & Osteoporosis

Full treatment depth as requested — selection among agents now genuinely depends on baseline fracture risk, not just T-score.

Screening

DEXA is recommended for all women age 65 and older. For younger postmenopausal women (50–65), screening decisions depend on risk-factor assessment (family history, low body weight, smoking, prior fragility fracture, glucocorticoid use, certain medical conditions) rather than universal screening. FRAX incorporates these factors to estimate 10-year major osteoporotic and hip fracture risk, informing both screening and treatment-initiation decisions.

The sequencing principle that matters most

Anabolic agents (romosozumab, teriparatide, abaloparatide) build bone fastest but their gains evaporate without a follow-on antiresorptive — sequencing anabolic-then-antiresorptive, never the reverse, is the standard of care for very-high-risk patients, and skipping the follow-on step is a genuine, avoidable treatment failure.

Drug Holidays

After 5 years of oral (or ~6 years of IV) bisphosphonate therapy, patients with a T-score better than −2.5 and no fragility fracture on therapy may be candidates for a treatment holiday, with reassessment every 2–4 years — this doesn't apply to denosumab (above) or the anabolic agents, which require immediate follow-on therapy rather than a holiday.

Cardiovascular & Metabolic Changes at Menopause

The loss of estrogen's cardioprotective effect is a major reason cardiovascular disease risk in women converges toward men's risk within about a decade after menopause.

What Changes, Mechanistically

  • Lipid profile: LDL cholesterol rises, HDL tends to fall — a shift toward a more atherogenic profile that begins during the transition itself, not only after the final period.
  • Body composition: a shift toward central/visceral adiposity, independent of overall weight change, which itself carries independent cardiometabolic risk.
  • Vascular function: loss of estrogen's favorable effects on endothelial function and vascular tone.
  • Insulin sensitivity: tends to decline, raising type 2 diabetes risk at the population level across the transition.

Where HRT Fits — And Doesn't

HRT is not indicated for primary cardiovascular prevention

Despite the favorable cardiovascular signal in younger, recently-menopausal women discussed in Module 3, hormone therapy is not FDA-approved or recommended as a primary cardiovascular prevention strategy — it's prescribed for vasomotor/genitourinary symptom management, with cardiovascular effects considered as part of the overall risk-benefit picture at the individual level, not as the indication itself. Standard cardiovascular risk reduction — lipid management, blood pressure control, glycemic control, exercise, smoking cessation — remains the primary prevention strategy regardless of HRT use.

Practical Screening Take-Home

The menopausal transition is a reasonable trigger to reassess cardiovascular risk factors proactively — updated lipid panel, blood pressure, glucose/HbA1c, and a genuine conversation about weight/activity changes — rather than waiting for symptoms, given how much of the excess risk accumulates silently across this window.

Premature Ovarian Insufficiency

A distinct clinical entity from typical menopause — not simply menopause arriving early — with real consequences for how aggressively HRT should be pursued.

Definition & Causes

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI): loss of normal ovarian function before age 40. Causes include autoimmune oophoritis, genetic conditions (Turner syndrome, Fragile X premutation), iatrogenic causes (chemotherapy, radiation, bilateral oophorectomy), and — in a substantial proportion of cases — no identifiable cause (idiopathic).

A detail that changes patient counseling

Unlike natural menopause, POI does not always mean complete, permanent ovarian failure — intermittent ovarian function can return unpredictably, with a small but real chance (roughly 5–10%) of spontaneous pregnancy after diagnosis. Contraception counseling remains relevant for any POI patient not actively trying to conceive, which is a frequently overlooked point once a "menopause" label is applied.

Why HRT Is Handled Differently Here

A woman with POI faces 10–15+ additional years of estrogen deficiency compared with a woman experiencing natural menopause at the average age — with corresponding extended exposure to the bone loss, cardiovascular risk, and genitourinary/cognitive effects of hypoestrogenism. For this reason:

  • Hormone therapy is recommended for POI patients essentially by default (absent a specific contraindication), continuing at least until the average natural age of menopause (~51) — this is fundamentally different framing from the individualized, symptom-driven, timing-hypothesis discussion in Module 3 for typical-age menopause.
  • Some POI patients require higher estrogen doses than typical postmenopausal HRT dosing to adequately replace what a premenopausal ovary would normally produce and to control symptoms — dosing should be titrated to symptom control and monitored, not simply capped at standard postmenopausal doses by default.
  • Bone density (DEXA) and cardiovascular risk factor assessment should happen earlier and be monitored more proactively than in typical-age menopause, given the extended deficiency window.
  • Fertility counseling, including discussion of donor oocyte options (Vol. 4, Module 2), is a core part of the initial conversation — this diagnosis often lands as a genuine shock in a patient who wasn't anticipating this discussion in her 20s or 30s, and deserves the same care in delivery as any other significant fertility diagnosis.

Screening Family Members

A confirmed genetic cause (Fragile X premutation in particular) has implications for other family members — appropriate to raise genetic counseling referral for the patient's siblings/relatives when a specific inherited cause is identified, not just for the patient's own future reproductive planning.

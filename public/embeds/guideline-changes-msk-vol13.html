What Changed · November 2025

A narrow but real upgrade to compartment syndrome fixation guidance

AAOS's rapid update to the 2018 Acute Compartment Syndrome guideline is deliberately modest in scope — a single recommendation moved from expert opinion to actual evidence — but it's paired with new supporting literature worth knowing, including a citation that undercuts a commonly taught diagnostic shortcut.

02 · Deep Dive

Operative fixation for fractures with concomitant ACS

Long bone fracture fixation timing Consensus → Limited evidence

For long bone fractures with concomitant acute compartment syndrome requiring fasciotomy, the recommendation itself is unchanged in substance — perform operative fixation (external or internal) for initial stabilization. What changed is the evidentiary footing: this moved from a pure expert-opinion consensus statement in 2018 to a recommendation now backed by limited, but actual, clinical evidence. New supporting studies span internal-vs-external fixation comparisons for tibial fractures, timing of definitive fixation relative to fasciotomy closure, and infection risk by fixation approach in high-energy tibial plateau fractures.

03 · Myth-Busting

Perfusion pressure isn't the diagnostic shortcut it's sometimes taught as

⚠ New supporting citation worth knowing

Among the new literature the 2025 update cites is a 2020 Journal of Orthopaedic Trauma study concluding that perfusion pressure (diastolic blood pressure minus measured compartment pressure) lacks diagnostic specificity for acute compartment syndrome. Some training pathways teach a perfusion-pressure threshold as a clean rule-in/rule-out tool — this citation is a reminder that compartment syndrome remains fundamentally a clinical diagnosis, with pressure measurements as an adjunct rather than a substitute for serial exams and clinical judgment.

04 · On the Horizon

Noninvasive pressure monitoring is being studied

The updated reference list includes a prospective pilot study of a dual-sensor ultrasound-based method for detecting elevated muscle compartment pressures — an early-stage, noninvasive alternative to needle manometry. This isn't part of the guideline's recommendations and shouldn't change practice yet, but it's a space worth watching for a future update.

05 · Bottom Line

For the bedside

  • Don't lean on a perfusion-pressure cutoff as a clean diagnostic rule — the evidence specifically says it lacks specificity. Serial clinical exam remains central.
  • For a long bone fracture with concomitant ACS requiring fasciotomy, operative fixation for initial stabilization now rests on actual evidence, not just expert consensus — useful context when discussing timing with orthopedics.
  • This was a narrow, scheduled evidence refresh, not a guideline overhaul — don't assume other 2018 ACS recommendations changed just because this one updated.

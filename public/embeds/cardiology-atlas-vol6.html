Normal Cardiovascular Physiology of Pregnancy

Before anything else in this volume: pregnancy itself produces changes large enough to mimic real cardiac disease — and disease large enough to hide behind "normal" pregnancy symptoms. Knowing the normal curve is the prerequisite for everything that follows.

The Hemodynamic Curve Across Pregnancy

Cardiac output and blood volume rise progressively, peaking around late second/third trimester, with a further transient spike immediately postpartum from autotransfusion — while systemic vascular resistance falls (driving the mildly lower blood pressure typical of mid-pregnancy) before renormalizing.
Why labor and delivery are a distinct hemodynamic event

Each uterine contraction autotransfuses roughly 300-500 mL of blood back into the maternal circulation, and delivery itself relieves aortocaval compression from the gravid uterus — together producing an acute surge in venous return and cardiac output right at delivery and in the immediate postpartum period. This is precisely why decompensation in a patient with limited cardiac reserve (significant valvular disease, cardiomyopathy) often happens at delivery or in the first 24-48 hours after, not necessarily during pregnancy itself.

Normal Findings That Mimic Disease

Symptoms

Dyspnea on exertion, mild peripheral edema, fatigue, and palpitations are all extremely common in normal pregnancy due to the physiologic changes above — the challenge is recognizing when these cross from expected to pathologic (orthopnea, PND, dyspnea at rest, or symptoms markedly out of proportion to gestational age).

Exam and testing

A soft systolic ejection ("flow") murmur from increased flow across normal valves is heard in the large majority of pregnant patients and is not pathologic on its own. ECG may show a leftward axis shift, and a mild degree of physiologic chamber enlargement with preserved-to-hyperdynamic EF is normal on echo — the distinction from pathologic remodeling rests on the degree of change and the presence of actual dysfunction, not just size.

Where the line actually is

New S3, diastolic murmurs of any kind, a loud or harsh systolic murmur, reduced EF, or symptoms that are severe, progressive, or occur at rest are not explained by normal physiology and warrant full cardiac workup — the rest of this volume covers what's on the other side of that line.

Peripartum Cardiomyopathy

Any heart failure presentation in late pregnancy or the postpartum period deserves this diagnosis on the differential — built to the 2025 ESC Guidelines for Cardiovascular Disease and Pregnancy.

Diagnosis, Mechanism, and Treatment

The 16-kDa prolactin fragment mechanism directly motivates the bromocriptine treatment strategy — suppressing prolactin release is intended to reduce generation of the pathogenic fragment itself, not just treat symptoms.
Do not miss peripartum cardiomyopathy

This is stated explicitly as a top priority in the 2025 ESC guideline's core messaging: in any case of heart failure during late pregnancy or postpartum, actively consider PPCM. Prompt treatment is critical, and may include bromocriptine as part of a broader GDMT strategy.

Bromocriptine — Where the Evidence Actually Stands

The evidence base remains genuinely uncertain despite widespread clinical interest. Multiple meta-analyses (2025) show bromocriptine improves LVEF recovery and remodeling compared to standard heart failure therapy alone, without a significant difference in thromboembolic events between groups. However, the ESC guideline gives it only a Class IIb recommendation ("may be considered") — reflecting that existing trials have been small, and the ongoing REBIRTH trial (results not expected until 2029) is specifically designed to resolve this uncertainty with more definitive data. The US has not reached consensus on routine bromocriptine use, creating a real regional practice variation worth being aware of.

If you use bromocriptine, anticoagulate

Bromocriptine carries its own prothrombotic risk, stacking on top of the baseline hypercoagulable state of both pregnancy/postpartum and severely reduced LV systolic function. Concurrent anticoagulation (typically prophylactic-dose, individualized to the specific risk profile) is a standard part of the treatment package when bromocriptine is used — not an optional add-on.

Prognosis and Subsequent Pregnancy

Recovery is common but not universal

A substantial proportion of PPCM patients recover LV function significantly, often within 6-12 months — but a meaningful minority have persistent dysfunction, and a small subset progress to advanced heart failure requiring transplant-level care.

Subsequent pregnancy risk

If LV function has not recovered, subsequent pregnancy carries substantial risk of relapse and further decompensation — the 2025 guideline is explicit that a subsequent pregnancy should be avoided in this circumstance. Even with full recovery, subsequent pregnancy carries some recurrence risk and warrants closer monitoring and prepregnancy counseling through a Pregnancy Heart Team.

Genetic overlap with dilated cardiomyopathy

Genetic testing is now recommended, since a meaningful subset of PPCM cases carry pathogenic variants in genes also implicated in dilated cardiomyopathy (TTN/titin being the most frequently identified) — suggesting that for some patients, pregnancy acts as a physiologic stress test that unmasks an underlying genetic predisposition rather than causing an entirely de novo disease process.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology (heart failure specialist if available) immediately upon suspicion — early GDMT initiation and bromocriptine decision-making benefit from specialist input rather than delay.
ICU admission
Any patient with significant hemodynamic compromise, the arrhythmic phenotype (malignant ventricular arrhythmia or arrest), or need for inotropic/mechanical circulatory support.
Transfer if no maternal cardiac/high-risk obstetric program on-site
Any moderate-to-severe presentation — ideally to a center with an established Pregnancy Heart Team model per the 2025 ESC framework, particularly if still pregnant and delivery planning needs to be coordinated with cardiac status.
Transport considerations
Continuous cardiac monitoring; if still pregnant, coordinate with obstetric/maternal-fetal medicine regarding fetal monitoring and delivery timing during transport; a wearable defibrillator should accompany the patient if already fitted, with device status communicated to the receiving team.
Who to call
Cardiology and maternal-fetal medicine jointly if still pregnant; cardiology and obstetrics jointly if postpartum — this is a genuinely multidisciplinary condition from diagnosis through subsequent-pregnancy counseling.

Preeclampsia & Eclampsia

A multisystem disease with cardiac involvement at its core (hypertensive crisis, pulmonary edema) — and one of the clearest examples in obstetrics of when "admit for observation" isn't enough and ICU-level care is genuinely required.

The Spectrum

Severe features can appear in isolation or in combination — any single one qualifies as "preeclampsia with severe features" and changes the management pathway, including delivery timing considerations regardless of gestational age in many cases.

Magnesium Sulfate — Seizure Prophylaxis and Treatment

Standard regimen: loading dose 4-6 g IV over 20-30 minutes, followed by a maintenance infusion of 1-2 g/hour, continued through delivery and typically 24 hours postpartum. Magnesium has a narrow therapeutic window — monitor for toxicity (loss of deep tendon reflexes is typically the first sign, followed by respiratory depression and, at very high levels, cardiac arrest) especially in renal impairment, where clearance is reduced and dose adjustment or closer monitoring is needed. Calcium gluconate is the reversal agent for magnesium toxicity.

Antihypertensive choices in pregnancy

IV labetalol and IV hydralazine are the traditional first-line agents for acute severe hypertension in pregnancy; oral nifedipine (immediate-release) is also effective and increasingly favored for its ease of administration. ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors are contraindicated throughout pregnancy given fetotoxicity (see Vol. 6's Drug Safety section for the full reference).

The CHAP trial changed chronic hypertension management

The 2022 CHAP trial demonstrated that treating chronic hypertension in pregnancy to a target below 140/90 — rather than the older, more permissive strategy of only treating severe hypertension — reduced severe preeclampsia, preterm birth, and other adverse outcomes, without increasing small-for-gestational-age infants. This shifted practice toward tighter chronic BP control throughout pregnancy rather than a wait-and-treat-only-if-severe approach.

Postpartum preeclampsia is a real and often-missed entity

Preeclampsia and eclampsia can present for the first time up to weeks after delivery, when clinical vigilance has typically dropped. Any postpartum patient presenting with new headache, visual changes, or hypertension deserves preeclampsia on the differential regardless of how unremarkable the pregnancy and delivery were.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Maternal-fetal medicine and/or obstetrics immediately for any severe features; anesthesiology early if delivery is anticipated, given implications for neuraxial anesthesia timing with thrombocytopenia or magnesium therapy.
ICU admission
Any of the ICU-level red flags above — eclampsia, respiratory compromise, HELLP with DIC/hepatic concern, refractory hypertension, neurologic emergency, or hemodynamic instability. This is not a "soft" threshold; these patients need continuous, high-acuity monitoring that a standard L&D unit typically cannot provide.
Transfer if no maternal-fetal medicine/ICU obstetric capability on-site
Any severe-features patient at a facility without appropriate maternal critical care capability, arranged before further deterioration — this includes considering maternal transport separately from neonatal transport logistics if delivery has already occurred.
Transport considerations
Continue magnesium infusion uninterrupted throughout transport with monitoring for toxicity; continuous BP monitoring with antihypertensive therapy readily available; a clinician capable of managing an eclamptic seizure en route; coordinate with the receiving facility's obstetric anesthesia and maternal-fetal medicine teams before departure.
Who to call
Maternal-fetal medicine for high-risk obstetric management; critical care/ICU jointly for any patient meeting ICU-level criteria; neurology emergently if stroke or significant neurologic findings are present.

Peripartum SCAD & Pregnancy ACS

Chest pain and troponin elevation in a peripartum patient is not automatically atherosclerotic disease — and treating it as such can make things worse.

Why Pregnancy Predisposes to SCAD

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection is disproportionately a disease of women, and pregnancy — particularly the peripartum and early postpartum period — represents its highest-risk window. Hormonal effects on the vessel wall (progesterone-mediated changes in collagen and elastin architecture) are thought to weaken the arterial media, predisposing to spontaneous intimal tear or intramural hematoma formation independent of atherosclerosis.

The management philosophy is nearly inverted from standard ACS: where atherosclerotic disease usually benefits from prompt PCI, SCAD often benefits from watchful waiting, since instrumentation itself is a risk to the fragile dissected wall.
Recognize it before the cath lab reflexively stents it

A young peripartum patient with chest pain, minimal traditional risk factors, and an angiogram showing a long, smooth, tapering stenosis (rather than a discrete atherosclerotic-appearing lesion) should raise immediate suspicion for SCAD. Communicating this possibility to the interventional team before the procedure — not just interpreting images after the fact — can change the immediate management approach in the cath lab itself.

When Intervention IS Warranted

Ongoing ischemia

Persistent chest pain with ECG changes or hemodynamic compromise despite medical therapy suggests the conservative approach isn't sufficient — intervention (with careful, gentle technique to minimize further dissection propagation) becomes reasonable.

High-risk anatomy

Left main or proximal dissection threatening a large myocardial territory changes the risk-benefit calculation toward intervention despite the general preference for conservative management in SCAD overall.

Broader Pregnancy-Associated MI Considerations

Beyond SCAD specifically, pregnancy-associated MI can also result from standard atherosclerotic disease (increasingly recognized as maternal age at pregnancy rises), coronary spasm, thrombosis in situ without underlying atherosclerosis, or (rarely) coronary embolism. The full ACS workup (ECG, serial troponins, and echocardiography) still applies — the key adaptation is maintaining a broader differential than "atherosclerotic plaque rupture" by default, particularly in a young patient without traditional risk factors.

Antiplatelet and imaging considerations

Standard antiplatelet therapy is still generally used in SCAD, though the intensity and duration are often individualized given the different underlying mechanism. Intracoronary imaging (OCT or IVUS) can help confirm the SCAD diagnosis and characterize the dissection when the diagnosis is uncertain on angiography alone — but this itself requires careful technique in a fragile, dissected vessel.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Interventional cardiology with specific experience in SCAD management if available — this is a condition where operator experience with the specific entity (not just general PCI volume) meaningfully affects decision-making quality.
ICU/monitored admission
Any confirmed or suspected SCAD, regardless of initial management approach (conservative or interventional) — these patients need close monitoring for extension or recurrent ischemia during the acute phase.
Transfer if no cardiac catheterization capability on-site
Any peripartum patient with suspected ACS needs cardiac catheterization capability for diagnosis, following standard ACS transfer urgency — the SCAD-specific management nuance applies once the diagnosis is established, not before.
Transport considerations
Standard ACS transport precautions (continuous ECG monitoring, avoid unnecessary agitation of a potentially fragile vascular lesion); coordinate with maternal-fetal medicine if still pregnant regarding fetal monitoring during transport.
Who to call
Interventional cardiology for diagnostic angiography and management decision-making; maternal-fetal medicine in parallel if the patient is still pregnant, since delivery timing and cardiac management need to be coordinated.

Amniotic Fluid Embolism & VTE

Two very different causes of sudden peripartum cardiopulmonary collapse — one a rare catastrophic reaction, the other a common and largely preventable risk that pregnancy itself amplifies substantially.

Amniotic Fluid Embolism

Modern understanding frames AFE more as an anaphylactoid/inflammatory reaction to fetal material than a purely mechanical embolic event — which is why the name persists despite not fully capturing the mechanism.
There is no confirmatory test — act on clinical suspicion

AFE is a diagnosis of exclusion made in real time based on the clinical picture; waiting for a definitive test before initiating aggressive resuscitation is not an option, because none exists at the bedside. Sudden hypoxia, hemodynamic collapse, and/or coagulopathy in the peripartum period should trigger immediate maximal supportive care and massive transfusion protocol activation in parallel, not sequentially.

Management Principles

Resuscitation

High-flow oxygen or intubation as needed, aggressive hemodynamic support (fluids judiciously, vasopressors/inotropes for cardiogenic component), and immediate delivery if the fetus has not yet been delivered — the fetus generally cannot be adequately supported if the mother is in extremis, and delivery itself can improve maternal hemodynamics by relieving aortocaval compression.

Coagulopathy management

DIC typically develops rapidly and severely — early, proactive activation of a massive transfusion protocol (rather than waiting for lab-confirmed coagulopathy) is standard given how quickly and severely the consumptive process progresses in AFE specifically.

Venous Thromboembolism in Pregnancy

Pregnancy is an independent, substantial VTE risk factor through all three components of Virchow's triad: venous stasis (mechanical compression by the gravid uterus, hormonally mediated venous dilation), hypercoagulability (pregnancy is a physiologically procoagulant state — increased clotting factors, decreased protein S), and vascular injury (particularly at delivery, especially cesarean delivery).

Risk persists postpartum, often underappreciated

VTE risk remains meaningfully elevated for approximately 6 weeks postpartum, with the highest relative risk in the first 3 weeks after delivery — clinicians and patients alike often mentally "close the book" on pregnancy-related risk at delivery, but this is exactly the highest-risk window. Any postpartum patient with dyspnea, chest pain, or leg swelling deserves the same VTE workup rigor as anyone else, without discounting symptoms as "normal postpartum recovery."

Diagnostic imaging is not contraindicated by pregnancy

Appropriately protocoled CT pulmonary angiography and V/Q scanning both carry acceptably low fetal radiation exposure and should not be withheld from a pregnant patient with genuine clinical suspicion for PE — the risk of a missed, untreated PE substantially outweighs the radiation exposure risk from a properly performed study. Compression ultrasound for suspected DVT carries no radiation concern at all and is a reasonable first step when the leg is symptomatic.

Treatment

Low molecular weight heparin is the preferred anticoagulant for VTE treatment and prophylaxis throughout pregnancy per the 2025 ESC guideline — it does not cross the placenta, has a favorable safety profile, and allows weekly anti-factor Xa monitoring to guide dosing. Direct oral anticoagulants are not recommended during pregnancy given limited safety data and placental transfer concerns; warfarin is generally avoided given teratogenicity, particularly in the first trimester (see Vol. 6's Drug Safety section for full detail on anticoagulant choices across pregnancy and lactation).

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Critical care, maternal-fetal medicine, obstetric anesthesia, and hematology (for coagulopathy management) essentially simultaneously for suspected AFE — this is a multidisciplinary crisis response, not a sequential consult chain.
ICU admission
Any suspected AFE, immediately and without exception. Massive or submassive PE with hemodynamic compromise also warrants ICU-level monitoring.
Transfer if no appropriate obstetric critical care capability on-site
AFE survivors requiring ongoing critical care support, or complex VTE cases (e.g., requiring catheter-directed thrombolysis or surgical embolectomy) needing capability not available locally — though initial resuscitation and stabilization should never be delayed for transfer in an acutely unstable patient.
Transport considerations
A physician-level critical care transport team given the potential for sudden deterioration; blood products immediately available given DIC risk in AFE; continuous hemodynamic and coagulation monitoring throughout.
Who to call
Activate your institution's massive transfusion protocol and obstetric emergency response team simultaneously for suspected AFE; hematology for complex coagulopathy management; vascular medicine or interventional radiology for advanced VTE interventions when indicated.

mWHO Risk Classification

The 2025 ESC Guidelines adopt mWHO 2.0, refined with the CARPREG II prognostic model — and introduce the Pregnancy Heart Team as the standard-of-care model for moderate-to-high-risk patients.

The Four Classes

ClassRisk levelModel of care
INo detectable increased maternal riskStandard obstetric management
IISmall increased riskCardiology follow-up; Pregnancy Heart Team not routinely required
II–IIIIntermediate riskIndividualized management; Pregnancy Heart Team often advised
IIIHigh riskMandatory Pregnancy Heart Team oversight, specialist center
IVVery high / extreme riskNo longer labeled "absolutely contraindicated" — requires shared decision-making within the Pregnancy Heart Team, detailed maternal-fetal risk counseling, and maximally safe monitoring if pregnancy continues
What changed from the 2018 classification

The 2025 update integrates the CARPREG II prognostic model to refine risk estimates within each class, formally establishes the Pregnancy Heart Team (cardiologist, obstetrician, anesthesiologist, and additional specialists as needed) as standard of care from preconception through 6 months postpartum for Class II-III and above, and — notably — softens Class IV from an absolute contraindication to a shared-decision-making framework, reflecting improved outcomes data and patient autonomy considerations.

Fertility treatment considerations

Fertility treatment requires careful risk-benefit assessment in mWHO Class III (and specific caution in patients on anticoagulation) and warrants especially careful shared decision-making in Class IV — the hemodynamic and hormonal stimulation of fertility treatment itself carries additional cardiovascular risk on top of pregnancy risk.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Cardiology preconception (ideally) or as early as possible in pregnancy for any known Class II-III or higher lesion — this should not wait for symptoms to develop.
Establish Pregnancy Heart Team
Mandatory for Class III and above per the 2025 guideline; strongly advised for Class II-III — this multidisciplinary structure should be in place well before delivery planning becomes urgent.
Transfer if no Pregnancy Heart Team/specialist center available
Any Class III or IV patient should be under the care of a center with this specific multidisciplinary capability — arrange transfer of care early in pregnancy, not at the time of delivery.
Transport considerations
For acute decompensation in a known high-risk lesion, transport considerations follow the specific underlying condition (see the relevant condition-specific sections throughout this atlas series) — communicate the mWHO class and specific lesion clearly to the receiving team.
Who to call
The patient's established Pregnancy Heart Team if one exists; otherwise, maternal-fetal medicine and cardiology jointly to establish one as early as possible.

The ACEi/ARB pregnancy-lactation divergence is worth remembering specifically

ACE inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated throughout pregnancy but captopril and enalapril are generally considered compatible with breastfeeding — this is one of the more counterintuitive and clinically important divergences in this entire reference, and worth flagging explicitly since the instinct to avoid an entire drug class "just in case" across both pregnancy and lactation would unnecessarily limit good postpartum options.

The general framework, when a specific drug isn't covered here

Favor drugs with the longest track record and most reassuring registry data over newer agents with theoretical advantages but limited pregnancy-specific safety data; involve maternal-fetal medicine and, where available, a specialized teratogen information service for anything not clearly covered by established guidance; and remember that untreated maternal cardiac disease carries its own substantial fetal risk — the goal is optimizing the maternal-fetal risk balance, not reflexively avoiding all medication.

Escalation & Transfer Criteria
Consult now
Maternal-fetal medicine for any medication decision outside the clearly-established safe options above, particularly involving anticoagulation strategy for mechanical valves or antiarrhythmic selection for complex arrhythmias.
Pharmacy involvement
Clinical pharmacy with obstetric/teratogen expertise for any complex multi-drug regimen or when local formulary constraints require substituting an unfamiliar agent.
Transfer considerations
Communicate exact medications, doses, and the specific reasoning behind any pregnancy-modified regimen clearly in all transfer documentation — a receiving team unfamiliar with the pregnancy-specific rationale may otherwise reflexively "correct" an intentionally modified regimen.
Who to call
Maternal-fetal medicine as the primary resource for medication safety questions in pregnancy; a regional teratogen information service (where available) for anything genuinely ambiguous or not well covered by standard references.

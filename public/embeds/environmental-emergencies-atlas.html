🌡️ Heat Illness

EntityPresentation
Heat crampsPainful involuntary muscle spasms, typically in heavily exercised muscles, from sodium/fluid losses during sweating — core temperature usually normal or only mildly elevated.
Heat exhaustionCore temperature elevated but generally <40°C, with fatigue, weakness, headache, nausea, and heavy sweating — critically, NO significant CNS dysfunction (patient is oriented). This distinction is the entire basis of the exhaustion-vs-stroke diagnosis.
Heat strokeCore temperature typically >40°C WITH central nervous system dysfunction (confusion, ataxia, seizure, coma) — the CNS dysfunction, not the specific temperature cutoff, is what actually defines heat stroke and separates it from severe heat exhaustion.

Classic Heat Stroke

Classic (non-exertional) heat stroke occurs in patients whose thermoregulatory capacity is overwhelmed by environmental heat alone — typically elderly patients, those with chronic illness, or patients on medications impairing thermoregulation (anticholinergics, diuretics, antipsychotics), during heat waves, often without exertion. Onset is gradual over days.

Exertional Heat Stroke

Exertional heat stroke (EHS) occurs in younger, often healthy individuals during strenuous physical activity (athletes, military recruits, laborers) where metabolic heat production from exercising skeletal muscle overwhelms dissipation — onset is rapid, over hours, and can occur even in mild or cool ambient conditions if exertion is intense enough.

Shared Pathophysiology
Both converge on the same final pathway: once core temperature exceeds a critical threshold (commonly cited around 40.8°C / 105.5°F), direct cellular thermal injury occurs across multiple organ systems — protein denaturation, cell membrane destabilization, mitochondrial dysfunction, and a systemic inflammatory response resembling sepsis physiology. Downstream complications include rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, hepatocellular injury, and multi-organ failure — the severity and reversibility of which depend heavily on how long core temperature remains above the critical threshold, not just the peak temperature reached.

Cooling: The Evidence

The single most important predictor of survival and organ recovery in heat stroke is not the peak temperature but the TIME spent above the critical thermal threshold — which makes cooling rate, not just eventual normothermia, the actual treatment target.

Figure — Cooling rate comparison by method
⚡ Cold Water Immersion Data
Cold water immersion (CWI) is the evidence-based gold standard, achieving cooling rates of at least 0.15°C/minute — roughly four times faster than air-based cooling methods, because water's thermal conductivity is approximately 24x that of air and its higher density/specific heat capacity allow near-complete body surface area contact. A systematic review of 32 studies (521 EHS patients) found that achieving a cooling rate >0.15°C/min was directly associated with reduced case-fatality. Properly supervised athletic/military events using immediate on-site CWI report near-zero fatality rates for EHS — a striking contrast to community-acquired (classic) heat stroke, where delayed recognition and lack of immediate cooling resources drive substantially worse outcomes.
Practical Notes
The 'Currie response' (a transient 0.1–0.2°C rise in a normothermic person's temperature upon initial cold water entry, from peripheral vasoconstriction trapping core heat) has historically been misinterpreted by clinicians as evidence CWI is dangerous or ineffective — it is not; the effect is transient, minor, and clinically insignificant in a hyperthermic patient. Emerging evidence suggests women may cool faster than men during CWI at equivalent hyperthermia despite similar metabolic rates, raising a not-yet-resolved question of overcooling risk that current unisex guidelines don't yet address. Where whole-body CWI isn't feasible (ED settings requiring airway management, remote/austere settings), ice-water-soaked sheet coverage over trunk and extremities, replaced every 4–5 minutes, is an emerging alternative with reasonable preliminary cooling efficacy.
Management Principles
  • Initiate cooling immediately, on-site, before or simultaneous with transport — do not delay cooling to first obtain IV access, labs, or transport to a facility
  • Continuous core temperature monitoring (rectal probe) during cooling — skin/oral/tympanic temperatures are unreliable in this setting and can lead to premature cessation of cooling
  • Stop active cooling around 38.5–39°C to avoid overshoot into iatrogenic hypothermia
  • Aggressively evaluate for and manage complications once cooling is underway: rhabdomyolysis (CK, fluid resuscitation), coagulopathy/DIC, hepatic injury, and electrolyte derangements
  • In illicit-drug-associated hyperthermia (stimulant toxicity, serotonin syndrome), the same aggressive cooling-within-15-minutes principle applies — treat the hyperthermia as the time-critical emergency it is regardless of etiology, while addressing the underlying toxidrome in parallel

🏕️ In the Field

  • Remove from the heat source and remove excess clothing/equipment immediately
  • Begin cooling with whatever is available before anything else — immerse in any available cold/cool water (stream, tub, kiddie pool), or failing that, douse with water and fan aggressively while applying ice packs to the neck, axillae, and groin (areas overlying major vessels)
  • Do NOT wait for advanced equipment or a rectal thermometer to start cooling — improvised cooling started in the first minutes matters more than achieving a perfect setup later
  • Do NOT give antipyretics (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) — heat stroke is not a hypothalamic-set-point fever, so antipyretics do nothing for the core problem and add hepatic/renal risk in a patient who may already have organ injury
  • If altered mental status is present, position for airway protection and prepare for the possibility of vomiting/aspiration during cooling
  • Continue cooling en route to the hospital — do not stop cooling efforts just because transport has started

🏥 In the ED

  • Confirm/continue aggressive cooling immediately on arrival if not already at goal — do not let ED triage or registration delay ongoing cooling
  • Continuous rectal core temperature monitoring throughout the ED stay
  • Labs: CBC, CMP, CK, coagulation studies (DIC screen), hepatic panel, lactate — send early given how quickly multi-organ complications can evolve
  • IV crystalloid resuscitation guided by hemodynamics and urine output, with attention to rhabdomyolysis-driven renal protection goals
  • ICU-level monitoring/admission for any patient with persistent altered mental status, significant organ dysfunction on labs, or need for ongoing intensive cooling/hemodynamic support
  • Reassess neurologic status frequently — persistent CNS dysfunction after adequate cooling is a poor prognostic sign warranting escalation of care

🛡️ Prevention

  • Gradual heat acclimatization over 10–14 days before intense exertion in hot conditions — the single most effective preventive measure for exertional heat stroke
  • Scheduled hydration before, during, and after activity — thirst is a lagging indicator, not a reliable real-time guide
  • Adjust or cancel activity based on wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) rather than air temperature alone — humidity and radiant heat load matter as much as ambient temperature
  • Buddy system for athletic/military/labor settings — early heat illness is often first recognized by a teammate, not the affected person themselves, since confusion is an early symptom
  • For classic (non-exertional) heat stroke prevention in vulnerable populations: welfare checks on elderly/isolated individuals during heat waves, access to air conditioning or cooling centers, and medication review for drugs impairing thermoregulation
  • Avoid peak-heat-hour exertion when possible; schedule strenuous activity for early morning or evening

🧊 Hypothermia

Water conducts heat away from the body roughly 25x faster than still air — which is why immersion hypothermia (fishing accidents, capsized boats) can occur rapidly even in water that doesn't feel dangerously cold, and why wet clothing without wind protection causes a substantial share of hypothermia fatalities at temperatures well above freezing. As core temperature falls, metabolic rate drops roughly 6% per 1°C decrease, progressively suppressing cardiac output, heart rate, respiratory rate, and consciousness. Shivering — the body's primary voluntary heat-generation mechanism — is maximal around 35°C and paradoxically STOPS around 30–32°C once the shivering centers themselves become too cold to fire, removing the body's own compensatory mechanism precisely when it's needed most.

Field Assessment
Core temperature measurement is frequently unavailable in the field, which is why the Revised Swiss Staging System is built around clinical findings (AVPU-based consciousness level, presence/absence of shivering, presence/absence of vital signs) rather than requiring a number — the field staging should guide triage and transport destination decisions even without a confirmed temperature.
⚡ Afterdrop
When rewarming begins — particularly if peripheral/extremity rewarming happens before core rewarming — peripheral vasodilation releases a bolus of cold, acidotic, hyperkalemic blood from the extremities back toward the core, transiently dropping core temperature further (typically 1–3°C over the first 20–30 minutes) and raising arrhythmia risk, particularly ventricular fibrillation. This is an EXPECTED phenomenon, not a sign rewarming is failing — the correct response is to continue rewarming (with core-focused technique: torso before extremities) while monitoring closely, not to stop.

Rewarming Methods

Passive external

Insulation, remove wet clothing, protect from wind/cold surfaces — sufficient for HT I, relies entirely on the patient's own residual heat production.

Active external

Forced-air warming systems (e.g., Bair Hugger), warm packs to torso/axillae/groin — raises core temperature roughly 0.8–2.5°C/hour; the mainstay for HT I–II.

Active internal (core)

Warmed IV fluids, warmed humidified oxygen, peritoneal lavage, bladder irrigation — adjuncts for more severe hypothermia, generally insufficient alone for HT III–IV.

Extracorporeal (ECLS/ECMO)

The definitive treatment for HT III with cardiac instability and HT IV (hypothermic cardiac arrest) — veno-arterial ECMO allows controlled, gradual core rewarming while simultaneously providing circulatory and respiratory support through the hemodynamically unstable rewarming period. Successful rewarming has been reported from core temperatures as low as 13.7°C and after as long as 6 hours 45 minutes of hypothermic cardiac arrest with ventricular fibrillation. Associated with improved neurologic outcomes compared to other internal rewarming techniques and should be considered whenever severe hypothermia fails to respond to other measures or presents as cardiac arrest, ideally via early transfer to a capable center.

⚡ Pearl
“No one is dead until they're warm and dead” — the classic teaching point reflecting that hypothermia itself is neuroprotective (reduced cerebral metabolic demand), meaning prolonged, aggressive resuscitation efforts are warranted in hypothermic cardiac arrest that would otherwise be considered futile in a normothermic patient.

🏕️ In the Field

  • Handle gently — rough movement/jostling of a moderately-to-severely hypothermic patient can precipitate ventricular fibrillation; move as if handling a trauma patient with suspected unstable injuries, even without one
  • Remove wet clothing and insulate from further heat loss (dry blankets/sleeping bag, vapor barrier, protect from wind and cold ground) — insulation from further loss matters as much as active warming in the field
  • Horizontal positioning rather than upright/walking — avoids orthostatic stress on a cold, bradycardic cardiovascular system and reduces the risk of triggering afterdrop-related arrhythmia
  • Warm sweet drinks are reasonable ONLY if the patient is clearly alert and able to protect their airway (HT I) — do not attempt oral intake in anyone with altered consciousness
  • Begin passive/active external rewarming (heat packs to torso/axillae/groin, forced-air warming if available) while arranging transport — do not delay transport waiting to fully rewarm in the field
  • Check for pulse/breathing for a FULL 60 seconds before declaring cardiac arrest — severe hypothermia can produce a pulse so slow and faint it's easily missed on a brief check, and starting compressions on a bradycardic-but-perfusing hypothermic heart can itself precipitate VF

🏥 In the ED

  • Continuous cardiac monitoring throughout rewarming — arrhythmia risk (including from afterdrop) persists until core temperature is well clear of the danger zone
  • Match rewarming method to Swiss stage: active external ± warmed IV fluids/humidified oxygen for HT I–II; escalate to active internal methods and consider ECMO/ECLS transfer for HT III; ECMO is the treatment of choice for HT IV (hypothermic cardiac arrest)
  • Early consultation/transfer to an ECMO-capable center for any HT III with cardiac instability or HT IV — do not delay this decision waiting to see if less invasive rewarming works first in a clearly severe presentation
  • Continue CPR throughout transport/rewarming in cardiac arrest — do not withhold or terminate resuscitation based on presentation alone; hypothermic patients have survived neurologically intact after hours of arrest and very low core temperatures
  • Screen for and treat precipitating/comorbid conditions (sepsis, hypoglycemia, alcohol intoxication, trauma) that may have contributed to or resulted from the hypothermic episode
  • Correct electrolyte and acid-base disturbances gradually alongside rewarming rather than aggressively all at once

🛡️ Prevention

  • Layered clothing with moisture-wicking base layers — avoid cotton, which retains moisture against the skin and accelerates heat loss when wet
  • Stay dry — change out of wet clothing (rain, sweat, stream crossings) as soon as practical; water conducts heat away roughly 25× faster than still air
  • Avoid alcohol before/during cold exposure — it causes peripheral vasodilation (a false sensation of warmth) while actually accelerating core heat loss
  • Adequate caloric intake and hydration — shivering thermogenesis is metabolically expensive and requires fuel
  • Buddy system in wilderness/cold-water settings — early hypothermia impairs judgment and self-recognition, making a companion's observation critical
  • Weather-appropriate trip planning with a margin for unexpected delays/exposure (extra layers, emergency shelter, communication device)

❄️ Frostbite & Trench Foot

Two distinct, sequential injury mechanisms drive frostbite. First, direct freeze injury: extracellular ice crystal formation as tissue temperature drops (blood/lymph flow effectively stops below ~15°C in digits, ~19°C in fingers specifically per some sources) draws water osmotically out of cells, causing cellular dehydration and direct mechanical/osmotic membrane damage. Second — and this is the mechanism modern treatment actually targets — progressive tissue ischemia AFTER rewarming, as the freeze-thaw cycle triggers endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis, and progressive tissue necrosis over the following 24–72 hours, even in tissue that appeared to survive the initial freeze. This second, delayed ischemic phase is why frostbite severity often can't be accurately assessed immediately after rewarming and why thrombolytic therapy targets a specific post-thaw treatment window rather than the initial freeze itself.

Field Care Quick Reference
  • Remove wet/constrictive clothing and jewelry before any freezing progresses further
  • Do NOT rub the affected area — mechanical trauma to already-injured, ice-crystal-laden tissue worsens damage, contrary to old folk teaching
  • Do NOT rewarm if there is any risk of refreezing before reaching definitive care — a freeze-thaw-refreeze cycle causes dramatically worse tissue damage than remaining frozen throughout transport
  • If rewarming is initiated, it should be rapid, not gradual — slow spontaneous rewarming (e.g., simply removing from the cold and waiting) produces worse outcomes than controlled rapid rewarming
Rewarming
Rapid rewarming in a circulating water bath maintained at 37–39°C (98.6–102.2°F) until the tissue becomes soft and pliable to the touch, typically ~30 minutes — water temperature above this range risks superimposed thermal burn on already-compromised tissue, and below it fails to achieve adequately rapid rewarming. This is intensely painful; adequate analgesia (often requiring opioids) should be provided proactively, and ibuprofen is specifically recommended as an adjunct given its dual analgesic and anti-thromboxane (anti-vasoconstrictive) effect relevant to the underlying pathophysiology.

Thrombolysis

Because the dominant tissue-destroying mechanism is progressive post-thaw microvascular thrombosis rather than the initial freeze injury itself, thrombolytic therapy targeting that thrombosis — not just rewarming — has become a guideline-supported treatment for severe (grade 3–4) frostbite when given early enough in the post-thaw window.

⚡ Evidence
Consensus guidelines (Wilderness Medical Society 2014 update, subsequent institutional protocols) recommend tPA within 24 hours of thawing for severe frostbite extending to or beyond the proximal interphalangeal joints, with the strongest benefit when given within 12 hours — one study found each hour of treatment delay reduced tissue salvage by approximately 28%. A landmark retrospective study (Bruen 2007) found intra-arterial tPA reduced digit amputation rates to 10% in the optimally-timed treatment group versus 41% in a control/late-treatment group (p<0.05). Both intravenous (0.15 mg/kg bolus + infusion over 6 hours) and intra-arterial (0.25–1 mg/hr for up to 48 hours) protocols are described in the literature, with limb salvage rates exceeding 75% reported in some series; intra-arterial administration with angiographic guidance allows direct visualization of perfusion improvement during treatment.
Adjuncts
Iloprost (a prostacyclin analog/vasodilator) and hyperbaric oxygen therapy have both shown benefit as adjuncts to thrombolysis or as alternatives when thrombolysis is contraindicated — one study found HBO plus iloprost improved digit salvage compared to iloprost alone.
Grading
Frostbite is graded 1–4 by severity (analogous to burn depth grading), but accurate grading is often impossible on initial exam given the delayed-ischemia mechanism described above — imaging (technetium-99 bone scan, angiography, or emerging bedside fluorescence microangiography) can help establish tissue viability and guide the thrombolysis decision earlier than watching-and-waiting for demarcation would allow.

Trench Foot (Immersion Foot)

Trench foot (immersion foot) is mechanistically distinct from frostbite — it results from prolonged exposure to wet cold ABOVE freezing (classically 0–15°C / 32–59°F) over many hours to days, rather than from tissue freezing. The injury mechanism is prolonged vasoconstriction and tissue hypoxia from cold-water immersion, not ice crystal formation.

Presentation
Progresses through a cold, numb, pale/mottled ischemic phase during exposure, followed by a hyperemic phase after rewarming featuring intense pain, swelling, redness, and in severe cases blistering and tissue necrosis — the delayed hyperemic-phase pain is often more severe and prolonged than the numbness during the cold-exposure phase itself.
Management
Gradual rewarming (unlike frostbite's rapid rewarming protocol) with the affected extremity elevated, kept dry, and protected from further pressure/trauma; avoid walking on affected feet during the hyperemic phase given fragility of the tissue. No role for thrombolysis given the different (hypoxic, not thrombotic) injury mechanism. Historically a major cause of military casualties (the namesake trench warfare of WWI) and remains relevant in homeless populations, refugees, and prolonged wilderness wet-cold exposure.

🏕️ In the Field

  • Remove wet/constrictive footwear, socks, and jewelry as soon as freezing is suspected — constriction from swelling in unremoved gear worsens outcomes
  • Do NOT rub or massage the affected area, and do NOT rewarm if there is any realistic chance of refreezing before reaching definitive care — a freeze-thaw-refreeze cycle is worse than staying frozen through transport
  • If rewarming in the field is appropriate (no refreezing risk), use body heat (skin-to-skin, e.g., placing frostbitten fingers in an armpit) or warm (not hot) water if available — never use a direct heat source like a campfire or vehicle exhaust, which risks superimposed thermal burn on numb, insensate tissue
  • Protect the area with loose, dry, bulky padding/dressings for transport once thawed — do not compress or wrap tightly
  • Provide analgesia if available/trained to do so — rewarming is intensely painful and undertreated pain can complicate cooperation with subsequent care
  • For trench foot specifically: remove wet footwear/socks as early as possible, dry and gently rewarm gradually (not rapidly, unlike true frostbite), and elevate the extremity

🏥 In the ED

  • Rapid rewarming in a circulating water bath at 37–39°C until soft/pliable (~30 min) if not already done and no refreezing occurred en route
  • Aggressive analgesia (often opioids) plus ibuprofen for its dual analgesic/anti-thromboxane effect
  • Assess severity and time-since-thaw immediately to determine thrombolysis candidacy — the tPA window (within 24h of thaw, ideally within 12h) is a genuine time-critical decision point, not a routine admission order
  • Imaging (technetium-99 bone scan, angiography, or bedside fluorescence microangiography where available) to assess tissue viability and support the thrombolysis/no-thrombolysis decision when exam alone is equivocal
  • Tetanus prophylaxis, selective blister management (drain clear blisters, leave hemorrhagic blisters intact), topical aloe vera with dressing changes
  • Delay definitive amputation/debridement decisions until true demarcation is established over days to weeks — early exam dramatically underestimates eventual tissue viability

🛡️ Prevention

  • Proper footwear/gloves rated for the expected conditions, sized to allow layering without constricting circulation — boots that are too tight are a common preventable contributor
  • Moisture management — change wet socks/gloves promptly, use vapor-barrier liners in extreme cold, avoid cotton against skin
  • Avoid nicotine and alcohol before/during cold exposure — both impair peripheral circulation, compounding cold-induced vasoconstriction
  • Buddy checks for early pallor/numbness in exposed skin (nose, ears, fingers, toes) — early frostnip is reversible and easy to miss in oneself
  • Chemical hand/toe warmers and adequate insulation for extremities specifically, since distal extremities are disproportionately affected given surface-area-to-volume ratio and peripheral vasoconstriction
  • For trench foot specifically: scheduled boot/sock changes and foot-drying breaks during prolonged wet-cold exposure (military, outdoor labor, wilderness travel)

🌊 Drowning

The World Congress on Drowning (2002) established the standard definition, adopted by the WHO, ILCOR, and the Wilderness Medical Society: drowning is “the process of experiencing respiratory impairment due to submersion or immersion in liquid.” Modeled on the Utstein style used for cardiac arrest reporting, this definition allows for only three outcomes — death, morbidity, or no morbidity (survival without injury) — as a single continuous process rather than a collection of separate named syndromes.

⚠ Myth: “Dry / Secondary Drowning”
Claim: "“Dry drowning” and “secondary/delayed drowning” are real, distinct conditions where a person can seem fine after a water incident and then collapse or die hours to days later"
Reality: These terms — along with “near-drowning,” “wet drowning,” and “active/passive drowning” — are explicitly rejected by every major medical and safety organization (WHO, ILCOR, Wilderness Medical Society, International Lifesaving Federation, American Red Cross, CDC) as scientifically unsupported and a source of dangerous confusion. A comprehensive literature review found no evidence supporting a condition where a person drowns, appears completely fine, and then dies hours or days later with no interceding symptoms. What the terms are usually (mis)describing is either: (1) ordinary aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia from water aspiration, which does have a delayed symptomatic course but is a recognized, monitorable complication rather than a mysterious separate entity, or (2) cases where the person actually had ongoing respiratory symptoms after the water incident that were not recognized as significant until they progressed — not a symptom-free interval followed by sudden death.
Bottom line: Use “drowning” for the process (fatal or non-fatal, with or without morbidity) and describe the actual clinical picture (aspiration pneumonitis, hypoxic injury, etc.) rather than reaching for retired terminology — both for accurate medical communication and because the retired terms actively contribute to public panic/misunderstanding about symptom-free children needing to be rushed to the ED after any water incident.

Cervical Spine Immobilization

Old teaching: Routine cervical spine immobilization for all drowning/submersion patients, out of general caution about occult trauma.

Current Evidence
Retrospective studies find the incidence of cervical spine injury in drowning patients is low, cited in the range of 0.5–5%, and that the injuries that do occur are strongly associated with a specific concerning mechanism — diving from a height, being found in shallow water with signs of impact, boating/watercraft collision, or a clear observed traumatic mechanism — rather than occurring in drowning generically. The 2024 Wilderness Medical Society drowning guideline update, incorporating this data along with the broader 2019 WMS spinal protection guideline reassessment, supports a targeted rather than routine immobilization approach.
⚡ Clinical Bottom Line
Reserve cervical spine immobilization for drowning patients with an actual history or exam findings suggestive of trauma (diving mechanism, visible injury, neurologic deficit, altered mentation without a clear hypoxic explanation) — do not apply it routinely to every submersion patient by default, since unnecessary immobilization can meaningfully delay airway management and effective CPR in a population where oxygenation is the single most time-critical intervention.
Resuscitation Notes
  • Unlike primary cardiac arrest, drowning arrest is a primary respiratory event — ventilation matters disproportionately, which is why the European Resuscitation Council recommends 5 initial rescue breaths (rather than the standard 2) before starting compressions in drowning-specific resuscitation, reflecting that initial ventilations are often less effective in a submersion victim (airway compromise, laryngospasm, aspirated fluid) and more of them are needed to establish effective oxygenation
  • The single best predictor of neurologic outcome is the patient's neurologic status over the first 24 hours after the event — early, aggressive resuscitation efforts are warranted even in seemingly severe presentations, since drowning-associated hypothermia (common in cold-water submersion) can be neuroprotective similarly to hypothermic cardiac arrest generally
  • Mechanical ventilation strategy for drowning-associated lung injury mirrors standard ARDS/lung-protective ventilation — low tidal volume (~6 mL/kg predicted body weight), plateau pressure <30 cmH2O — given the aspiration-pneumonitis-driven lung injury pattern
  • Neuromuscular blockade should generally be avoided if possible (unless required for refractory respiratory failure) specifically to preserve the ability to perform serial neurologic examinations, which are central to prognostication and monitoring in this population
Epidemiology
Drowning is the leading cause of accidental death in children under 5 in locations with easy pool/beach access, and the second leading cause of unintentional injury death (after motor vehicle crashes) in children 5–14. More than 90% of drowning fatalities occur in low- and middle-income countries. Location patterns shift with age — toddlers most often drown in bathtubs and residential pools, while adolescents and adults more often drown in natural bodies of water, frequently with concurrent risk-taking behavior or alcohol involvement.

🏕️ In the Field

  • Rescuer safety first — do not become a second victim; use reach/throw/row/go hierarchy (reach with an object, throw a flotation device, row a boat to the victim, and only go in yourself as a last resort with flotation support) rather than an unequipped swimming rescue
  • Begin rescue breaths as soon as the victim's airway can be supported, even before fully out of the water if the rescuer is trained and it's safe to do so — ventilation is the single highest-yield early intervention given the primary-respiratory nature of drowning arrest
  • Give 5 initial rescue breaths (not the standard 2) before starting compressions if no breathing/pulse — initial ventilations are often less effective in a submersion victim due to airway compromise and aspirated fluid
  • Do NOT apply routine cervical spine immobilization unless there is an actual history or exam finding suggesting trauma (diving mechanism, shallow water impact, boating collision) — routine immobilization in a generic submersion delays effective airway management and CPR for no proven benefit
  • Remove wet clothing and insulate against hypothermia once out of the water — cold-water submersion victims are frequently also hypothermic, which can be neuroprotective but also complicates resuscitation
  • Transport ALL drowning victims for evaluation even if they appear to have fully recovered on scene — aspiration pneumonitis and delayed respiratory decompensation are real, monitorable complications that warrant observation, even though "dry/secondary drowning" as a distinct mystery syndrome is not real

🏥 In the ED

  • Continuous pulse oximetry and reassessment for evolving respiratory symptoms over several hours — the observation period is the actual clinical tool for the delayed-symptom concern that “secondary drowning” folklore is (inaccurately) trying to describe
  • Chest imaging and ABG/VBG for any patient with respiratory symptoms, hypoxia, or a concerning submersion history
  • Lung-protective ventilation strategy (~6 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau pressure <30 cmH2O) if mechanical ventilation is required, mirroring standard ARDS management given the aspiration-driven lung injury pattern
  • Avoid neuromuscular blockade where possible to preserve the ability to perform serial neurologic exams, which are central to prognostication
  • Targeted cervical spine imaging/immobilization only if the history or exam actually suggests trauma — do not order routine imaging on every submersion patient by default
  • Admit for observation any patient with hypoxia, abnormal lung exam/imaging, or altered mentation; a truly asymptomatic patient with a normal exam and several hours of unremarkable observation can generally be safely discharged — confirm against your institution's specific observation-duration protocol

🛡️ Prevention

  • Formal swim lessons for children and non-swimmers — one of the most consistently evidence-supported drowning-prevention interventions
  • Four-sided pool fencing with self-closing/self-latching gates, isolating the pool from the house and yard — estimated to prevent a large share of pediatric drowning deaths
  • Constant, dedicated adult supervision ("water watcher") during any child water exposure — not just general proximity, since drowning is frequently silent and fast
  • US Coast Guard-approved life jackets for boating and open-water activity, worn (not just available) — especially for weak/non-swimmers and young children
  • Avoid alcohol before/during swimming, boating, or supervising children near water
  • CPR training for parents, caregivers, and pool owners — bystander CPR started immediately, before EMS arrival, measurably improves outcomes
  • Know local water conditions before diving — never dive into unknown-depth or unfamiliar water given the trauma risk discussed above

⚡ Electrical Injury & Lightning Strikes

Conventional Electrical Injury

Conventional electrical injury (household current, industrial/high-voltage contact) causes tissue damage primarily through Joule heating — resistive heat generation proportional to current squared, resistance, and time of contact — as current travels along a path of least resistance through the body, typically following blood vessels and nerves (lower resistance) rather than skin/bone (higher resistance). This produces a genuine current PATHWAY through deep tissue, with damage occurring along that entire path, not just at visible entry/exit wound sites — the visible skin injury dramatically underestimates the extent of deep tissue damage, which is why electrical injury is a classic cause of compartment syndrome and rhabdomyolysis that can be missed if assessment stops at the skin.

AC vs. DC
Alternating current (AC, standard household/industrial power) is generally more dangerous than DC at equivalent voltage because it can cause tetanic muscle contraction — if the current path crosses the hand, the victim's own grip reflex can cause them to be unable to release the source, prolonging contact time and total energy delivered. AC at certain frequencies (particularly around 50–60 Hz, the standard household frequency) is also more likely to induce ventricular fibrillation than DC at equivalent current.
Cardiac Risk
Cardiac arrhythmia is the most immediately life-threatening complication of conventional electrical injury — current traversing the chest (hand-to-hand or hand-to-foot pathways crossing the heart) carries the highest risk of inducing ventricular fibrillation. All patients with a concerning current pathway, loss of consciousness, or abnormal initial ECG warrant cardiac monitoring.
Management Principles
  • Scene safety first — ensure the patient is no longer in contact with the electrical source and the scene is confirmed safe before approaching, a step that differs critically from lightning injury (see below)
  • Assess and monitor for arrhythmia — 12-lead ECG and cardiac monitoring, particularly with any hand-to-hand or hand-to-foot current pathway
  • High index of suspicion for deep tissue injury disproportionate to visible skin findings — assess for compartment syndrome (especially in high-voltage injury), obtain CK to screen for rhabdomyolysis, and monitor renal function/urine output
  • Aggressive fluid resuscitation when rhabdomyolysis is present, targeting adequate urine output to protect renal function, similar to crush injury/burn resuscitation principles
  • Full trauma survey — tetanic contractions and falls associated with electrical contact frequently cause concurrent traumatic injury (fractures, particularly from the fall or the contraction itself) that can be overlooked when attention is focused on the electrical injury

🏕️ In the Field

  • Scene safety is the absolute first step — confirm the power source is off/the patient is no longer in contact before anyone touches them; use a non-conductive object to separate the patient from the source if the circuit cannot be immediately de-energized and delay is dangerous
  • Once safe to approach, assess ABCs and initiate CPR if indicated — cardiac arrest from conventional electrical injury is generally VF-mechanism, so standard ACLS/defibrillation algorithms apply (unlike lightning, see below)
  • Immobilize the spine if a fall from height or violent tetanic contraction raises concern for traumatic injury
  • Cover burns with dry, clean/sterile dressings; do not apply ice or ointments in the field
  • Anticipate that visible burns underrepresent internal injury — treat any high-voltage contact as a potential multi-system trauma, not just a burn, even with reassuring-looking skin findings

🏥 In the ED

  • 12-lead ECG and cardiac monitoring for any concerning current pathway, loss of consciousness, or abnormal initial rhythm
  • CK and urine myoglobin screening for rhabdomyolysis; aggressive IV fluid resuscitation targeting adequate urine output if positive
  • Serial exams for compartment syndrome, particularly in high-voltage injury with a limb in the current pathway — have a low threshold for compartment pressure measurement and surgical consultation
  • Renal function monitoring given the combined risk of rhabdomyolysis and, in more severe cases, direct tissue injury
  • Full trauma evaluation for concurrent injury from falls/tetanic contraction
  • Burn wound care per standard burn protocols, with surgical/burn center referral for significant full-thickness or high-voltage contact-point injuries

🛡️ Prevention

  • Lockout/tagout procedures for anyone working on electrical systems — the standard industrial safeguard against inadvertent re-energization during maintenance
  • GFCI (ground-fault circuit interrupter) outlets in wet-prone areas (kitchens, bathrooms, outdoors) — detect current leakage and cut power fast enough to prevent lethal shock
  • Maintain safe clearance distances from overhead power lines during any work involving ladders, cranes, or long equipment
  • Appropriate PPE (insulated gloves, tools) for anyone working on or near live electrical systems
  • Child-proofing outlets in homes with young children

Lightning Strikes — A Different Mechanism Entirely

Lightning injury is mechanistically distinct from conventional electrical injury despite both being “electrical” in casual description — lightning delivers an extremely brief (microseconds to milliseconds) but massive DC countershock (currents up to 200,000+ amps, versus household current in single-digit to low-double-digit amps), and critically, most of that current travels OVER the body's external surface (a phenomenon called 'flashover') rather than through deep tissue, because the contact time is too brief for current to penetrate. This is why lightning strike survivors, unlike conventional electrical injury victims, typically have only superficial burns (roughly 90% of lightning burns are superficial) and relatively little deep tissue/compartment syndrome risk despite the vastly larger current involved — a genuinely counterintuitive point worth teaching explicitly.

⚡ Cardiac Arrest Mechanism
The massive, brief DC countershock acts like a whole-body defibrillation-in-reverse: it depolarizes the entire myocardium simultaneously, typically producing asystole as the primary arrest rhythm (rather than ventricular fibrillation, the primary mechanism in conventional electrical injury). The heart's own inherent automaticity can spontaneously restart after this — BUT the medullary respiratory center, also stunned by the strike, typically takes longer to recover than cardiac automaticity does. If ventilation isn't supported during that gap, the patient develops a SECONDARY hypoxic cardiac arrest from respiratory failure, even though their heart would have recovered on its own — this is the physiologic basis for why aggressive ventilatory support (not just chest compressions) is uniquely critical in lightning resuscitation.
Keraunoparalysis
A distinctive, lightning-specific finding: transient paralysis of the limbs (lower extremities more than upper), typically accompanied by mottled/blue, cold, pulseless skin — caused by autonomic nervous system-mediated vasospasm rather than any structural nerve or spinal cord injury, and generally NOT a true neurologic paralysis despite the alarming presentation. Usually resolves spontaneously within a few hours (occasionally up to 24 hours). Important pitfall: keraunoparalysis can closely mimic spinal cord injury or acute limb ischemia — if pallor/pulselessness persists beyond the expected resolution window, reconsider compartment syndrome or genuine vascular injury rather than assuming ongoing keraunoparalysis.
Lichtenberg Figures
Pathognomonic (diagnostic) fern-like or feathering branched patterns on the skin, caused by electron showering across the skin surface producing red blood cell extravasation from superficial dermal vessels — NOT a true burn histologically (no significant tissue damage), and transient, typically fading within hours and rarely persisting beyond 24 hours. Their presence essentially confirms a lightning mechanism when the history is uncertain, but their absence does not rule out a lightning strike.

Reverse Triage

Figure — Standard vs. lightning mass-casualty triage
⚡ Principle
In a lightning mass-casualty incident (e.g., a strike affecting a group), triage is deliberately inverted from standard trauma triage: patients in cardiac and/or respiratory arrest are treated FIRST, not last or 'expectant' as they typically would be under standard field triage systems.
Rationale
This is a direct consequence of the pathophysiology above — because cardiac automaticity often recovers spontaneously after a lightning-induced asystolic arrest, and the primary threat to these patients is a preventable secondary hypoxic arrest from unsupported ventilation during the respiratory-center-recovery gap, aggressive resuscitation of the apparently 'dead' patients is disproportionately likely to succeed compared to standard cardiac arrest resuscitation. Patients who are conscious, ambulatory, or breathing on their own after a lightning strike are, by contrast, very likely to survive even without immediate intervention — the walking wounded genuinely can wait.
Evidence
This approach is supported by case reports of successful resuscitation using this triage logic (including a documented case from Sequoia and Kings Canyon National Park) and is now standard teaching in wilderness medicine — a clean, high-yield reversal of the 'normal' triage rule worth explicitly flagging as such.
Other Findings
  • Tympanic membrane rupture in roughly half of lightning strike victims — the most common otologic injury; suspect skull base fracture if otorrhea is present
  • Cataracts — a recognized delayed/late complication, not an acute finding
  • Fixed and dilated pupils can occur in lightning strike survivors WITHOUT irreversible brain injury (from the same autonomic stunning mechanism as keraunoparalysis) — explicitly should NOT be used alone to prognosticate or guide a decision to withhold resuscitation
  • Nonspecific ECG changes (QT prolongation, T-wave inversion, ST changes) are common; cardiac biomarkers do not reliably correlate with injury severity
  • Rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome can still occur (less commonly than conventional electrical injury) — obtain CK and monitor
  • Any lightning strike during pregnancy requires fetal monitoring and comprehensive obstetric evaluation given fetal vulnerability to the current pathway and maternal hemodynamic disturbance

🏕️ In the Field

  • Scene safety — the 30-30 rule: if thunder follows lightning by less than 30 seconds, the storm is close enough to strike again; seek substantial shelter and wait at least 30 minutes after the last thunder before resuming outdoor activity or approaching a victim in an exposed area if it can be safely delayed
  • Apply reverse triage in a multi-victim strike — go to apparently dead (pulseless/apneic) victims FIRST, not last, since these are the patients most likely to be salvageable with prompt ventilation and CPR
  • Aggressive ventilatory support is uniquely critical — support breathing even in a patient who regains a pulse, since the respiratory center recovers slower than cardiac automaticity and unsupported ventilation risks a preventable secondary hypoxic arrest
  • Do not use fixed/dilated pupils to withhold resuscitation — this finding can reflect autonomic stunning rather than irreversible brain injury in lightning strike victims specifically
  • Assess for and address keraunoparalysis (transient limb paralysis/pulselessness) without assuming permanent injury, while still keeping compartment syndrome on the differential if it doesn't resolve as expected
  • Full trauma assessment — the explosive force and violent muscle contraction of a strike can cause falls, blunt trauma, and tympanic membrane rupture

🏥 In the ED

  • Cardiac monitoring and serial ECGs — watch for the nonspecific QT prolongation/T-wave/ST changes described above; cardiac biomarkers do not reliably correlate with injury severity so trend clinically rather than anchoring on a single troponin
  • CK and renal function monitoring for rhabdomyolysis, though generally less pronounced than in conventional high-voltage electrical injury
  • Full neurologic exam and reassessment as keraunoparalysis/autonomic stunning resolves; CT head for any persistent or worsening deficit to evaluate for structural injury (intracranial hemorrhage, especially basal ganglia/brainstem)
  • Ophthalmologic and otologic evaluation — assess for tympanic membrane rupture acutely and counsel on delayed cataract risk
  • Burn care for the (usually superficial) skin findings; Lichtenberg figures themselves need no treatment and should be documented/photographed since they resolve within hours to a day
  • Obstetric evaluation and fetal monitoring for any pregnant patient

🛡️ Prevention

  • The 30-30 rule for outdoor activity planning around thunderstorms (see Field Care)
  • Avoid open fields, isolated tall trees, elevated terrain, and being the tallest object in an area during a storm
  • Avoid water (swimming, boating) and contact with metal objects/fencing during thunderstorm activity
  • Seek substantial shelter (a fully enclosed building or hard-topped vehicle) rather than a partial shelter (picnic pavilion, tent, golf cart) — partial shelters do not reliably protect against lightning
  • If caught in the open with no shelter available, avoid open fields and high ground, and minimize contact with the ground (the “lightning crouch” is a historically taught last-resort position, though evidence for its effectiveness is limited)
  • Postpone outdoor group activities (sports, camps, events) proactively when thunderstorms are forecast rather than waiting for visible lightning

🐍 Envenomation

US Pit Vipers

Species: Copperheads, cottonmouths/water moccasins, and rattlesnakes (genus Crotalus and Agkistrodon) account for roughly 98% of venomous snakebites in the United States — approximately 7,000 documented possible snake envenomation cases annually per National Poison Data System reporting.

Venom effects: Crotalid venom is predominantly cytotoxic and hemotoxic — local tissue destruction (pain, swelling, ecchymosis, potential necrosis) plus a coagulopathy from venom components that consume fibrinogen and interfere with platelet function, producing a genuine bleeding risk distinct from the local tissue injury.

AntivenomProfile
CroFab (Crotalidae Polyvalent Immune Fab, ovine)FDA-approved 2000, derived from sheep immunized against four US pit vipers (cottonmouth, Mojave rattlesnake, Eastern and Western diamondback). Fab fragments only (Fc portion removed), which reduces hypersensitivity risk. Comparative registry data (North American Snakebite Registry) found CroFab required fewer total vials (median 10) and fewer repeat administrations to achieve control than Anavip for rattlesnake envenomation specifically.
Anavip (Crotalidae Immune F(ab')2, equine)FDA-approved 2018 (rattlesnake), expanded 2021 (copperhead/cottonmouth). F(ab')2 fragments (larger than Fab), which confers a longer half-life — theoretically advantageous for preventing recurrent coagulopathy after initial control, though registry data found a higher median vial requirement (18) and more frequent need for repeat dosing (69% of Anavip-only patients required more than one administration event, versus 39% of CroFab-only patients) in practice.
⚡ Clinical Pearls
  • Species identification is NOT required to treat appropriately — both antivenoms are FDA-approved for all North American pit viper envenomations, and treatment is guided by clinical signs/symptoms and coagulation studies, not by confirming exactly which pit viper species caused the bite
  • Do not delay transport/treatment attempting to identify, capture, or kill the snake for identification — a photo taken quickly and safely is useful for epidemiologic purposes but should never delay care
  • Compartment syndrome following pit viper envenomation is exceptionally rare despite dramatic swelling — most apparent compartment-syndrome-like presentations respond to MORE antivenom, not fasciotomy; surgical consultation should generally follow, not precede, adequate antivenom dosing
  • Antivenom is indicated for progressive local tissue effects, hematologic venom effects (coagulopathy), systemic signs, or swelling involving the hands/airway — not every bite requires antivenom, but the threshold for treating progressive findings should be low
  • Monitor coagulation studies at 6–12 hours after initial control, prior to discharge, and at two further outpatient intervals after discharge — recurrent coagulopathy after apparent initial resolution is a recognized phenomenon, particularly relevant to Anavip's longer-half-life rationale above

Coral Snake

The only native elapid (neurotoxic-venom family, same family as cobras/mambas) in North America. Venom is predominantly neurotoxic rather than the cytotoxic/hemotoxic pattern of pit vipers — a fundamentally different clinical picture: minimal local tissue reaction, but risk of progressive neuromuscular weakness, including potential respiratory failure, that can be delayed in onset by several hours after the bite.

⚠ Antivenom Supply Crisis
There is a genuine, ongoing US supply crisis for North American Coral Snake Antivenin (Micrurus fulvius, equine origin) — manufactured by Wyeth (now Pfizer), production ceased years ago, and the FDA has been repeatedly extending the expiration date on the last remaining lots (most recently extended through June 2025 at time of last verification) because there is NO alternative product currently licensed in the US for coral snake envenomation. This is a real and clinically consequential supply constraint, not a theoretical concern — verify current antivenom availability with your regional poison control center, as the situation continues to evolve.
Treatment Approach
Historically, ALL confirmed or presumed coral snake bites were treated with empiric antivenom regardless of symptoms, given the risk of delayed-onset respiratory failure and the historical belief that antivenom is far less effective once neurotoxic symptoms are established. A 2025 retrospective comparison of empiric antivenom versus expectant (symptom-triggered) treatment for Eastern coral snakebites has begun directly examining this practice given the antivenom scarcity — an evolving, actively-studied area rather than a settled question; consult your regional poison control center for current recommendations given both the evidence uncertainty and the supply situation.
Clinical Pearls
  • Neurotoxic symptom onset can be delayed 12+ hours after a coral snake bite — a patient with an unremarkable initial exam cannot be safely discharged early the way a clearly non-envenomated pit viper bite sometimes can; extended observation is standard
  • “Red touches yellow, kill a fellow; red touches black, friend of Jack” — the classic mnemonic for distinguishing the venomous coral snake from non-venomous mimics (e.g., scarlet kingsnake) by band pattern, valid for North American species specifically and NOT a reliable rule for coral snake species elsewhere in the Americas

US Spiders

Black Widow (Latrodectus species)

Mechanism: Venom contains alpha-latrotoxin, which causes massive, sustained presynaptic neurotransmitter release (acetylcholine and norepinephrine) at the neuromuscular junction and autonomic synapses — producing the syndrome of latrodectism.

Presentation: Severe muscle pain and rigidity (classically abdominal rigidity that can mimic a surgical abdomen), diaphoresis, hypertension, tachycardia, and generalized systemic symptoms — disproportionate systemic illness relative to the often minimal-appearing local bite site.

Treatment: Supportive care (analgesia, benzodiazepines for muscle spasm/rigidity) is first-line and sufficient for most cases; antivenom (equine-derived Latrodectus antivenom) is reserved for severe, refractory symptoms given real anaphylaxis/serum sickness risk from the equine-derived product — a genuine risk-benefit tradeoff rather than a reflexive treatment.

Brown Recluse (Loxosceles reclusa)

Mechanism: Venom contains sphingomyelinase D, which damages cell membranes and can trigger local dermonecrosis and, rarely, a systemic hemolytic/coagulopathic reaction (loxoscelism).

Presentation: Classically a slowly-enlarging necrotic ulcer with a pale, ischemic center (the 'red, white, and blue sign') developing over days — though most brown recluse bites cause only a self-limited local reaction without significant necrosis.

Treatment: Overdiagnosis is a major, well-recognized clinical problem — brown recluse spiders have a genuinely limited native geographic range (central/southern US), and a large proportion of presumed 'brown recluse bites' diagnosed outside that range (or even within it) are actually other conditions entirely, most commonly community-acquired MRSA skin/soft tissue infection. No antivenom is available in the US; management is supportive, with surgical debridement reserved for clearly demarcated necrotic tissue rather than early/aggressive excision.

Marine Envenomation

Box Jellyfish / Irukandji Syndrome

Mechanism: Box jellyfish (Chironex fleckeri and related species, primarily Indo-Pacific/Australian waters) venom causes direct cardiotoxicity and severe local pain via nematocyst-injected toxins. Irukandji syndrome, from a distinct group of smaller box jellyfish species, involves a delayed (20–30 minute) onset of severe systemic symptoms out of proportion to a frequently trivial-seeming sting.

Presentation: Chironex: severe immediate pain, linear whip-like skin lesions, and in severe cases cardiovascular collapse/cardiac arrest within minutes. Irukandji syndrome: severe muscle cramping, back/abdominal pain, hypertension, and a distinctive sense of impending doom, developing after a delay following a sting that may have seemed minor.

Treatment: Vinegar irrigation to inactivate undischarged nematocysts (specifically effective for Chironex-family box jellyfish — notably NOT universally appropriate for all jellyfish species, see practical note below), hot water immersion (see below) for pain, antivenom exists for Chironex fleckeri specifically in endemic regions, and aggressive supportive/cardiovascular care for severe cases including Irukandji syndrome.

Stingray

Mechanism: Puncture injury from a barbed tail spine delivers a heat-labile protein venom causing intense local pain, plus direct mechanical tissue trauma from the barb itself.

Presentation: Severe, immediate local pain out of proportion to the visible wound; retained barb fragments are common and should be actively sought.

Treatment: Hot water immersion (as hot as tolerable, typically 43–45°C / 110–113°F) for 30–90 minutes is first-line and highly effective, since the venom's heat-labile proteins are denatured by heat — the same principle underlies hot-water treatment for several other marine envenomations (lionfish, scorpionfish, catfish) with heat-labile venom components. Wound exploration for retained barb material and standard wound care/tetanus prophylaxis.

Lionfish / Scorpionfish

Mechanism: Venomous dorsal, pelvic, and anal spines deliver a heat-labile protein venom on puncture — mechanistically similar to stingray envenomation.

Presentation: Immediate severe local pain, edema; systemic symptoms (nausea, diaphoresis) possible with more significant envenomation.

Treatment: Hot water immersion, same rationale and protocol as stingray envenomation given the shared heat-labile venom mechanism; wound care and tetanus prophylaxis.

General Field, ED & Prevention Principles — All Envenomations

🏕️ In the Field

  • Move away from the animal — do not attempt to catch, kill, or handle it for identification; a photo from a safe distance is useful but never worth a second bite/sting
  • Snakebite: immobilize the bitten extremity at approximately heart level (not elevated, not dependent), remove rings/watches/constrictive items before swelling progresses, and keep the patient as calm and still as possible to slow systemic venom spread
  • Do NOT use outdated field measures for snakebite — no tourniquets, no incision-and-suction, no ice application; all have been shown to worsen outcomes (tourniquets and ice worsen local tissue injury, incision/suction devices remove negligible venom while adding wound complications) and delay definitive care
  • Mark the leading edge of swelling/erythema with a pen and note the time — genuinely useful, low-tech data for the receiving ED to gauge progression
  • Marine puncture envenomation (stingray, lionfish, scorpionfish): immerse the affected area in water as hot as tolerable (roughly 43–45°C / 110–113°F) for 30–90 minutes as soon as possible — denatures the heat-labile venom proteins and is highly effective for pain
  • Box jellyfish—family stings: irrigate generously with vinegar to inactivate undischarged nematocysts before any further manipulation of the sting site; do NOT rinse with fresh water or rub the area, both of which can trigger additional nematocyst discharge
  • Spider bites (widow/recluse): clean the wound, apply a cold pack (not heat) for comfort, and transport for evaluation — no field antivenom exists for either

🏥 In the ED

  • Pit viper: assess for progressive local findings, coagulopathy, and systemic signs to determine antivenom need; if indicated, initiate CroFab or Anavip per weight-based/symptom-triggered dosing protocol and reassess serially; baseline and serial coagulation studies (6–12h post-control, pre-discharge, and after discharge) given recurrence risk
  • Coral snake: extended observation (often 24h) even with an initially unremarkable exam given delayed neurotoxic onset; empiric vs. expectant antivenom decision should follow current regional poison control guidance given the ongoing supply constraint
  • Black widow: supportive care first — analgesia and benzodiazepines for muscle rigidity/spasm; reserve antivenom for severe/refractory cases given equine-product anaphylaxis/serum sickness risk
  • Brown recluse: supportive wound care; actively consider and rule out alternative diagnoses (especially MRSA skin/soft tissue infection) before anchoring on a presumed spider bite, particularly outside the species' native range
  • Marine envenomation: continue hot water immersion for pain, explore/irrigate puncture wounds for retained barb fragments (stingray), tetanus prophylaxis, and consider region-specific antivenom (e.g., Chironex fleckeri antivenom) where available and indicated
  • All envenomations: tetanus status update, wound care/monitoring for secondary infection, and analgesia titrated to the often-severe pain these injuries cause

🛡️ Prevention

  • Snake: wear closed-toe boots and long pants in tall grass/brush/rocky terrain; use a flashlight and watch foot/hand placement at night or when stepping over logs/rocks; never reach blindly into holes, dense brush, or under rocks/debris
  • Spider: shake out shoes, gloves, and clothing left outdoors or in storage before use; use caution moving stored boxes/firewood/debris where widow and recluse spiders commonly nest
  • Marine: use the “stingray shuffle” (dragging feet rather than stepping) when wading in sandy coastal shallows to warn off buried stingrays rather than stepping directly on one; avoid touching unfamiliar marine life; wear protective footwear on reefs
  • Box jellyfish/marine stinger season: heed local beach warning flags/signage in endemic regions and consider protective swimwear (stinger suits) where advised
  • Travel medicine counseling before wilderness/safari/rural travel to regions with medically significant elapids (mambas, cobras) or vipers (saw-scaled viper) — know the location of the nearest facility with antivenom before heading into remote areas
  • General: never handle any wild snake or spider, venomous or not, to avoid misidentification-driven bites; supervise children closely in known-endemic outdoor areas

🏔️ High-Altitude Illness

All acute altitude illness syndromes stem from the same root cause — hypobaric hypoxia at elevation outpacing the rate of physiologic acclimatization. AMS and HACE are thought to represent a single continuum of cerebral pathology (mild to severe cerebral edema/dysfunction from hypoxia-driven cerebral vasodilation and increased capillary permeability), while HAPE is mechanistically distinct: an exaggerated, uneven hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV) response causes regional pulmonary capillary overperfusion and pressure-driven capillary leak, producing a fundamentally hydrostatic/permeability pulmonary edema rather than a cardiac-failure-driven one.

AMS & HACE

AMS: Acute Mountain Sickness is diagnosed clinically using the Lake Louise Symptom Score (self-reported headache plus GI symptoms, fatigue, dizziness, and sleep disturbance) in the setting of recent ascent — headache is required for diagnosis, distinguishing it from simple fatigue after exertion at altitude.

HACE: High Altitude Cerebral Edema is considered the severe end of the AMS/cerebral-edema continuum, defined by the presence of ataxia and/or altered mental status in someone with AMS symptoms (or, less commonly, in someone with HAPE) after ascent — a genuine medical emergency requiring immediate descent.

⚡ Treatment
Dexamethasone is highly effective for both AMS and HACE treatment and is considered the primary HACE treatment specifically — note that it treats symptoms without facilitating acclimatization, so further ascent should be delayed until the patient is asymptomatic OFF the medication, not just improved while on it. Acetazolamide can be used as an adjunct to dexamethasone in HACE but is not a substitute. Descent (or, where unavailable, supplemental oxygen or a portable hyperbaric chamber) remains the definitive treatment for HACE regardless of pharmacologic therapy.
Prevention
Acetazolamide is the standard chemoprophylaxis for AMS/HACE in moderate-to-high-risk ascent profiles (e.g., sleeping elevation ≥2800m on day one, or ascent rate ≥500m/day above 3000m per the 2024 WMS risk stratification model) — it accelerates the renal compensation for the respiratory alkalosis of hyperventilation-driven acclimatization, effectively speeding up a process the body would eventually do on its own.

HAPE

High Altitude Pulmonary Edema — progressive dyspnea, cough, and reduced exercise tolerance following ascent, distinguished from a simple upper respiratory infection by the altitude-and-ascent-rate context and, when auscultated, crackles reflecting non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

⚡ Treatment
Immediate descent is the single most effective HAPE treatment when feasible. Supplemental oxygen and portable hyperbaric therapy (Gamow bag or similar) are effective alternatives when descent is impossible or must be delayed. Nifedipine (a pulmonary vasodilator, targeting the exaggerated HPV mechanism directly) is the preferred pharmacologic treatment when descent/oxygen/hyperbaric options are all unavailable; tadalafil or sildenafil (PDE5 inhibitors, same pulmonary-vasodilator rationale) can be used when nifedipine specifically is unavailable. CPAP can be considered as an adjunct when oxygen/vasodilators alone are insufficient. Notably, per the 2024 WMS guideline, there is NO established role for beta-agonists, diuretics, acetazolamide, or dexamethasone in HAPE treatment specifically — dexamethasone should only be added if there is concern for concurrent HACE, not for HAPE alone.
Prevention
Nifedipine is the preferred prophylactic agent for individuals with a history of HAPE (particularly multiple prior episodes), started the day before ascent and continued until descent begins. Tadalafil is an accepted alternative. Dexamethasone can be used for HAPE prevention specifically in susceptible individuals who cannot use nifedipine/tadalafil, though its mechanism of benefit for HAPE specifically (as opposed to AMS/HACE) is not well understood and evidence is comparatively limited. Acetazolamide has NOT been shown effective for primary HAPE prevention despite blunting hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction in some studies — the reduction seen in trials has not reached statistical significance and is smaller than the effect size of nifedipine/tadalafil/dexamethasone; its one specific supported use is prevention of re-entry HAPE (a distinct phenomenon in people who live at high altitude, descend, then rapidly return).
Guideline Reference
The 2024 Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guideline update is the current reference standard for altitude illness prevention, diagnosis, and treatment — the recommendations above reflect that update. Gradual ascent remains the primary prevention strategy for all three syndromes; pharmacologic prophylaxis is an adjunct for higher-risk profiles or individuals with a prior history, not a substitute for sensible ascent pacing.

🏕️ In the Field

  • Stop ascending at the first sign of any altitude illness symptom — do not push through headache/nausea to reach a planned camp or summit
  • Descend at the first sign of ataxia, altered mental status, or worsening symptoms despite rest — descent is the single most effective field intervention for all three syndromes and should never be delayed while trying medications first if HACE or HAPE is suspected
  • Even a modest descent (300–1000m) can produce significant symptomatic improvement — you don't necessarily need to return all the way to the trailhead
  • Supplemental oxygen or a portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag) if available and descent is delayed/impossible (e.g., weather, terrain, nightfall) — effective temporizing measures while arranging actual descent or evacuation
  • For AMS: rest at the current altitude (no further ascent) plus symptomatic treatment (analgesics for headache, antiemetics for nausea) is reasonable if symptoms are mild and there is no ataxia/altered mentation
  • Never leave a symptomatic person alone or send them down alone if any neurologic symptoms are present — HACE can progress to incapacitation

🏥 In the ED

  • Dexamethasone for AMS/HACE per weight-based dosing; supplemental oxygen titrated to adequate saturation
  • For HAPE: oxygen supplementation as first-line, nifedipine or PDE5 inhibitors (tadalafil/sildenafil) if oxygen/descent are inadequate, and CPAP as an adjunct in refractory cases
  • Chest imaging for suspected HAPE to characterize the pattern of edema and rule out alternative diagnoses (pneumonia, cardiogenic edema)
  • ICU-level monitoring for severe HACE (persistent altered mentation) or HAPE (significant hypoxia, respiratory distress)
  • Reinforce that dexamethasone treats symptoms without facilitating acclimatization — counsel against further ascent until asymptomatic OFF the medication, and coordinate safe descent/evacuation planning as part of ED disposition for wilderness-origin patients
  • Rule out overlapping or alternative diagnoses in an altitude traveler with neurologic or respiratory symptoms (e.g., stroke, pneumonia, PE) rather than anchoring exclusively on altitude illness

🛡️ Prevention

  • Gradual ascent remains the primary prevention strategy for all three syndromes — the 2024 WMS risk model flags sleeping elevation ≥2800m on day one or ascent rate ≥500m/day above 3000m as moderate-to-high risk warranting extra caution or chemoprophylaxis
  • Acetazolamide for AMS/HACE chemoprophylaxis in moderate-high risk ascent profiles or those with a prior history
  • Nifedipine (or tadalafil) for HAPE-specific chemoprophylaxis in individuals with a history of prior HAPE episodes, started the day before ascent
  • Build rest/acclimatization days into the itinerary rather than a straight ascent profile, particularly above 3000m
  • “Climb high, sleep low” — a classic mountaineering strategy of ascending to a higher elevation during the day and returning to a lower elevation to sleep, which aids acclimatization
  • Avoid alcohol and sedating medications at altitude, which can blunt the respiratory drive needed for acclimatization and mask early symptoms

🤽 Diving & Dysbarism

Diving-related injury (dysbarism) encompasses several distinct mechanisms unified by the physics of pressure change with depth — understanding which mechanism is in play (dissolved gas coming out of solution vs. trapped gas expanding) determines both the clinical picture and the urgency of treatment.

Decompression Sickness

During a dive, inert gas (nitrogen, in standard compressed-air diving) dissolves into blood and tissue under increased ambient pressure. If ascent is too rapid relative to the dive profile, dissolved nitrogen comes out of solution as gas bubbles within blood and tissue — analogous to opening a shaken soda bottle — rather than being safely off-gassed through the lungs during a controlled ascent.

Type I (Mild)
Type I (“the bends”, mild): joint and musculoskeletal pain (classically the shoulders/elbows), skin changes (cutis marmorata, itching), and lymphatic involvement — uncomfortable but not immediately life-threatening.
Type II (Severe)
Type II (severe): neurologic involvement (spinal cord DCS causing paresis/paralysis, cerebral DCS causing altered mentation/stroke-like deficits), cardiopulmonary involvement (“the chokes” — dyspnea, chest pain, cough from pulmonary vascular gas bubble load), or inner ear DCS (vertigo, hearing loss) — a genuine emergency requiring urgent recompression.
⚡ Treatment
100% oxygen immediately (accelerates nitrogen off-gassing via a favorable diffusion gradient) plus urgent transport to a hyperbaric chamber for recompression therapy, which is the definitive treatment — recompression mechanically shrinks the gas bubbles while re-establishing a gradient for nitrogen to redissolve and be safely eliminated. Time to recompression matters significantly for neurologic outcome.

Arterial Gas Embolism

Distinct from decompression sickness: caused by pulmonary barotrauma, typically from breath-holding during ascent (a diver panics or simply forgets to exhale while ascending) — expanding gas in the lungs at reduced ambient pressure ruptures alveoli, forcing gas directly into the pulmonary venous circulation, which then travels to the systemic arterial circulation (brain, coronary arteries) as gas emboli.

Presentation
Sudden, dramatic onset of neurologic symptoms (loss of consciousness, focal deficits, seizure) or cardiac symptoms (chest pain, arrhythmia) within minutes of surfacing — the rapid, dramatic onset immediately upon or shortly after surfacing is the key distinguishing feature from decompression sickness, which typically has a somewhat more gradual onset over minutes to hours.
⚡ Treatment
Same definitive treatment as severe DCS — immediate 100% oxygen and emergent recompression therapy — but with even greater time-sensitivity given the direct cerebral/coronary arterial gas embolization mechanism; place the patient supine (historically left lateral/Trendelenburg positioning was taught to trap bubbles away from the cerebral circulation, though this specific positioning recommendation has become more controversial/de-emphasized in current teaching given lack of strong supporting evidence and practical transport concerns) and transport emergently to the nearest hyperbaric facility.

Barotrauma

Direct mechanical injury to air-containing body spaces (middle ear, sinuses, lungs, occasionally GI tract) from failure to equalize pressure during descent or ascent — distinct from both DCS and AGE in that it's a direct pressure-differential injury to a gas-containing space, not a dissolved-gas or embolic phenomenon.

Ear/Sinus
The most common diving injury overall — middle ear barotrauma from failure to equalize during descent causes pain and potential tympanic membrane rupture; sinus barotrauma causes facial/sinus pain, usually from descent with congestion blocking normal sinus pressure equalization. Generally managed conservatively (decongestants, analgesia, avoiding further diving until resolved) unless TM rupture or significant injury is present.
Pulmonary
Pulmonary barotrauma from ascent (the same mechanism underlying arterial gas embolism above) can also cause pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum without necessarily causing arterial gas embolism — evaluate any diver with chest pain/dyspnea after ascent for both possibilities.
⚡ Clinical Pearl — DCS vs. AGE
The distinguishing question between DCS and AGE at the bedside is often timing and dive profile: AGE presents dramatically and immediately upon or within minutes of surfacing, often after a rapid or emergency ascent with breath-holding; DCS more typically follows a somewhat more gradual (though still urgent) course after a dive profile involving inadequate decompression stops. In practice, both are treated with the same immediate steps (100% oxygen, emergent recompression referral), so the distinction matters more for understanding mechanism than for initial management.

🏕️ In the Field

  • 100% oxygen by non-rebreather mask as early as possible for any suspected DCS or AGE — the single most important field intervention, since it improves the diffusion gradient for off-gassing inert nitrogen regardless of which mechanism is in play
  • Position the patient supine; the historical teaching of left lateral/Trendelenburg positioning to “trap” cerebral emboli has become de-emphasized/controversial given weak supporting evidence and practical transport difficulties — don't let attempts at special positioning delay oxygen and transport
  • Contact a dive-injury hotline (e.g., DAN — Divers Alert Network) early for real-time guidance and to help identify the nearest appropriate hyperbaric facility — not every hospital has recompression capability
  • Keep the patient still and avoid further exertion — physical activity can worsen bubble formation/embolization
  • Do NOT allow the patient to re-descend/re-enter the water as an improvised field treatment (an old and dangerous myth) — definitive treatment requires a controlled hyperbaric chamber, not uncontrolled water re-entry
  • For barotrauma (ear/sinus pain without neurologic or respiratory symptoms): supportive care and analgesia are reasonable while still arranging evaluation, since this doesn't carry the same emergent recompression urgency as DCS/AGE

🏥 In the ED

  • Continue 100% oxygen while arranging emergent transfer to a hyperbaric recompression chamber — recompression therapy is the definitive treatment and time to recompression significantly affects neurologic outcome in DCS/AGE
  • IV fluid resuscitation — dive-related dehydration is common and adequate hydration supports bubble resolution during recompression
  • Full neurologic exam and serial reassessment while awaiting transfer, to document baseline and trend severity
  • Chest imaging/exam for suspected pulmonary barotrauma (pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum) — identify and manage a pneumothorax before any hyperbaric chamber transfer, since chamber pressure changes can dramatically worsen an untreated pneumothorax
  • Coordinate directly with the receiving hyperbaric facility (often via a dive-injury hotline) rather than a generic transfer — not every facility with a hyperbaric chamber is staffed/equipped for emergent DCS/AGE treatment 24/7
  • For isolated ear/sinus barotrauma: conservative management (decongestants, analgesia), ENT follow-up, and counseling to avoid further diving/flying until resolved

🛡️ Prevention

  • Strict adherence to dive tables or a dive computer's calculated no-decompression limits and ascent rates — the direct prevention of the pressure-differential mechanism underlying DCS
  • Never hold your breath while ascending — breathe continuously and normally throughout ascent to prevent pulmonary barotrauma/AGE
  • Avoid flying or ascending to altitude for the recommended interval after diving (commonly 12–24 hours depending on dive profile) — reduced cabin pressure can trigger DCS in nitrogen-loaded tissue even after a seemingly uneventful dive
  • Buddy diving rather than solo diving, with regular equipment checks
  • Stay well-hydrated and avoid alcohol before diving — dehydration is a recognized DCS risk factor
  • Equalize ears/sinuses gradually during descent rather than forcing through discomfort, and abort the dive if unable to equalize

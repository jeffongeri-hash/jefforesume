01 · Pharmacokinetics

Onset, Peak, Duration — the Basic Vocabulary

Four numbers that describe the entire life cycle of a drug in the body.

“I gave IV morphine 8 minutes ago and it hasn't touched the pain — do I redose now, or is this just too soon?”

TermWhat it meansWhy it matters at the bedside
Onset of actionTime from administration to first detectable clinical effectRedosing before onset has occurred risks stacking doses rather than treating undertreated pain/agitation
Tmax (time to peak)Time to maximum plasma concentrationPeak effect — and peak risk of adverse effect — occurs here, not at the moment of administration
Duration of actionHow long the clinical effect lasts, which depends on both dose and half-lifeDetermines redosing interval; often longer than half-life would suggest at high doses
Half-life (t½)Time for plasma concentration to fall by 50%The single most useful number for predicting how long a drug will remain active or detectable

Route drives onset more than any other single factor: IV onset is typically measured in minutes (limited only by circulation time), IM/SC in 10–30 minutes, oral in 30–90 minutes depending on absorption and first-pass metabolism. Onset and half-life are independent of each other — a drug can have a fast onset and a long half-life (e.g., IV fentanyl: onset ~1–2 minutes, but repeated dosing still accumulates over its full half-life) or a slow onset and short half-life.

01 · Pharmacokinetics

Half-Life, Elimination & Washout

The rule of thumb that answers most “how long until this drug is gone” questions in the ED.

Figure 1. A single dose falls by half with every elapsed half-life — by 5 half-lives, ~97% has been eliminated.
The number worth memorizing: after 5 half-lives, a drug is considered essentially fully eliminated (~97% gone) — useful for estimating when a drug will be undetectable on testing, when it's safe to assume an ingestion is no longer actively absorbing/redistributing, or when a sedating agent should have cleared enough to reassess mental status.

The same 5-half-life rule runs in reverse for repeated dosing: with a fixed maintenance dose given at a fixed interval, plasma concentration climbs and plateaus at steady state after roughly 5 half-lives — not after the first dose, and not linearly. This is exactly why a loading dose exists: it's a single larger dose designed to reach the therapeutic range immediately, which the subsequent maintenance doses then simply sustain, instead of making the patient wait 5 half-lives (which, for a drug like amiodarone with a multi-week half-life, would be clinically absurd) to reach a therapeutic level by maintenance dosing alone.

Figure 2. A loading dose reaches the therapeutic range immediately; maintenance dosing alone climbs to the same plateau only after ~5 half-lives.
Loading dose sizing follows from volume of distribution, not half-life: loading dose ≈ Vd × target concentration. A drug with a large volume of distribution (extensively tissue-bound, like digoxin) needs a proportionally larger loading dose to “fill” that larger distribution space before plasma concentration reflects the target level.

First-order vs. zero-order elimination — the exception worth knowing

Everything above assumes first-order kinetics — a constant fraction of the drug is eliminated per unit time, which is how the vast majority of drugs behave and what makes the half-life concept work cleanly. A handful of clinically important drugs switch to zero-order kinetics at toxic or high therapeutic doses — a constant amount is eliminated per unit time because the metabolizing enzyme system is saturated. Phenytoin, ethanol, and high-dose aspirin are the classic examples. The practical consequence: the “half-life” stops being a fixed, predictable number once elimination is zero-order — a small dose increase can cause a disproportionately large rise in level, which is exactly why phenytoin dosing adjustments are made in small increments and why ethanol elimination is roughly linear (about one standard drink per hour) rather than exponential.

Myth: “half-life is a fixed property of the drug, full stop.” It's a fixed property only while elimination stays first-order — renal or hepatic impairment, enzyme saturation, and drug interactions affecting metabolism can all prolong the effective half-life well beyond the textbook number.

Illustrative half-lives across drug classes

02 · Susceptibility & MIC

What MIC Actually Is

One number that gets reported as a clean ‘S/I/R’ on the chart — worth knowing what's actually behind that letter.

“The susceptibility report says this E. coli is ‘susceptible’ to ciprofloxacin with an MIC of 1 — susceptible compared to what, exactly?”

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is the lowest concentration of an antibiotic that visibly inhibits growth of a specific bacterial isolate in a standardized lab test — it's a direct, isolate-specific measurement, not a category. The lab then compares that measured MIC against a breakpoint — a threshold set by a standards body (CLSI in the US, EUCAST in Europe) for that specific drug-organism pair — to assign the Susceptible / Intermediate / Resistant category actually printed on the report. Breakpoints are set based on what concentration is achievable in serum/tissue at standard dosing, so “Susceptible” means a standard dose of this drug should produce a concentration comfortably above this isolate's MIC, not that the drug is guaranteed to work.

A low MIC is not automatically “this drug is a great choice” in isolation — tissue penetration matters just as much. A drug with an excellent (low) MIC against an organism but poor penetration into the relevant tissue (e.g., many agents into the CNS or prostate) may still fail clinically despite a favorable lab number.
02 · Susceptibility & MIC

MIC Meets Dosing: the PK-PD Index

Different antibiotic classes kill bacteria by different rules — and the rule determines whether you should dose bigger, more often, or both.

Figure 3. Three PK-PD patterns — the shape of the concentration curve relative to MIC, not just the MIC itself, drives optimal dosing strategy.
PatternGoalPractical dosing implication
Time-dependent (T>MIC)Maximize the time concentration stays above MICBeta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems) — favors more frequent dosing or extended/continuous infusion over a single large dose
Concentration-dependent (Cmax/MIC)Maximize the peak concentration relative to MICAminoglycosides — favors larger, less frequent (e.g., once-daily) dosing over splitting into smaller frequent doses
AUC-dependent (AUC/MIC)Maximize total drug exposure over time relative to MICVancomycin, fluoroquinolones — total daily dose/exposure matters more than the exact schedule used to deliver it
Myth: “a susceptible MIC means any standard regimen will work equally well.” The PK-PD pattern above explains why under-dosing frequency (for a time-dependent drug) or under-dosing peak (for a concentration-dependent drug) can produce clinical failure despite a susceptible MIC on the report — the category tells you the organism is theoretically treatable with this drug class; the dosing strategy determines whether your specific regimen actually achieves the target.
Verify before use: current CLSI/EUCAST breakpoints are revised periodically and vary by organism-drug pair — confirm against your lab's current reporting standard rather than a fixed number, since breakpoints for a given drug can change without the drug itself changing.
03 · Culture Interpretation

The Organizing Principle

One question underlies every specimen type below: is this site normally sterile, or normally colonized?

The significance of a given colony count or organism depends almost entirely on where the specimen came from. Blood and CSF are normally sterile — essentially any growth deserves serious consideration. Urine, the respiratory tract, and skin/wounds are normally colonized to varying degrees — growth there has to clear a higher bar, and the specific bar depends on collection method, colony count, and whether the organism identified is a plausible pathogen or a usual-suspect contaminant/colonizer for that site.

03 · Culture Interpretation

Urine

The classic ‘100,000 or nothing’ teaching is outdated — the real threshold depends on symptoms and collection method.

“This urine culture grew 10,000 CFU/mL E. coli — the textbook number is 100,000, so is this just contamination?”

The 105 CFU/mL threshold (the Kass criterion) was originally derived from studies of asymptomatic bacteriuria and pyelonephritis risk in pregnant women — it was never intended as a universal cutoff for every clinical scenario, and modern guidance (IDSA/ACOG) uses lower thresholds for symptomatic patients specifically because waiting for 105 misses real, symptomatic infections.

ScenarioThreshold generally considered significant
Symptomatic woman, acute uncomplicated cystitis≥10³ CFU/mL (1,000)
Symptomatic patient, complicated UTI or pyelonephritis≥10⁴–10⁵ CFU/mL depending on guideline
Asymptomatic bacteriuria screening (e.g., pregnancy, pre-urologic-procedure)≥10⁵ CFU/mL (two consecutive specimens traditionally required for true asymptomatic bacteriuria)
Straight catheterized specimenLower thresholds (≥10²–10³ CFU/mL) often considered significant — less contamination risk than a voided specimen
Chronic indwelling catheterColonization is expected and near-universal — a positive culture alone does not define infection; requires accompanying symptoms (CAUTI is a clinical, not just microbiologic, diagnosis)
So: 10,000 CFU/mL can absolutely be as significant as 100,000 CFU/mL — in a symptomatic patient, particularly with pyuria on the accompanying urinalysis, 10⁴ E. coli is a real, treatable result, not a result to dismiss while waiting for a bigger number. The colony count needs to be read together with symptoms and collection method, not as a single universal gatekeeper.
Myth: “Anything under 100,000 is a contaminant.” That number only ever applied cleanly to asymptomatic screening scenarios. Mixed growth of 2–3 different organisms at low counts, especially from a voided specimen, is the pattern that actually suggests contamination — not a single organism at a moderate count in a symptomatic patient.
Verify before use: exact CFU thresholds vary by guideline (IDSA, ACOG, AUA) and continue to be revised — confirm current institutional/guideline threshold, particularly for the complicated-UTI and catheterized-specimen categories.

Blood

Blood is normally sterile, so the question shifts from ‘how much grew’ to ‘which organism, and how many bottles.’

Why 2–4 sets are drawn from separate sites: it's not redundancy — it's specifically to distinguish contamination (typically only 1 of several bottles/sets positive) from true bacteremia (typically concordant across multiple, separately-drawn sets). A single blood culture set drawn is a genuinely weaker diagnostic specimen than 2–4 sets for exactly this reason.

Respiratory / Sputum

The specimen type most prone to being misleadingly “positive” — it has to pass through colonized oropharynx to get to the cup.

Expectorated sputum is never a clean specimen — it's contaminated by upper airway flora by definition. Gram stain quality assessment (Bartlett criteria) should precede culture interpretation: a good-quality lower respiratory specimen shows >25 neutrophils and <10 squamous epithelial cells per low-power field. A specimen dominated by squamous cells reflects oral contamination, and any culture result from it should be interpreted cautiously — ideally the lab rejects it and requests recollection.

Specimen typeQuantitative threshold for significance
Expectorated sputumNo reliable quantitative cutoff — interpret via Gram stain quality, dominant organism morphology, and clinical correlation, not colony count alone
Endotracheal aspirate≥10⁵–10⁶ CFU/mL (quantitative), used mainly in ventilated patients
Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)≥10⁴ CFU/mL
Protected specimen brush (PSB)≥10³ CFU/mL
More invasive specimens (BAL, PSB) exist specifically because expectorated sputum is so prone to false-positive-by-contamination results in ambiguous cases — they bypass the oropharynx, which is why their quantitative thresholds are trusted in a way a sputum colony count generally isn't.

Wound

A superficial swab tells you what's colonizing the surface — not necessarily what's driving the infection underneath.

Swabbing the surface of an open wound reliably captures environmental and skin colonizers alongside (or instead of) any true pathogen, and chronic wounds (diabetic foot ulcers especially) are almost always polymicrobial on a surface swab regardless of whether invasive infection is present. A deep tissue specimen — curettage from a debrided wound base, an aspirate of purulent fluid, or bone biopsy when osteomyelitis is suspected — is a substantially more actionable specimen than a surface swab.

Myth: “the swab grew 4 organisms, so I need to cover all 4.” Polymicrobial surface-swab growth in a chronic wound usually reflects colonization of the wound bed, not 4 simultaneous invasive pathogens — treat based on the clinical picture (surrounding cellulitis, systemic signs, imaging concerning for osteomyelitis), not by covering everything the swab happened to grow.
Verify before use: quantitative wound/burn biopsy thresholds (historically >10⁵ organisms/gram tissue to suggest invasive infection) come from burn literature and are not routinely performed outside specialized centers — most wound management decisions rely on clinical exam plus deep-specimen culture rather than quantitative biopsy.
03 · Culture Interpretation

Cerebrospinal Fluid

The other normally-sterile site — treated with the same low threshold for concern as blood, with one notable exception.

Any bacterial growth from CSF is treated as significant until proven otherwise, given the stakes of missing bacterial meningitis. Culture positivity should always be interpreted alongside the CSF cell count, protein, and glucose — a positive culture with a completely normal cell count/chemistry profile is a more suspicious pattern for contamination (or a shunt-related low-grade colonization) than a positive culture with a clearly inflammatory profile (elevated WBC, elevated protein, low glucose).

The specific exception: in a patient with a CSF shunt or external ventricular drain, low-virulence skin organisms (coagulase-negative staphylococci, Cutibacterium acnes) recovered from CSF can represent either a true indolent shunt infection or introduction during collection — unlike community-acquired meningitis, this specific scenario genuinely requires correlation with repeat cultures, CSF chemistry, and the clinical picture (shunt malfunction symptoms, fever) rather than automatic treatment of every positive culture as definite infection.
Verify before use: gram stain sensitivity for bacterial meningitis is meaningfully reduced by prior antibiotic exposure — a negative gram stain or culture in a partially treated patient does not rule out bacterial meningitis, and empiric treatment decisions should not wait on culture results.

Antibiotic Indications Atlas

The flip side of empiric-antibiotics teaching: for 18 common conditions, exactly where the evidence says treat vs. withhold, and oral vs. IV — anchored to IDSA (and companion AGA/ATS/AAP) guidelines, with the specific trial data behind each threshold. A dedicated corticosteroid section covers 11 more conditions where the same "does the evidence actually support this" lens applies.

Stewardship Philosophy & the IV-to-PO Framework

The organizing principle across this entire atlas

IDSA's core position, stated explicitly in their 2018 sepsis statement and implicit throughout every guideline below: if there is no infection, there is no indication for antibiotic therapy — and even where infection exists, many common conditions are self-limited or adequately treated with source control (drainage, observation) alone. Every condition below follows the same format: what actually defines "needs antibiotics," what defines "needs IV specifically," and what the evidence base is for the threshold.

When does IV actually beat oral? (Applies across every condition below)

Route selection is frequently over-defaulted to IV out of habit rather than pharmacology. IV is genuinely necessary when: (1) the patient can't reliably absorb oral medication (vomiting, ileus, short bowel, severe mucositis), (2) the infection is severe/systemic enough that faster, more reliable serum levels matter (sepsis — see the companion Sepsis Atlas, meningitis, endocarditis), (3) no adequate oral option with sufficient bioavailability/tissue penetration exists for the specific organism/site (e.g., IV vancomycin for invasive MRSA — oral vancomycin doesn't absorb systemically). Conversely, highly bioavailable oral agents (fluoroquinolones, linezolid, metronidazole, doxycycline, TMP-SMX, and to a lesser extent amoxicillin-clavulanate) achieve serum levels close enough to IV dosing that route alone should rarely be the deciding factor once a patient can tolerate oral intake and isn't in shock. IV-to-PO switch criteria (afebrile ≥24h, hemodynamically stable, tolerating oral intake, clinically improving) apply across nearly every condition in this atlas and are a standing stewardship opportunity most inpatient teams underuse.

System 01

Gastrointestinal

Diverticulitis AGA / ACP

This is your example, and it's a genuine paradigm shift most training predates: uncomplicated diverticulitis increasingly does not need antibiotics at all.

Hinchey stageDescriptionAntibiotic decision
IaConfined pericolic inflammation/phlegmon, no abscessSelective — AGA 2015: "antibiotics should be used selectively, rather than routinely." Multiple RCTs (AVOD, DIABOLO, STAND, DINAMO) show no difference in complications, recurrence, or surgery rate with observation alone in reliable, non-immunocompromised outpatients without sepsis.
Ib / IIPericolic or pelvic abscessAntibiotics + drainage — abscess ≥3–4cm generally warrants IR drainage plus antibiotics; smaller abscesses may respond to antibiotics alone.
IIIPurulent peritonitisIV antibiotics + emergent surgery
IVFeculent peritonitisIV antibiotics + emergent surgery
The evidence for skipping antibiotics

AVOD trial (2012, n=623): no difference in complications/emergency surgery (1.9% vs 1.0%) between antibiotics and IV fluids alone — 11-year follow-up (2019) confirmed no difference in recurrence (31.3% both arms), complications, or cancer detection.

DIABOLO trial (2017) and STAND trial (2020, placebo-controlled, Hinchey Ia only): both confirmed non-inferiority of observation.

DINAMO trial (480 outpatients): similarly low return-visit and hospitalization rates with vs. without amoxicillin-clavulanate.

Who still needs antibiotics even for "uncomplicated" disease

Immunosuppression, pregnancy, inability to tolerate oral intake, significant comorbidity, signs of sepsis, or failure of 48–72h observation — all of the major RCTs above explicitly excluded these patients, so the "skip antibiotics" evidence doesn't generalize to them.

If treating: regimen

Oral (outpatient, uncomplicated): amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125mg BID, or ciprofloxacin 500mg BID + metronidazole 500mg TID, for 4–7 days (shorter courses increasingly favored).

IV (complicated, Hinchey II–IV, or failed oral): a second/third-generation cephalosporin (cefuroxime or ceftriaxone) + metronidazole, or piperacillin-tazobactam, or a carbapenem — mirrors the intra-abdominal-source empiric logic from the Sepsis Atlas Volume 4.

Location note: right-sided diverticulitis (more common in Asian populations) is generally managed with the same severity-based framework as left-sided disease, though it more often mimics appendicitis and warrants closer attention to the alternate diagnosis.

C. difficile Colitis IDSA/SHEA 2021

Antibiotics are indicated here — the question is severity-tailored route and agent

Unlike diverticulitis, once CDI is confirmed, antibiotics (specifically anti-C. diff agents) are indicated at every severity level — the withholding question here is about the triggering antibiotic (stop the offending agent) and about using oral vancomycin/fidaxomicin instead of the historically reflexive metronidazole.

SeverityCriteriaRegimen
Non-severeWBC ≤15,000 AND Cr <1.5 mg/dLOral fidaxomicin 200mg PO BID ×10d (preferred, lower recurrence) OR vancomycin 125mg PO QID ×10d
SevereWBC >15,000 OR Cr ≥1.5 mg/dLOral — same regimens as non-severe; severity does not change the drug choice per 2021 update
FulminantHypotension, shock, ileus, megacolonOral/NG vancomycin 500mg QID PLUS IV metronidazole 500mg q8h — the one scenario in this table where IV therapy is added; fidaxomicin has no data in fulminant disease and should not be used

Note the counterintuitive pharmacology: oral vancomycin works in CDI because it acts locally in the gut lumen (not systemically absorbed) — this is the opposite logic from most infections, where oral vancomycin would be useless and IV would be required.

Infectious / Bacterial Colitis (non-C. diff)

Most bacterial gastroenteritis does not need antibiotics

Salmonella, Campylobacter, and non-O157 E. coli gastroenteritis in an immunocompetent, non-severely-ill host is typically self-limited and managed with supportive care (hydration) alone — treatment can prolong Salmonella carriage and, for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC/O157:H7), antibiotics are actively contraindicated outside specific circumstances because they may increase the risk of hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) by promoting toxin release.

When treatment IS indicated

Severe disease (high fever, ≥6 bloody stools/day, hospitalization required), immunocompromise, age extremes (infants, frail elderly), suspected Shigella (treatment shortens duration and transmission), or invasive/bacteremic disease. Empiric outpatient regimen when indicated: azithromycin 500mg PO daily ×3d or a fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin 500mg BID ×3-5d, resistance-dependent). IV therapy reserved for bacteremia, severe systemic illness, or inability to tolerate oral intake — most infectious colitis, even when treatment is warranted, is a PO-appropriate diagnosis.

Cholecystitis & Cholangitis Tokyo Guidelines

Source control (not antibiotics alone) is the definitive treatment

Antibiotics are a bridge to — not a substitute for — cholecystectomy (cholecystitis) or biliary drainage (cholangitis). Tokyo Guidelines grade severity I (mild) through III (severe, organ dysfunction) and grade drives both antibiotic breadth and urgency of source control, mirroring the source-control-timing principle from the Sepsis Atlas.

Mild-moderate (Grade I-II)

IV ceftriaxone ± metronidazole, or a single agent like piperacillin-tazobactam, until surgery/drainage; can often transition to oral once source-controlled and clinically improving.

Severe (Grade III) / cholangitis with sepsis

IV broad-spectrum coverage (piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) — this overlaps directly with unknown-source septic-patient coverage from the Sepsis Atlas Volume 4 — plus urgent biliary decompression (ERCP) for cholangitis, which should not be delayed for antibiotics to "work" first.

Appendicitis CODA Trial

Antibiotics-alone is a legitimate option for uncomplicated disease — not the default, but not fringe either

The CODA trial (NEJM 2020, n=1,552) found antibiotics alone (non-inferior primary outcome) avoided appendectomy in ~70% of patients at 90 days, though patients with an appendicolith had higher subsequent appendectomy rates in the antibiotics arm. This is shared-decision-making territory, not a strict guideline mandate — surgery remains standard of care, but antibiotics-alone is evidence-based for uncomplicated appendicitis without appendicolith, perforation, or abscess in a patient who understands the ~30% chance of eventual appendectomy.

Regimen if treating medically or as pre-op coverage: IV piperacillin-tazobactam, or ceftriaxone + metronidazole — same intra-abdominal logic as diverticulitis/cholecystitis above. Complicated appendicitis (perforation, abscess) requires source control (drainage or surgery) plus IV antibiotics, not medical management alone.

System 02

Genitourinary

Asymptomatic Bacteriuria IDSA 2019

This is your second example, and it's the single clearest "don't treat the number, treat the patient" teaching point in all of outpatient medicine.

The rule

Do NOT screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria — defined as ≥10⁵ CFU/mL bacterial growth, with or without pyuria, in a patient with no urinary symptoms — in: healthy nonpregnant women (pre- or postmenopausal), older men or women (including those with delirium or a fall, provided there's no fever, new GU symptoms, or hemodynamic instability), diabetics, patients with indwelling catheters, spinal cord injury patients, or patients with urologic devices (including at implantation). This is a strong recommendation, and treating it is one of the most common sources of inappropriate antibiotic use and downstream C. difficile risk in both inpatient and outpatient medicine.

The two exceptions — screen AND treat

Pregnancy — 4–7 days of therapy (nitrofurantoin or a beta-lactam preferred for pregnancy safety), because untreated ASB in pregnancy is linked to pyelonephritis and preterm labor.

Before an invasive urologic procedure with expected mucosal trauma/bleeding (e.g., TURP) — treat immediately before the procedure to prevent bacteremia from mucosal disruption.

Your example, specifically

A "mostly asymptomatic woman" with a positive urinalysis or culture, no dysuria/frequency/urgency/suprapubic pain/fever — by IDSA 2019, this is textbook ASB. The urine culture should often not have been sent in the first place absent symptoms; if it was and it's positive, the correct action is no antibiotics, not treatment "to be safe."

Uncomplicated Cystitis (Symptomatic) IDSA / ESMID 2010

The moment symptoms (dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain) are present in a healthy, nonpregnant woman without structural/functional urinary abnormality, this becomes a genuinely different — and PO-only — decision than ASB above.

First-line oral regimens (all outpatient, no IV role)

Nitrofurantoin 100mg PO BID ×5 days — avoid if early pyelonephritis suspected (inadequate tissue levels) or CrCl <30.

TMP-SMX DS PO BID ×3 days — avoid if local resistance >20% or recent use.

Fosfomycin 3g PO single dose — convenient but slightly lower efficacy than the above in comparative trials.

Pivmecillinam where available — additional first-line option per the joint guideline.

Fluoroquinolones and beta-lactams (amoxicillin-clavulanate, cefpodoxime) are considered second-line for uncomplicated cystitis specifically due to collateral resistance/C. diff concerns with fluoroquinolones and modestly inferior efficacy for beta-lactams — reserve fluoroquinolones for pyelonephritis or complicated UTI where their tissue penetration matters more.

Pyelonephritis

Oral is often still appropriate — IV is for instability or intolerance, not reflex

A well-appearing patient who can tolerate oral intake, is hemodynamically stable, and has no concerning comorbidity can often be treated entirely as an outpatient with oral fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin 500mg BID or levofloxacin 750mg daily) for 5–7 days, or TMP-SMX for 14 days if susceptible — frequently after a single ED-administered IV/IM dose of a long-acting agent (e.g., ceftriaxone 1g) to ensure early adequate levels while oral absorption catches up.

IV / admission indications

Hemodynamic instability or sepsis criteria (cross-reference Sepsis Atlas Vol. 1), inability to tolerate oral intake (vomiting), pregnancy, urologic obstruction (needs source control — nephrostomy/stent, not antibiotics alone), or failure of outpatient therapy. IV regimen: ceftriaxone, or a fluoroquinolone IV if oral not tolerated, or piperacillin-tazobactam/carbapenem if risk factors for resistant organisms (recent hospitalization, prior ESBL, healthcare exposure).

Acute Bacterial Prostatitis

Unlike cystitis, prostatitis needs agents with reliable prostatic tissue penetration and a much longer course — fluoroquinolones or TMP-SMX for 2–4 weeks (not the 3–7 days typical of cystitis), due to poor drug penetration into inflamed prostatic tissue and high relapse risk with shorter courses. IV therapy (ceftriaxone or a fluoroquinolone IV, ± an aminoglycoside for severe presentations) is warranted for patients with systemic toxicity, inability to tolerate oral intake, or urinary retention requiring catheterization — otherwise this is an oral-appropriate diagnosis once the patient is stable enough for the required prolonged course.

System 03

Respiratory

Acute Bronchitis Antibiotics NOT Indicated

The clearest "don't" in this entire atlas

Acute bronchitis is viral in the overwhelming majority of cases. Purulent sputum is not a reliable marker of bacterial infection — over 95% of patients with purulent sputum in this context do not have pneumonia. ACCP and CDC guidance: routine antibiotic treatment is not justified regardless of cough duration (which routinely lasts 2–3 weeks even in uncomplicated viral bronchitis) or sputum color. Roughly 2.7 million outpatient visits annually are for this diagnosis, and the CDC estimates at least half of antibiotic prescriptions for acute respiratory conditions are unnecessary — bronchitis is a major driver of that number.

The only reason to reconsider: clinical findings suggesting pneumonia instead — HR>100, RR>24, temp>38°C, or focal exam findings — at which point this is no longer "bronchitis," it's suspected CAP (next section) and should be worked up/treated as such, ideally with a CXR to distinguish the two.

Community-Acquired Pneumonia IDSA/ATS 2019

Site-of-care decision drives route — use a validated score, not gestalt alone

IDSA/ATS 2019 prefers the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI) over CURB-65 for outpatient-vs-inpatient decisions (PSI was validated with a larger, more granular derivation cohort), though CURB-65 remains far more practical for quick bedside use. Neither score determines ICU-level need directly — for that, use the 2007 ATS/IDSA severe CAP criteria (1 major criterion — septic shock needing vasopressors, or respiratory failure needing mechanical ventilation — OR ≥3 minor criteria), which predicts ICU need with 84% sensitivity/78% specificity using the major criterion alone, or 56%/91% using ≥3 minor criteria without a major one.

Procalcitonin should NOT be used to withhold antibiotics when CAP is clinically suspected and radiographically confirmed — its sensitivity for bacterial infection (38–91% across studies) isn't reliable enough for that decision in this specific context, even though PCT has more established roles elsewhere (see Sepsis Atlas Vol. 2).

COPD Exacerbation Anthonisen Criteria

Antibiotics only with the right symptom combination — not every exacerbation

The classic Anthonisen criteria: increased dyspnea, increased sputum volume, increased sputum purulence. Antibiotics are most clearly beneficial with all three (Type 1) or with increased purulence plus one other criterion (Type 2). Patients with only one criterion (Type 3), or requiring mechanical ventilation, generally derive less clear benefit from antibiotics unless mechanically ventilated (where they're recommended regardless). Route: oral (azithromycin, doxycycline, or amoxicillin-clavulanate depending on local resistance/severity) suffices for the large majority; IV is reserved for those requiring hospitalization who can't tolerate oral intake or have severe/decompensated presentations.

System 04

Skin & Soft Tissue

Purulent SSTI / Abscess IDSA 2014

Incision and drainage is the treatment — antibiotics are the adjunct, not the default

For cutaneous abscesses, furuncles, carbuncles, and inflamed epidermoid cysts: I&D is primary therapy, and systemic antibiotics are unnecessary for mild, uncomplicated cases — even MRSA — once adequately drained. This surprises people trained to reflexively add antibiotics to every I&D.

SeverityDefinitionAntibiotics?
MildNo systemic signs of infectionNo — I&D alone
ModeratePurulent infection + systemic signs of infectionYes, oral — TMP-SMX or doxycycline (anti-MRSA coverage)
SevereFailed I&D + oral antibiotics, OR SIRS/temp>38°C/HR>90/RR>24/abnormal WBC, OR immunocompromisedYes, IV — vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid, telavancin, or ceftaroline

The decision to add antibiotics at all should be driven by the presence/absence of SIRS criteria (Sepsis Atlas Vol. 1 callback) or impaired host defenses — not reflexively for every abscess regardless of size or systemic findings.

Nonpurulent Cellulitis IDSA 2014

The opposite logic from purulent SSTI: with no drainable focus, antimicrobial therapy is the primary treatment — there's nothing to drain.

When to add empiric MRSA coverage even for nonpurulent disease

Penetrating trauma, evidence of MRSA infection elsewhere, known MRSA nasal colonization, injection drug use, purulent drainage from an otherwise nonpurulent-appearing lesion, or SIRS in severe disease.

Necrotizing Fasciitis Surgical Emergency

Brief flag since it's the one skin/soft-tissue diagnosis where the framework above doesn't apply: this is never an antibiotics-alone or outpatient-oral decision. Emergent surgical debridement is the definitive treatment; IV broad-spectrum coverage (e.g., vancomycin/piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem, PLUS clindamycin for its antitoxin/protein-synthesis-inhibition effect against toxin-producing strains) is started immediately but does not substitute for surgery. Rapidly progressive pain out of proportion to exam findings, bullae, skin necrosis, or crepitus should trigger surgical consultation before imaging delays intervention.

System 05

ENT / Oral

Acute Bacterial Sinusitis IDSA 2012

Most "sinusitis" is viral rhinosinusitis — these three criteria define when it's not

IDSA diagnostic criteria for acute bacterial rhinosinusitis (ABRS), any ONE of which supports antibiotics: (1) persistent symptoms ≥10 days without improvement, (2) severe symptoms/signs — fever ≥39°C plus purulent nasal discharge or facial pain — lasting ≥3-4 consecutive days at illness onset, or (3) "double sickening" — new fever, headache, or worsening nasal discharge after initial improvement of a typical viral URI lasting 5-6 days.

Regimen: amoxicillin-clavulanate (preferred over amoxicillin alone per this guideline, due to beta-lactamase-producing organisms) 500/125mg TID or 875/125mg BID for 5-7 days (adults); high-dose (2g BID) for severe illness, age >65, recent hospitalization, recent antibiotic use, or immunocompromise. Doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone for penicillin allergy. Route: oral only — this is never an IV-indicated diagnosis outside of a complication (orbital or intracranial extension), which is a different, far more serious presentation.

Acute Otitis Media AAP

Watchful waiting is guideline-supported in specific age/severity combinations

AAP guidance supports observation without antibiotics (with a safety-net prescription or clear return-precautions) for: children 6mo-2yr with unilateral AOM and non-severe symptoms, and children ≥2yr with unilateral or bilateral AOM and non-severe symptoms (mild otalgia <48h, temp <39°C). Non-severe, reliable follow-up, and shared decision-making with caregivers are the operative conditions. Severe symptoms (moderate-severe otalgia, otalgia ≥48h, temp ≥39°C), otorrhea, bilateral disease in children <2yr, or an unreliable follow-up situation all favor immediate antibiotics.

First-line: amoxicillin 80-90mg/kg/day divided BID (high-dose, for resistant pneumococcus coverage); amoxicillin-clavulanate if amoxicillin in the past 30 days, concurrent purulent conjunctivitis, or history of recurrent AOM unresponsive to amoxicillin. Route: oral — AOM is essentially never an IV-antibiotic diagnosis in an immunocompetent child; IM ceftriaxone is a historical option for a child who can't tolerate oral medication, not a routine choice.

Pharyngitis Centor / McIsaac

Test before you treat — don't empirically cover strep on gestalt alone

Centor/McIsaac criteria (1 point each): fever >38°C, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, absence of cough, plus an age adjustment (+1 if 3-14yr, -1 if ≥45yr). Score 0-1: no testing/antibiotics — viral cause overwhelmingly likely. Score 2-3: rapid strep test or culture, treat only if positive. Score ≥4: empiric treatment reasonable while awaiting confirmation, or test-and-treat depending on local practice. The whole point of the score is preventing exactly the reflexive "sore throat + fever = amoxicillin" pattern that drives unnecessary prescribing.

If confirmed GAS pharyngitis: penicillin V or amoxicillin ×10 days remains first-line (no resistance reported to date); a single dose of IM benzathine penicillin is an option for adherence concerns. Route: oral (or single-dose IM) — never an IV-indicated diagnosis absent a suppurative complication (see peritonsillar abscess, below).

Peritonsillar Abscess

Unlike simple pharyngitis, this is a drainage-plus-antibiotics diagnosis — needle aspiration, incision and drainage, or (for recurrent/severe cases) tonsillectomy, combined with antibiotics covering GAS and oral anaerobes: ampicillin-sulbactam or clindamycin initially. Route: IV is reasonable for the initial dose(s) if the patient has trismus/odynophagia limiting oral intake, transitioning to oral amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin once drained and tolerating intake — most cases can complete therapy orally as an outpatient after drainage, reserving admission for airway concern, dehydration, or diabetic/immunocompromised patients.

Dental / Oral Infections

Drainage (extraction, incision, root canal access) is definitive — antibiotics are adjunctive, not primary

A localized dental abscess without spreading infection or systemic signs is primarily a dental procedural problem, not an antibiotic problem — antibiotics without source control (drainage/extraction) frequently fail and simply delay definitive care. Antibiotics are indicated as an adjunct when there's spreading infection (facial swelling extending beyond the immediate tooth, trismus), systemic signs (fever, malaise), or when definitive dental procedural care isn't immediately available and needs to be bridged.

Regimen: penicillin VK or amoxicillin first-line; amoxicillin-clavulanate or clindamycin (penicillin-allergic, or if anaerobic coverage needs broadening) for more extensive infections; add metronidazole to a penicillin for severe/spreading infection. Route: oral for the large majority; IV reserved for Ludwig's angina, deep neck space extension, or airway compromise — these are surgical-emergency-adjacent presentations requiring admission, not routine dental infection management.

System 06

Adjunctive Corticosteroids

When Steroids Actually Help

The organizing principle, mirroring Section 1's antibiotic philosophy

Steroids help when the harm comes from the host's own inflammatory response — cerebral edema in meningitis, airway edema in croup/asthma, cytokine-driven lung injury in severe pneumonia, cord edema from tumor — not simply because a patient "looks sick." The evidence below ranges from strongly positive (meningitis, croup, COPD) to genuinely negative-by-default (anaphylaxis) — this section is deliberately honest about where the data doesn't support the reflex.

Severe CAP CAPE COD Trial, 2023

A genuine practice-changer, still catching up in US guidelines

CAPE COD (NEJM 2023, n=795, 31 French ICUs): adults with severe CAP (PSI >130, P/F ratio <300, or need for mechanical/noninvasive ventilation with PEEP≥5) randomized to hydrocortisone 200mg/day continuous infusion (tapered from day 4) vs. placebo. 28-day mortality: 6.2% vs. 11.9% (p=0.006) — a striking absolute risk reduction, with reduced need for intubation and no increase in major adverse events (hyperglycemia was the most consistent side effect; no increase in GI bleed or secondary infection).

Why this isn't universally adopted yet

The 2022 ESCAPe trial (VA, methylprednisolone) was negative — but underpowered (586 of a planned 1,420 patients) and treated later in the illness course (median ~1.5–2 days from admission vs. much earlier in CAPE COD). A 2023 meta-analysis combining both found an overall mortality benefit, with a dose-response relationship above ~6mg dexamethasone-equivalent (~160mg hydrocortisone) — suggesting dose and timing, not the CAPE COD result itself, explain the discrepancy. As of this writing, SCCM (2024) and ERS recommend steroids for severe CAP; ATS/IDSA guidance is still pending formal update — worth knowing you're currently practicing ahead of the slower-moving US pulmonary society guideline, not behind it.

Not indicated for non-severe/outpatient CAP — this is specifically a severe-disease, ICU-level intervention.

COPD Exacerbation REDUCE Trial

Short courses work as well as long ones — and oral beats IV

REDUCE trial: 5 days of prednisone was noninferior to 14 days for re-exacerbation within 6 months, while cutting cumulative glucocorticoid exposure roughly in half (379mg vs. 793mg). A pooled analysis of REDUCE and CORTICO-COP found the short-course (2–5 day) group had more days alive and out of hospital than the 14-day group, with no difference in death/ICU admission. GOLD/ATS: prednisone 40mg PO daily ×5 days is now standard — no taper needed for a course this short.

Oral, not IV, by default — a large observational study (~80,000 non-ICU patients) found IV corticosteroids associated with longer hospital stays and higher costs with no outcome benefit over oral dosing; no significant difference in mortality, rehospitalization, or treatment failure between routes. Reserve IV for patients who can't tolerate oral intake, not as an automatic inpatient default.

Bacterial Meningitis de Gans Trial

Timing is not a minor detail here — it's the entire mechanism

de Gans & van de Beek (NEJM 2002, n=301 adults): dexamethasone 10mg IV given 15–20 minutes before or with the first dose of antibiotic, then every 6 hours for 4 days. Rationale: antibiotic-induced bacterial lysis triggers subarachnoid inflammation — steroids given after that cascade has started lose most of their benefit. Giving dexamethasone after antibiotics have already been running is a documented, common timing error worth actively avoiding.

What the evidence actually shows, honestly

A 2013 Cochrane review (25 studies, 4,121 patients) found steroids significantly reduced hearing loss (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.51–0.88) with a trend toward reduced neurological sequelae; overall mortality reduction was non-significant (17.8% vs 19.9%, p=0.07) except in a pre-specified pneumococcal meningitis subgroup (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.72–0.98). A separate individual-patient-data meta-analysis (2,029 patients, mixed pediatric/adult, high HIV-prevalence settings included) found no significant mortality benefit overall but did find reduced hearing loss among survivors (24.1% vs 29.5%, p=0.04) — the benefit is real but concentrated in specific outcomes (hearing) and populations (pneumococcal, non-HIV, developed-world settings), not a uniform mortality effect everywhere it's been studied.

Croup Well-Established, Any Severity

Single-dose oral dexamethasone 0.6mg/kg is standard for croup of any severity. Two separate noninferiority RCTs found low-dose dexamethasone (0.15mg/kg) and oral prednisolone (1mg/kg) both performed equivalently to the standard 0.6mg/kg dose on Westley Croup Score and re-attendance rates — meaning the historically "standard" higher dose isn't required for effect, useful to know when dexamethasone concentration/formulation is limited. Nebulized epinephrine is added for moderate-severe cases, but the corticosteroid dose is a single dose regardless — no multi-day course needed for typical croup.

Asthma Exacerbation

Reduces relapse and admission — and single-dose dexamethasone is now a reasonable alternative to multi-day prednisone

Systemic corticosteroids are standard of care for moderate-severe asthma exacerbations, reducing relapse, subsequent admission, and beta-agonist requirement. Adult ED data (Rehrer et al., Ann Emerg Med 2016): single-dose oral dexamethasone was noninferior to 5 days of prednisone. Pediatric protocols show the same pattern comparing single-dose dexamethasone (0.3–0.6mg/kg) against 3–5 day prednisolone courses — the appeal being avoidance of the compliance problems (taste, vomiting, multi-day adherence) that come with prednisolone in children.

Sinusitis & Pharyngitis — Adjunctive, Not Primary

Sinusitis

Intranasal corticosteroids (not oral) are IDSA-recommended as an adjunct to antibiotics for ABRS, particularly in patients with an allergic-rhinitis history — oral systemic steroids are not part of the standard ABRS regimen and shouldn't be reached for as a primary intervention here.

Pharyngitis / severe odynophagia

A single dose of oral dexamethasone modestly shortens time-to-pain-resolution in acute sore throat (Cochrane review) — a reasonable adjunct for severe odynophagia (including peritonsillar cellulitis/early PTA) alongside, never instead of, appropriate antibiotics when GAS is confirmed. Effect size is modest — this is a comfort adjunct, not a disease-modifying intervention the way it is in croup or meningitis.

Anaphylaxis Not Routinely Indicated

The clearest "don't" in this entire steroid section

Epinephrine is the only proven, first-line treatment for anaphylaxis. Corticosteroids have been reflexively co-administered for decades on the theoretical premise that they prevent biphasic reactions (recurrence of symptoms 1+ hours after apparent resolution, occurring in roughly 1–20% of cases depending on the study). The evidence does not support this. A 2022 JHM "Things We Do For No Reason" review, a 2017 systematic review (22 human studies), and multiple ED cohort studies (including Grunau et al., Ann Emerg Med 2015) consistently found no reduction in relapse/biphasic reactions associated with steroid use; steroids have a slow onset of action that doesn't match the acute pathophysiology, and current European Resuscitation Council guidance explicitly recommends against routine use. Steroids may modestly shorten hospital length of stay in some observational data, but that's a different (and much weaker) justification than "preventing a dangerous rebound."

Malignant Spinal Cord Compression Oncologic Emergency

Suspected malignant epidural spinal cord compression (new severe back pain + malignancy history + any neuro deficit) warrants immediate high-dose dexamethasone while urgent MRI and radiation oncology/neurosurgery are arranged — steroids reduce cord edema and can preserve neurologic function during the window before definitive therapy (radiation or surgical decompression). An evidence-based guideline review found good evidence for high-dose dexamethasone (96mg/day) but only inconclusive evidence for moderate-dose (16mg/day) regimens — many centers now use moderate dosing given similar functional outcomes with fewer steroid-related complications in more recent practice, but the strongest trial evidence specifically backs the high-dose regimen. Not indicated in patients who are neurologically intact and ambulatory pretreatment with only subclinical/imaging-only compression — steroids there add toxicity without a demonstrated benefit.

Gout Flare

NSAIDs and colchicine remain first-line for acute gout. Corticosteroids (oral prednisone ~30-40mg daily, tapered over 5-10 days, or intra-articular injection for a mono-articular flare) are an equally effective alternative specifically for patients where NSAIDs/colchicine are contraindicated or high-risk — renal impairment, active GI bleeding or PUD history, therapeutic anticoagulation, or colchicine intolerance/drug interactions. This is a "use when the usual first-line is unsafe" indication, not a routine substitute.

IBD Flare (Ulcerative Colitis / Crohn's)

Systemic corticosteroids (oral prednisone for moderate flares; IV methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone for severe/hospitalized flares) are standard induction therapy for moderate-to-severe flares — but explicitly not for maintenance; steroids induce remission, they don't sustain it, and prolonged use should trigger a bridge to a steroid-sparing agent (biologic, immunomodulator) rather than repeated steroid courses. Mild flares are generally managed with 5-ASA agents alone, without steroids.

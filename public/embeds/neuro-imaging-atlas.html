Brain: Intracranial Hemorrhage

11 findings

1. Epidural hematoma

Concerning

Bleeding between the skull and dura, classically from a middle meningeal artery tear associated with a temporal bone fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Biconvex (lentiform), hyperdense collection that does not cross suture lines but can cross dural attachments/midline; often with an associated overlying skull fracture.

Differential
  • Arterial epidural hematoma (classic, rapid expansion)
  • Venous epidural hematoma (slower course, often from dural venous sinus injury)
Next Step

Neurosurgical emergency evaluation; rapid evacuation for larger or symptomatic hematomas given classic 'lucid interval' followed by rapid deterioration.

2. Subdural hematoma, acute

Concerning

Bleeding between the dura and arachnoid, from bridging vein tearing, classically in trauma or on anticoagulation.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic, hyperdense collection that crosses suture lines but does not cross the midline (limited by the falx); can cause mass effect and midline shift.

Differential
  • Traumatic acute subdural hematoma
  • Spontaneous (anticoagulation, coagulopathy)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; evacuation based on thickness, midline shift, and clinical status.

3. Subdural hematoma, chronic

Variable

Older subdural collection, common in elderly or anticoagulated patients, often from minor or unrecognized trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic, hypodense (relative to acute blood) collection, sometimes with internal membranes or loculations; may be bilateral.

Differential
  • Chronic subdural hematoma (classic in elderly, alcoholics, anticoagulated patients)
  • Subdural hygroma (CSF-density, non-hemorrhagic)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral, particularly if symptomatic or causing significant mass effect; smaller/asymptomatic collections may be observed.

4. Subdural hematoma, mixed acute-on-chronic

Concerning

Acute hemorrhage into a pre-existing chronic subdural collection, often from re-bleeding after minor trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Layering or mixed-density collection with a hyperdense (acute) component within an overall hypodense (chronic) collection, sometimes with a fluid-fluid level.

Differential
  • Acute-on-chronic subdural hematoma
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; often requires evacuation given the acute component.

5. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, traumatic

Concerning

Bleeding into the subarachnoid space from trauma, the most common cause of SAH overall.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense material within the sulci and cisterns, typically localized near the site of impact/contusion rather than diffusely centered on the circle of Willis.

Differential
  • Traumatic SAH (localized pattern)
  • Aneurysmal SAH (more centered on basal cisterns, consider if pattern atypical for trauma)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/trauma consultation; monitor for vasospasm in significant SAH.

6. Subarachnoid hemorrhage, aneurysmal

Concerning

Bleeding into the subarachnoid space from a ruptured cerebral aneurysm, classically presenting with 'thunderclap' headache.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense blood filling the basal cisterns and sulci in a pattern often centered on the circle of Willis, with distribution pattern (Fisher grade) informing vasospasm risk.

Differential
  • Ruptured cerebral aneurysm (most common cause of non-traumatic SAH)
  • Perimesencephalic non-aneurysmal SAH (more benign pattern, blood confined to perimesencephalic cisterns)
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical/neurointerventional consultation; CT angiography to identify the aneurysm and plan securing (clipping or coiling).

7. Intraparenchymal hemorrhage, hypertensive pattern

Concerning

Bleeding within the brain parenchyma, classically from chronic hypertension affecting deep perforating vessels.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense hematoma in the deep gray matter structures (basal ganglia, thalamus, pons, cerebellum), the classic hypertensive hemorrhage locations.

Differential
  • Hypertensive hemorrhage (deep location, most common)
  • Hemorrhage from an underlying vascular malformation or tumor (consider if atypical location/appearance)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurocritical care consultation; blood pressure management and monitoring for expansion.

8. Intraparenchymal hemorrhage, lobar (amyloid pattern)

Concerning

Bleeding within the peripheral, lobar brain parenchyma, associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy in older adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense hematoma in a peripheral, lobar (rather than deep) location, often with prior evidence of microhemorrhages on susceptibility MRI if available; recurrent in different locations over time is characteristic.

Differential
  • Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (classic in older, normotensive patients)
  • Underlying vascular malformation or tumor
  • Hemorrhagic conversion of an infarct
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurology consultation; blood pressure management and consideration of amyloid angiopathy workup.

9. Intraventricular hemorrhage

Concerning

Blood within the cerebral ventricular system, from extension of parenchymal hemorrhage or primary intraventricular bleeding.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense material layering within the ventricles, often with a fluid-fluid level; can cause obstructive hydrocephalus if significant.

Differential
  • Extension from adjacent parenchymal hemorrhage (most common)
  • Extension from aneurysmal SAH
  • Primary IVH (choroid plexus or periventricular vascular malformation source)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; monitor for hydrocephalus requiring external ventricular drainage.

10. Hemorrhagic contusion

Concerning

Traumatic brain injury with punctate or confluent hemorrhage within contused brain tissue, classically at sites of direct impact or contrecoup injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, mixed hyper- and hypodense foci within the brain parenchyma, most often at the inferior frontal and temporal lobes, which can evolve/expand over the first 24-48 hours ('blossoming').

Differential
  • Traumatic hemorrhagic contusion
Next Step

Neurosurgical/trauma consultation; repeat imaging within 6-24 hours to assess for progression, particularly with anticoagulation.

11. Epidural hematoma, posterior fossa

Concerning

Epidural hemorrhage in the posterior fossa, less common than supratentorial epidural hematomas but with higher risk given limited space for mass effect.

Imaging Characteristics

Biconvex, hyperdense collection in the posterior fossa, often associated with an occipital bone fracture; can rapidly cause brainstem compression or obstructive hydrocephalus given the confined space.

Differential
  • Posterior fossa epidural hematoma (surgical emergency given limited compensatory space)
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical consultation given the high risk of rapid clinical deterioration in this confined compartment.

Brain: Ischemic Stroke

9 findings

1. Hyperdense MCA sign

Concerning

Early CT sign of acute thrombus within the middle cerebral artery, visible before parenchymal changes develop.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, linear hyperdensity within the MCA (M1 segment or a branch) relative to the contralateral side, reflecting acute thrombus.

Differential
  • Acute MCA thrombus/embolus (large vessel occlusion)
  • Vessel calcification (bilateral, symmetric, distinguishing feature)
  • Elevated hematocrit (diffusely hyperdense vessels, bilateral)
Next Step

Time-critical; assess for large vessel occlusion and thrombectomy candidacy.

2. Acute MCA territory infarct, early signs

Concerning

Early parenchymal changes of acute ischemic stroke, often subtle in the first few hours.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of gray-white matter differentiation, insular ribbon sign (loss of normal insular cortex definition), and sulcal effacement in the MCA territory; ASPECTS scoring quantifies the extent of early ischemic change.

Differential
  • Acute MCA territory ischemia
Next Step

Time-critical stroke protocol; assess thrombolysis and thrombectomy eligibility based on time window and imaging.

3. Acute large territory ('malignant') infarct

Concerning

Extensive infarct involving a large vascular territory, at high risk for malignant edema and herniation.

Imaging Characteristics

Extensive loss of gray-white differentiation and sulcal effacement across a large territory (often >1/3 of the MCA territory), with early mass effect.

Differential
  • Large territory ischemic stroke (malignant MCA infarct pattern)
Next Step

Neurocritical care/neurosurgery consultation; monitor closely for malignant edema, consider decompressive hemicraniectomy per established criteria.

4. Lacunar infarct

Variable

Small, deep infarct from occlusion of a single penetrating arteriole, associated with chronic hypertension and small vessel disease.

Imaging Characteristics

Small (typically <15 mm), well-defined hypodensity in the deep gray matter (basal ganglia, thalamus) or internal capsule/pons.

Differential
  • Acute lacunar infarct
  • Chronic lacunar infarct/prior small vessel ischemic change (differentiate by acuity/clinical correlation)
Next Step

Stroke workup per standard protocol; secondary prevention focused on vascular risk factor control.

5. Watershed (border zone) infarct

Variable

Infarct at the border between two vascular territories, classically from hypoperfusion/hypotension rather than embolism.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or wedge-shaped hypodensity along the border zone between the anterior/middle or middle/posterior cerebral artery territories.

Differential
  • Hypoperfusion/hypotensive event (cardiac arrest, severe sepsis, prolonged hypotension)
  • Severe carotid stenosis/occlusion with impaired collateral flow
Next Step

Identify and correct the underlying hemodynamic cause; evaluate for severe carotid disease.

6. Venous infarct

Concerning

Infarct from dural venous sinus or cortical vein thrombosis, often with a non-arterial territory distribution and hemorrhagic component.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypodensity (often with hemorrhagic transformation) that does not conform to a typical arterial territory, frequently bilateral/parasagittal if from superior sagittal sinus thrombosis.

Differential
  • Venous sinus thrombosis-related infarct (correlate with CT venography)
  • Arterial infarct (territory-conforming, key differential)
Next Step

CT or MR venography to confirm venous thrombosis; anticoagulation is the mainstay of treatment despite the hemorrhagic component.

7. Chronic infarct / encephalomalacia

Benign

Old infarct with tissue loss and gliosis, representing remote ischemic injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined area of encephalomalacia (CSF-density tissue loss) with associated volume loss and ex vacuo dilation of the adjacent ventricle/sulci, conforming to a vascular territory.

Differential
  • Remote infarct (most common)
  • Post-surgical or post-traumatic encephalomalacia
Next Step

No acute action; correlate with clinical history and prior imaging if available.

8. Infarct with hemorrhagic transformation

Concerning

Bleeding into a region of infarcted brain tissue, a recognized complication of ischemic stroke, especially after reperfusion therapy.

Imaging Characteristics

Petechial or confluent hyperdensity within a pre-existing area of hypodense infarction.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke (spontaneous or post-thrombolysis/thrombectomy)
Next Step

Neurocritical care consultation; may require holding or reversing antithrombotic therapy depending on severity.

9. CT perfusion mismatch (core/penumbra)

Concerning

Advanced CT technique distinguishing irreversibly infarcted core tissue from salvageable penumbra, used to extend thrombectomy eligibility windows.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced cerebral blood flow with markedly reduced cerebral blood volume defines the infarct core; reduced cerebral blood flow with preserved/increased mean transit time but relatively preserved blood volume defines the penumbra.

Differential
  • Favorable mismatch (small core, large penumbra - favors intervention)
  • Unfavorable pattern (large established core - lower benefit from late intervention)
Next Step

Guides extended-window thrombectomy decision-making per current stroke guidelines.

Brain: Primary Tumors

12 findings

1. Glioblastoma (CT)

Concerning

Most common and most aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, irregularly enhancing mass with central necrosis (hypodense core), surrounding vasogenic edema, and mass effect; often crosses the corpus callosum ('butterfly glioma').

Differential
  • Glioblastoma (most common in older adults)
  • Metastasis (usually multiple, at gray-white junction)
  • Abscess (ring-enhancing, but with restricted diffusion centrally on MRI)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral for biopsy/resection and adjuvant therapy planning.

2. Low-grade glioma (CT)

Concerning

Diffusely infiltrating glioma with a lower grade than glioblastoma, though still requiring oncologic management.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, non-enhancing or minimally enhancing hypodense mass, often in a younger patient, without significant surrounding edema or mass effect.

Differential
  • Low-grade glioma
  • Focal cortical dysplasia (in the appropriate epilepsy context)
  • Chronic infarct (less mass-like)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral; MRI for further characterization and biopsy for grading.

3. Meningioma

Variable

Most common benign extra-axial brain tumor, arising from the meninges.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, extra-axial mass with a broad dural base, often homogeneously hyperdense/enhancing, sometimes with calcification and adjacent hyperostosis of the overlying skull.

Differential
  • Meningioma (most common, typically benign)
  • Dural metastasis (less well-defined dural base, known malignancy history)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for symptomatic or growing lesions; asymptomatic small meningiomas are often observed with serial imaging.

4. Pituitary adenoma

Variable

Tumor of the pituitary gland, classified as microadenoma (<1 cm) or macroadenoma (≥1 cm).

Imaging Characteristics

Sellar mass, often with suprasellar extension and optic chiasm compression in larger lesions; CT is less sensitive than MRI for microadenomas.

Differential
  • Pituitary adenoma (most common sellar mass)
  • Craniopharyngioma
  • Rathke cleft cyst
Next Step

Endocrine and neurosurgical referral; MRI and hormonal workup for further characterization.

5. Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)

Variable

Benign tumor of the vestibulocochlear nerve sheath, classically in the cerebellopontine angle.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, enhancing mass centered on the internal auditory canal, often with canal widening; CT is less sensitive than MRI for small lesions.

Differential
  • Vestibular schwannoma (most common CPA mass)
  • Meningioma (CPA location, broader dural base)
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgical referral; MRI is the study of choice for characterization and surveillance.

6. Craniopharyngioma

Variable

Tumor arising from Rathke pouch remnants, most common in the sellar/suprasellar region, bimodal age distribution (children and older adults).

Imaging Characteristics

Suprasellar mass, often with a cystic component and characteristic calcification, particularly in the pediatric (adamantinomatous) subtype.

Differential
  • Craniopharyngioma (calcification is a helpful distinguishing feature)
  • Pituitary adenoma
  • Rathke cleft cyst
Next Step

Neurosurgical/endocrine referral.

7. Medulloblastoma (pediatric)

Concerning

Most common malignant pediatric brain tumor, arising in the posterior fossa (cerebellum).

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperdense (reflecting high cellularity), midline cerebellar mass, often causing obstructive hydrocephalus from fourth ventricle compression.

Differential
  • Medulloblastoma (classic midline cerebellar location)
  • Pilocytic astrocytoma (often more cystic, better prognosis)
  • Ependymoma (arises from the fourth ventricle floor)
Next Step

Emergent pediatric neurosurgical/oncology referral given hydrocephalus risk and need for prompt management.

8. Ependymoma

Concerning

Tumor arising from ependymal cells lining the ventricles, most common in the fourth ventricle in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass centered within or extending from the fourth ventricle, often with calcification, that can extrude through the foramina of Luschka/Magendie ('plastic ependymoma').

Differential
  • Ependymoma
  • Medulloblastoma
  • Choroid plexus tumor
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgical/oncology referral.

9. CNS lymphoma

Concerning

Primary or secondary lymphoma involving the CNS, with a predilection for periventricular white matter.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneously hyperdense (reflecting high cellularity), often periventricular mass with homogeneous enhancement; classically shows dramatic response to corticosteroids (can transiently disappear, a diagnostic pitfall if steroids given before biopsy).

Differential
  • CNS lymphoma (particularly in immunocompromised patients)
  • Toxoplasmosis (in HIV/AIDS, key differential)
  • Glioblastoma
Next Step

Avoid corticosteroids before biopsy if possible given the risk of masking the diagnosis; neuro-oncology referral.

10. Colloid cyst

Variable

Benign cyst typically located at the foramen of Monro, with risk of acute obstructive hydrocephalus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often hyperdense (proteinaceous content) round lesion at the foramen of Monro, at the anterosuperior third ventricle.

Differential
  • Colloid cyst (classic location and appearance)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; can cause sudden, life-threatening obstructive hydrocephalus and requires prompt evaluation for resection.

11. Hemangioblastoma

Variable

Vascular tumor most often in the cerebellum, associated with von Hippel-Lindau disease in a subset of patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic mass with an enhancing mural nodule, most often in the cerebellum; multiple lesions suggest von Hippel-Lindau disease.

Differential
  • Hemangioblastoma
  • Pilocytic astrocytoma (similar cystic-with-mural-nodule appearance)
  • Metastasis
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; screen for von Hippel-Lindau disease if multiple or in a young patient.

12. Dermoid/epidermoid cyst (brain)

Benign

Congenital inclusion cyst, benign but can cause mass effect or chemical meningitis if it ruptures.

Imaging Characteristics

Epidermoid: well-defined, CSF-density (but with restricted diffusion on MRI, unlike a simple arachnoid cyst), often in the cerebellopontine angle. Dermoid: may contain fat and calcification, often midline.

Differential
  • Epidermoid cyst (restricted diffusion is the key distinguishing MRI feature from arachnoid cyst)
  • Dermoid cyst (fat-containing, midline)
  • Arachnoid cyst
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for symptomatic lesions; rupture of a dermoid can cause chemical meningitis requiring recognition.

Brain: Metastatic & Other Mass Lesions

6 findings

1. Brain metastases

Concerning

Secondary malignant brain lesions, most often from lung, breast, melanoma, renal cell, and colorectal primaries.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple (though can be solitary), well-defined lesions at the gray-white junction, typically with surrounding vasogenic edema disproportionate to lesion size and ring or nodular enhancement.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (multiplicity and gray-white junction location favor this)
  • Abscess
  • Primary glioma (if solitary)
Next Step

Oncology/neuro-oncology referral for staging and treatment planning (surgery, radiation, systemic therapy).

2. Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis

Concerning

Diffuse malignant infiltration of the leptomeninges, from a systemic or CNS primary malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or nodular leptomeningeal enhancement along the sulci and cranial nerves, sometimes subtle on CT and better seen on contrast-enhanced MRI.

Differential
  • Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis
  • Infectious/inflammatory meningitis (key differential requiring CSF correlation)
Next Step

Oncology referral; CSF cytology and MRI for confirmation given the poor prognosis and need for accurate diagnosis.

3. Arachnoid cyst

Benign

Congenital CSF-filled cyst between the arachnoid membrane layers, common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, CSF-density (non-enhancing) extra-axial collection, most often in the middle cranial fossa, without restricted diffusion (distinguishing it from an epidermoid cyst).

Differential
  • Arachnoid cyst (most common)
  • Epidermoid cyst (restricted diffusion on MRI, key distinguishing feature)
Next Step

No treatment needed for an incidental, asymptomatic arachnoid cyst; neurosurgical referral if causing mass effect or symptoms.

4. Pineal cyst

Benign

Common benign cystic lesion of the pineal gland.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, CSF-density or slightly higher-attenuation cystic lesion in the pineal region, typically <1-1.5 cm.

Differential
  • Pineal cyst (common incidental finding)
  • Pineal tumor (larger, more solid, warrants further characterization if atypical)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a small, classic pineal cyst; further workup if large or atypical features present.

5. Choroid plexus papilloma

Variable

Benign tumor of the choroid plexus, most common in children, associated with hydrocephalus from CSF overproduction.

Imaging Characteristics

Lobulated, enhancing intraventricular mass, most often in the lateral ventricle in children (fourth ventricle more common in adults), often with associated hydrocephalus.

Differential
  • Choroid plexus papilloma (benign, more common)
  • Choroid plexus carcinoma (more aggressive, invasive features)
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgical referral for resection.

6. Rathke cleft cyst

Benign

Benign, congenital sellar cyst arising from Rathke pouch remnants.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, non-enhancing cystic sellar or suprasellar lesion, typically without the calcification seen in craniopharyngioma.

Differential
  • Rathke cleft cyst
  • Craniopharyngioma (calcification favors this)
  • Cystic pituitary adenoma
Next Step

Endocrine/neurosurgical referral if symptomatic (mass effect, hormonal dysfunction).

Brain: Hydrocephalus & CSF Disorders

6 findings

1. Obstructive (non-communicating) hydrocephalus

Concerning

Ventricular dilation from a mechanical obstruction to CSF flow at a specific point.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilation of the ventricular system proximal to the obstruction (e.g., all lateral and third ventricles dilated with a normal fourth ventricle in aqueductal stenosis), with periventricular hypodensity from transependymal CSF flow in acute cases.

Differential
  • Aqueductal stenosis
  • Mass lesion obstructing CSF pathways (colloid cyst, posterior fossa tumor)
  • Intraventricular hemorrhage with clot obstruction
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; may require urgent CSF diversion (external ventricular drain) if acute and symptomatic.

2. Communicating hydrocephalus

Concerning

Ventricular dilation from impaired CSF resorption rather than a mechanical obstruction, with all ventricles typically dilated proportionally.

Imaging Characteristics

Proportional dilation of all four ventricles, including the fourth ventricle (distinguishing it from obstructive patterns), often with periventricular hypodensity if acute/decompensated.

Differential
  • Post-hemorrhagic (impaired arachnoid granulation resorption)
  • Post-infectious/post-meningitic
  • Idiopathic
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; may require CSF diversion (shunt) if symptomatic.

3. Normal pressure hydrocephalus

Variable

Chronic communicating hydrocephalus in older adults, classically presenting with the triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and cognitive decline.

Imaging Characteristics

Ventriculomegaly disproportionate to sulcal atrophy, with a callosal angle typically <90 degrees and often disproportionately enlarged Sylvian fissures relative to the vertex sulci (DESH pattern).

Differential
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus (classic clinical triad)
  • Age-related ventriculomegaly/atrophy (proportionate sulcal enlargement, distinguishing feature)
Next Step

Neurology/neurosurgery referral; large-volume lumbar puncture as a diagnostic/prognostic test before shunt consideration.

4. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, CT signs

Variable

Elevated intracranial pressure without an identifiable structural cause, classically in young obese women.

Imaging Characteristics

Slit-like ventricles, an empty or partially empty sella, and flattening of the posterior globes may be seen, though CT findings can be subtle or normal.

Differential
  • Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)
  • Dural venous sinus thrombosis (important to exclude, as it can mimic or cause IIH)
Next Step

CT or MR venography to exclude venous sinus thrombosis; ophthalmology and neurology referral.

5. Chiari I malformation, CT

Variable

Downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum, often incidental but occasionally symptomatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Cerebellar tonsils extending ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum (threshold varies somewhat by age and source), sometimes with an associated syrinx of the cervical spinal cord.

Differential
  • Chiari I malformation
  • Tonsillar herniation from elevated ICP (acute, symptomatic context, key differential)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral if symptomatic (headache, cerebellar/brainstem symptoms) or associated with a syrinx; MRI for full characterization.

6. Ventriculomegaly ex vacuo

Benign

Ventricular enlargement from brain volume loss (atrophy) rather than a CSF flow disturbance.

Imaging Characteristics

Proportional enlargement of the ventricles and sulci together, reflecting generalized parenchymal volume loss rather than active hydrocephalus.

Differential
  • Age-related atrophy
  • Neurodegenerative disease-related atrophy
Next Step

No specific hydrocephalus workup needed; correlate with cognitive/functional status and consider neurodegenerative disease workup if clinically indicated.

Brain: Trauma - Skull & Extra-axial

8 findings

1. Linear skull fracture

Variable

Simple, non-displaced fracture through the calvarium.

Imaging Characteristics

Sharp, lucent line through the skull without displacement, typically following a straight or slightly curved course (distinguishing it from a normal suture).

Differential
  • Linear skull fracture
  • Normal suture (compare to expected suture locations and symmetry)
  • Vascular groove (smoothly marginated, branching pattern)
Next Step

Assess for associated intracranial injury; most isolated linear fractures without underlying injury are managed conservatively.

2. Depressed skull fracture

Concerning

Fracture with inward displacement of a bone fragment, higher risk of underlying brain injury or dural tear.

Imaging Characteristics

Inward displacement of a skull fragment relative to the adjacent normal calvarial contour, best assessed on bone windows and 3D reconstructions.

Differential
  • Depressed skull fracture (open or closed)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; open/compound depressed fractures typically require surgical elevation and debridement.

3. Basilar skull fracture

Concerning

Fracture involving the skull base, associated with characteristic clinical signs (raccoon eyes, Battle sign, CSF leak) and higher complication risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the skull base (petrous temporal bone, sphenoid, occipital bone), sometimes with associated pneumocephalus, opacification of the mastoid air cells, or a visible fracture through the carotid canal.

Differential
  • Basilar skull fracture (assess for carotid canal, jugular foramen, and internal auditory canal involvement)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/ENT consultation; assess for CSF leak, cranial nerve injury, and vascular injury (CT angiography if fracture involves the carotid canal).

4. Pneumocephalus

Concerning

Air within the intracranial cavity, from a skull fracture, sinus fracture, or post-surgical communication.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent (air-density) collection within the epidural, subdural, subarachnoid, or intraparenchymal space; large volumes can show a 'Mount Fuji sign' with bifrontal air compressing the frontal lobes.

Differential
  • Traumatic pneumocephalus (skull/sinus fracture with dural tear)
  • Post-surgical pneumocephalus (expected in the immediate postoperative period)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; assess for an active CSF leak and tension pneumocephalus (rare but can require urgent decompression).

5. Growing skull fracture (pediatric)

Concerning

Rare complication of a pediatric skull fracture with an associated dural tear, where a leptomeningeal cyst progressively widens the fracture over time.

Imaging Characteristics

Progressively widening lucent fracture line on serial imaging, often with a soft-tissue mass (leptomeningeal cyst) herniating through the defect.

Differential
  • Growing skull fracture (typically presents weeks to months after the initial injury in a young child)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for surgical repair of the dural defect.

6. Temporal bone fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the temporal bone, classified as longitudinal, transverse, or mixed relative to the petrous ridge, with different complication profiles.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture line through the temporal bone; longitudinal fractures (more common) often spare the otic capsule with higher conductive hearing loss/ossicular injury risk, while transverse fractures more often involve the otic capsule with higher sensorineural hearing loss and facial nerve injury risk.

Differential
  • Longitudinal temporal bone fracture
  • Transverse temporal bone fracture
  • Mixed pattern
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgical consultation; assess for facial nerve injury, hearing loss, and CSF otorrhea.

7. Orbital blowout fracture

Variable

Fracture of the orbital floor or medial wall from blunt trauma, with risk of extraocular muscle entrapment.

Imaging Characteristics

Fracture of the thin orbital floor (or medial wall) with herniation of orbital fat and/or the inferior rectus muscle into the maxillary sinus, sometimes with a 'teardrop sign.'

Differential
  • Orbital blowout fracture with entrapment (surgical emergency, especially in children with a 'white-eyed' trapdoor fracture)
  • Orbital blowout fracture without entrapment
Next Step

Ophthalmology/ENT/oculoplastics referral; urgent surgical consultation if muscle entrapment with restricted extraocular movements or a trapdoor fracture in a child.

8. Facial fracture complex (LeFort pattern)

Concerning

Complex facial fracture patterns from high-energy trauma, classified by the level of the fracture through the midface.

Imaging Characteristics

LeFort I: fracture through the maxilla above the tooth apices. LeFort II: pyramidal fracture through the maxilla, nasal bones, and medial orbits. LeFort III: craniofacial dissociation through the zygomaticofrontal sutures and orbits.

Differential
  • LeFort I, II, or III fracture pattern (patterns often mixed/asymmetric in practice)
Next Step

Oral maxillofacial surgery/ENT referral; assess airway and for associated skull base/intracranial injury given the high-energy mechanism.

Brain: Trauma - Diffuse Axonal Injury & Contusion

5 findings

1. Diffuse axonal injury

Concerning

Widespread axonal shearing injury from rapid acceleration-deceleration forces, often with clinical severity out of proportion to initial CT findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Small punctate hemorrhages at the gray-white matter junction, corpus callosum, and dorsolateral brainstem; CT is often initially normal or shows only subtle findings despite significant clinical impairment, and MRI (particularly susceptibility-weighted imaging) is far more sensitive.

Differential
  • Diffuse axonal injury (classic locations: gray-white junction, corpus callosum, brainstem)
Next Step

Neurocritical care management; MRI for more sensitive detection if clinical severity exceeds CT findings.

2. Cerebral contusion

Concerning

Bruising of the brain parenchyma from direct impact, classically at the inferior frontal and anterior temporal lobes.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous area of edema and petechial hemorrhage at the site of impact (coup) or opposite the impact site (contrecoup), most often inferior frontal/temporal given adjacent bony skull base irregularities.

Differential
  • Cerebral contusion (coup or contrecoup)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/trauma consultation; serial imaging given risk of progression/'blossoming' over the first 24-48 hours.

3. Coup-contrecoup injury pattern

Concerning

Injury occurring both at the site of direct impact (coup) and the opposite side of the brain (contrecoup) from the brain rebounding within the skull.

Imaging Characteristics

Contusions/hemorrhage at both the site of direct impact and the diametrically opposite brain surface.

Differential
  • Coup-contrecoup traumatic brain injury
Next Step

Neurosurgical/trauma consultation; assess overall injury burden and monitor for progression.

4. Traumatic subdural hygroma

Variable

CSF collection in the subdural space from an arachnoid tear, distinct from a hematoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic, CSF-density (rather than hyperdense) collection in the subdural space, similar in shape to a subdural hematoma but without the hyperdensity of acute blood.

Differential
  • Traumatic subdural hygroma
  • Chronic subdural hematoma (differentiate by density and clinical timeline)
  • Cerebral atrophy with prominent subdural space (less mass effect)
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; neurosurgical referral if enlarging or symptomatic.

5. Chronic traumatic encephalopathy-related changes

Variable

Imaging changes associated with repetitive head trauma, though CTE itself remains a pathologic (not radiologic) diagnosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Nonspecific findings may include cavum septum pellucidum, generalized atrophy, and prior microhemorrhage burden if MRI is available; CT findings are generally nonspecific.

Differential
  • Nonspecific findings associated with repetitive head trauma history
  • Other causes of atrophy/cavum septum pellucidum
Next Step

Neurology referral; CTE diagnosis currently requires neuropathologic confirmation and cannot be made from imaging alone.

Brain: Infection & Abscess

6 findings

1. Cerebral abscess

Concerning

Localized intracranial infection with a mature capsule, from hematogenous spread or contiguous infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Ring-enhancing lesion with a relatively thin, smooth capsule (typically thinner medially) and surrounding vasogenic edema; central restricted diffusion on MRI (if available) helps distinguish from a necrotic tumor.

Differential
  • Cerebral abscess (thinner, smoother ring than tumor typically)
  • Necrotic glioblastoma or metastasis
  • Resolving hematoma
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation for possible aspiration/drainage and targeted antibiotics; identify the source (sinus, ear, hematogenous, dental).

2. Meningitis, CT signs

Concerning

Infection/inflammation of the meninges; CT is often normal in uncomplicated meningitis and is primarily used to exclude mass effect before lumbar puncture.

Imaging Characteristics

CT is frequently normal in early/uncomplicated meningitis; findings when present may include sulcal effacement, ventriculomegaly, or abnormal meningeal enhancement (better seen with contrast).

Differential
  • Bacterial meningitis
  • Viral/aseptic meningitis
  • Normal CT does not exclude meningitis
Next Step

Do not delay lumbar puncture and empiric antibiotics for imaging unless there are specific indications (focal deficit, papilledema, immunocompromise, seizure) suggesting a need to exclude mass effect first.

3. Ventriculitis

Concerning

Infection of the ventricular ependymal lining, often a complication of meningitis, ventricular shunt/drain infection, or ruptured abscess.

Imaging Characteristics

Ependymal enhancement lining the ventricles, sometimes with debris/pus layering within the ventricular system and associated hydrocephalus.

Differential
  • Ventriculitis (often associated with an infected CSF shunt/drain or ruptured abscess)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation for shunt/drain management if present; targeted antibiotics per CSF culture.

4. Subdural or epidural empyema

Concerning

Purulent collection in the subdural or epidural space, from contiguous spread (sinusitis, otitis) or hematogenous infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic, rim-enhancing collection in the subdural or epidural space, often with restricted diffusion (on MRI) distinguishing it from a sterile effusion; frequently associated with adjacent sinus or mastoid opacification.

Differential
  • Subdural/epidural empyema (surgical emergency)
  • Sterile subdural effusion/hygroma
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical consultation for drainage plus targeted antibiotics; identify and treat the contiguous source.

5. Encephalitis (HSV pattern)

Concerning

Viral brain infection, classically herpes simplex virus with a characteristic temporal lobe predilection.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypodensity and swelling involving the temporal lobe(s) and insular cortex, often asymmetric, sometimes with petechial hemorrhage; CT can be normal early in the disease course.

Differential
  • Herpes simplex encephalitis (classic temporal lobe involvement, medical emergency)
  • Other viral/autoimmune encephalitis
  • Ischemic infarct (territory-conforming pattern helps distinguish)
Next Step

Empiric IV acyclovir should not be delayed for imaging or CSF results given the high morbidity/mortality of untreated HSV encephalitis; MRI is more sensitive if CT is unrevealing.

6. Neurocysticercosis

Variable

Parasitic CNS infection from Taenia solium larvae, endemic in parts of Latin America, Asia, and Africa, and a common cause of seizures in endemic areas.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small cystic lesions in various stages: vesicular (cystic with a visible scolex), colloidal vesicular (surrounded by edema, the most symptomatic stage), and calcified (nodular, the end stage).

Differential
  • Neurocysticercosis (stage-dependent appearance)
  • Other calcified granulomas (tuberculomas, toxoplasmosis)
Next Step

Neurology/infectious disease referral; treatment approach depends on cyst stage and number, with antiparasitic therapy sometimes contraindicated in certain scenarios given inflammatory risk.

Brain: Herniation Syndromes

6 findings

1. Subfalcine herniation

Concerning

Displacement of brain tissue (typically the cingulate gyrus) under the falx cerebri, from unilateral mass effect.

Imaging Characteristics

Midline shift of the septum pellucidum and lateral ventricles away from the side of the mass lesion, with the cingulate gyrus displaced beneath the falx.

Differential
  • Mass effect from hemorrhage, tumor, or edema
Next Step

Neurosurgical emergency evaluation; identify and treat the underlying mass lesion.

2. Uncal (transtentorial) herniation

Concerning

Displacement of the medial temporal lobe (uncus) through the tentorial incisura, classically causing ipsilateral third nerve palsy from oculomotor nerve compression.

Imaging Characteristics

Medial displacement of the uncus/parahippocampal gyrus into the suprasellar cistern, with effacement of the ipsilateral suprasellar cistern and often compression of the ipsilateral cerebral peduncle against the tentorium (Kernohan notch).

Differential
  • Uncal herniation from an expanding supratentorial mass
Next Step

Neurosurgical emergency; immediate intervention (mass decompression, CSF diversion, hyperosmolar therapy) to prevent progression to brainstem compression.

3. Central (downward) transtentorial herniation

Concerning

Symmetric downward displacement of the diencephalon and midbrain through the tentorial incisura, from diffuse or bilateral mass effect.

Imaging Characteristics

Effacement of the suprasellar and perimesencephalic cisterns with downward displacement of the brainstem, often accompanied by loss of the normal basal cistern anatomy.

Differential
  • Diffuse cerebral edema
  • Bilateral mass lesions
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurocritical care emergency; aggressive ICP management.

4. Tonsillar herniation

Concerning

Downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum, risking brainstem/respiratory center compression.

Imaging Characteristics

Cerebellar tonsils displaced below the foramen magnum with effacement of the surrounding CSF spaces, distinguishing acute herniation from a chronic, asymptomatic Chiari I malformation.

Differential
  • Acute tonsillar herniation from elevated ICP (posterior fossa mass, diffuse edema)
  • Chronic Chiari I malformation (stable, often asymptomatic or with chronic symptoms)
Next Step

Neurosurgical emergency; lumbar puncture is contraindicated given the risk of precipitating further herniation.

5. Upward (cerebellar) herniation

Concerning

Upward displacement of the cerebellum through the tentorial incisura, from a posterior fossa mass or after rapid supratentorial CSF diversion.

Imaging Characteristics

Effacement of the quadrigeminal cistern with upward bulging of the superior cerebellum/vermis through the tentorial incisura.

Differential
  • Posterior fossa mass lesion
  • Rapid ventricular decompression from a supratentorial shunt/EVD (iatrogenic)
Next Step

Neurosurgical emergency; caution with rapid CSF drainage in the setting of a posterior fossa mass.

6. External (post-craniectomy) herniation

Variable

Brain tissue bulging outward through a craniectomy defect, expected to some degree after decompressive surgery but concerning if excessive.

Imaging Characteristics

Brain parenchyma protruding beyond the margins of a craniectomy defect, assessed for degree and any associated venous compromise at the bone edge.

Differential
  • Expected post-decompressive craniectomy swelling
  • Excessive herniation with venous compromise at the bone edge (a recognized complication)
Next Step

Neurosurgical follow-up; significant or worsening external herniation warrants prompt re-evaluation.

Brain: Vascular - Aneurysm, AVM & Fistula

7 findings

1. Cerebral aneurysm, unruptured (CTA)

Variable

Focal outpouching of a cerebral artery, most often at branch points of the circle of Willis, detected incidentally or during workup.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, rounded or saccular outpouching from a cerebral artery on CT angiography, most commonly at the anterior communicating artery, posterior communicating artery origin, or MCA bifurcation.

Differential
  • Unruptured saccular aneurysm (risk stratified by size, location, and morphology)
  • Infundibulum (normal funnel-shaped widening at a vessel origin, key benign mimic)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurointerventional referral for risk-benefit discussion regarding observation versus treatment (coiling/clipping) based on size, location, and patient factors.

2. Ruptured cerebral aneurysm with SAH

Concerning

Aneurysm rupture causing subarachnoid hemorrhage, a neurosurgical emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Aneurysm identified on CTA in a patient with SAH, with the pattern of blood often helping localize the ruptured aneurysm (e.g., blood concentrated in one Sylvian fissure suggesting an ipsilateral MCA aneurysm).

Differential
  • Ruptured aneurysm (requires urgent securing)
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical/neurointerventional consultation for aneurysm securing (clipping or coiling) and management of associated complications (vasospasm, hydrocephalus, rebleeding risk).

3. Arteriovenous malformation (AVM)

Concerning

Congenital tangle of abnormal vessels with direct arteriovenous shunting, bypassing a capillary bed, with hemorrhage risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Tangle of abnormally dilated, tortuous vessels ('bag of worms') with feeding arteries and early draining veins, best characterized on CT angiography.

Differential
  • Brain AVM (risk of hemorrhage, particularly with deep venous drainage or an associated aneurysm)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurointerventional referral for risk assessment and treatment planning (surgery, embolization, radiosurgery).

4. Cavernous malformation (cavernoma)

Variable

Vascular malformation composed of abnormal, low-flow vascular channels without intervening normal brain tissue, better characterized by MRI than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Often subtle or occult on CT; may show a well-defined, sometimes calcified lesion, though susceptibility-weighted MRI is far more sensitive ('popcorn' appearance).

Differential
  • Cavernous malformation
  • Old hemorrhage of another etiology
Next Step

MRI for definitive characterization; neurosurgical referral if symptomatic (seizures, hemorrhage) given the risk of recurrent bleeding.

5. Dural arteriovenous fistula

Concerning

Abnormal direct connection between a dural arterial branch and a venous sinus or cortical vein, acquired rather than congenital.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal early filling of a dural venous sinus or cortical vein on CT angiography, sometimes with associated venous sinus stenosis/thrombosis.

Differential
  • Dural AV fistula (aggressive features include cortical venous reflux, associated with higher hemorrhage risk)
Next Step

Neurointerventional referral; aggressive fistulas with cortical venous drainage warrant more urgent treatment given hemorrhage risk.

6. Vasospasm, post-subarachnoid hemorrhage

Concerning

Delayed narrowing of cerebral vessels after aneurysmal SAH, a leading cause of delayed cerebral ischemia, typically occurring days 3-14 post-bleed.

Imaging Characteristics

Segmental or diffuse narrowing of the intracranial arteries on CT angiography, correlating with the distribution of subarachnoid blood.

Differential
  • Vasospasm following aneurysmal SAH (classic timing days 3-14)
Next Step

Neurocritical care management (induced hypertension, consideration of intra-arterial therapy); serial monitoring given the delayed and evolving nature of vasospasm.

7. Carotid-cavernous fistula

Concerning

Abnormal connection between the carotid artery and the cavernous sinus, from trauma or spontaneous rupture of an intracavernous aneurysm.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, early-filling cavernous sinus and superior ophthalmic vein on CTA, often with proptosis and orbital congestion.

Differential
  • Direct (high-flow, often traumatic) carotid-cavernous fistula
  • Indirect (dural, lower-flow) fistula
Next Step

Neurointerventional referral for embolization, particularly for direct high-flow fistulas given rapid visual/neurologic decline risk.

Brain: Vascular - Venous

4 findings

1. Dural venous sinus thrombosis

Concerning

Clot within a dural venous sinus, risking venous infarction and hemorrhage.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect within a dural venous sinus on CT venography; the 'empty delta sign' describes a filling defect in the superior sagittal sinus on contrast-enhanced axial images.

Differential
  • Dural venous sinus thrombosis (superior sagittal sinus, transverse sinus most common)
Next Step

Anticoagulation is generally indicated even in the presence of associated hemorrhage; neurology/neurointerventional referral for severe or refractory cases.

2. Cortical vein thrombosis

Concerning

Thrombosis of a cortical vein, which can occur with or without associated dural sinus thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Filling defect or non-visualization of an expected cortical vein on CT venography, often with adjacent parenchymal edema or hemorrhage.

Differential
  • Isolated cortical vein thrombosis
  • Associated with dural sinus thrombosis
Next Step

Anticoagulation; neurology referral for hypercoagulability workup.

3. Venous infarct with hemorrhage

Concerning

Infarction from venous outflow obstruction, frequently hemorrhagic given the congestive rather than purely ischemic mechanism.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypodensity with hemorrhagic components that does not conform to an arterial territory, often bilateral/parasagittal with superior sagittal sinus thrombosis or temporal with transverse sinus thrombosis.

Differential
  • Venous infarct secondary to sinus/cortical vein thrombosis
Next Step

Anticoagulation remains indicated despite the hemorrhagic component; neurocritical care management.

4. Empty delta sign

Concerning

Specific CT venography sign of superior sagittal sinus thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Triangular filling defect (the thrombus) surrounded by enhancing dural collateral channels within the posterior superior sagittal sinus on contrast-enhanced axial images.

Differential
  • Superior sagittal sinus thrombosis
Next Step

Confirms the diagnosis; anticoagulation and neurology referral.

Brain: Congenital & Developmental

6 findings

1. Agenesis of the corpus callosum

Variable

Congenital absence (complete or partial) of the corpus callosum, the primary white matter tract connecting the cerebral hemispheres.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent or partial corpus callosum with characteristic secondary findings: widely separated, parallel ('racing car sign') lateral ventricles and colpocephaly (disproportionate enlargement of the occipital horns).

Differential
  • Complete or partial agenesis of the corpus callosum
  • Associated syndromic conditions (variable)
Next Step

Neurology/genetics referral; correlate with developmental status and screen for associated anomalies.

2. Dandy-Walker malformation

Concerning

Congenital posterior fossa malformation with cerebellar vermian hypoplasia/agenesis and fourth ventricle cystic dilation.

Imaging Characteristics

Large posterior fossa cyst continuous with a dilated fourth ventricle, cerebellar vermian hypoplasia or agenesis, and often an enlarged posterior fossa with elevated torcular herophili.

Differential
  • Dandy-Walker malformation (classic triad)
  • Mega cisterna magna (normal vermis, no fourth ventricle communication, key distinguishing feature)
  • Blake pouch cyst
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgery/neurology referral; associated hydrocephalus is common and may require CSF diversion.

3. Septo-optic dysplasia

Concerning

Congenital condition with absence of the septum pellucidum, optic nerve hypoplasia, and often pituitary dysfunction.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent septum pellucidum with squared-off, 'box-like' frontal horns of the lateral ventricles, sometimes with associated optic nerve/chiasm hypoplasia and pituitary abnormalities.

Differential
  • Septo-optic dysplasia (classic triad, though not all features are always present)
Next Step

Pediatric endocrine/ophthalmology/neurology referral for hormonal and visual assessment.

4. Cortical dysplasia / malformation of cortical development

Concerning

Abnormal cortical development, an important structural cause of epilepsy, better characterized by MRI than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Often subtle or occult on CT; may show focal cortical thickening or an abnormal gyral pattern in more severe cases, though dedicated epilepsy-protocol MRI is far more sensitive.

Differential
  • Focal cortical dysplasia
  • Other malformations of cortical development (polymicrogyria, lissencephaly, heterotopia)
Next Step

Epilepsy-protocol MRI and neurology/epileptology referral, particularly in the setting of drug-resistant focal epilepsy.

5. Colpocephaly

Variable

Disproportionate enlargement of the occipital horns of the lateral ventricles, often associated with corpus callosum agenesis or periventricular white matter injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged occipital horns of the lateral ventricles disproportionate to the frontal horns.

Differential
  • Associated with corpus callosum agenesis
  • Associated with periventricular leukomalacia/white matter injury
Next Step

Correlate with associated findings and developmental history; pediatric neurology referral.

6. Hydranencephaly

Concerning

Near-complete absence of the cerebral hemispheres, replaced by CSF, with the brainstem and cerebellum typically preserved.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, CSF-filled supratentorial space replacing the cerebral hemispheres, with a preserved falx and typically intact brainstem/cerebellum/thalami.

Differential
  • Hydranencephaly (thought to result from severe bilateral in utero vascular insult)
  • Severe hydrocephalus (some residual cortical mantle typically remains, distinguishing feature)
  • Alobar holoprosencephaly (fused thalami, absent falx, distinguishing feature)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology referral; carries a poor neurodevelopmental prognosis.

Brain: Atrophy & Degenerative

5 findings

1. Generalized cerebral atrophy

Variable

Diffuse volume loss of the brain parenchyma, from normal aging or an underlying neurodegenerative process.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse sulcal widening and ventricular enlargement proportionate to the degree of parenchymal volume loss.

Differential
  • Age-appropriate atrophy
  • Neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer disease and others)
  • Prior global insult (hypoxic-ischemic injury, chronic substance use)
Next Step

Correlate with cognitive status and age-appropriateness; neurology referral if atrophy is disproportionate to age or accompanied by cognitive symptoms.

2. Hippocampal atrophy

Concerning

Focal volume loss of the hippocampus, associated with Alzheimer disease and mesial temporal sclerosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal volume loss of the hippocampus, better assessed on dedicated MRI than CT; asymmetric involvement raises concern for mesial temporal sclerosis in the epilepsy context.

Differential
  • Alzheimer disease (typically bilateral, symmetric)
  • Mesial temporal sclerosis (often asymmetric, epilepsy context)
Next Step

MRI with dedicated hippocampal imaging and neurology referral.

3. Cerebellar atrophy

Variable

Focal volume loss of the cerebellum, from a range of degenerative, toxic, and paraneoplastic causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened cerebellar folia and fissures with associated fourth ventricle and cerebellopontine cistern enlargement.

Differential
  • Chronic alcohol use (classic anterior superior vermian predominance)
  • Spinocerebellar ataxia and other genetic ataxias
  • Paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration
  • Medication toxicity (phenytoin)
Next Step

Neurology referral for etiology workup based on the clinical context.

4. Basal ganglia calcification (Fahr disease pattern)

Variable

Symmetric calcification of the basal ganglia and other deep gray structures, from a range of metabolic and idiopathic causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, bilateral calcification of the basal ganglia (particularly the globus pallidus), often with additional involvement of the thalami, dentate nuclei, and subcortical white matter in more extensive disease.

Differential
  • Idiopathic (age-related, common and often benign in older adults)
  • Hypoparathyroidism/pseudohypoparathyroidism (important metabolic cause to exclude)
  • Primary familial brain calcification (Fahr disease)
Next Step

Correlate with calcium/parathyroid hormone levels, particularly in younger patients or with extensive calcification.

5. Leukoaraiosis / chronic small vessel ischemic change

Variable

Chronic white matter changes from small vessel disease, extremely common with age and vascular risk factors.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, periventricular and/or subcortical hypodensity, sparing the subcortical U-fibers, correlating with chronic microvascular ischemic disease.

Differential
  • Chronic hypertensive/small vessel ischemic disease (most common)
  • Normal age-related change (mild degree)
Next Step

Correlate with vascular risk factor management; not typically an indication for acute intervention.

Brain: Incidental & Miscellaneous

9 findings

1. Arachnoid granulation

Benign

Normal CSF-absorbing structure that can appear as a filling defect within a dural venous sinus, a common mimic of sinus thrombosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, round, CSF-density filling defect within a dural venous sinus (most often the transverse sinus), distinct from the more irregular filling defect of thrombosis.

Differential
  • Normal arachnoid granulation (common, benign)
  • Dural venous sinus thrombosis (key differential, distinguished by CSF density and smooth margins of a granulation versus the thrombus appearance)
Next Step

No action needed for a classic arachnoid granulation.

2. Choroid plexus calcification

Benign

Physiologic calcification of the choroid plexus, extremely common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, bilateral calcification within the atria of the lateral ventricles (glomus of the choroid plexus).

Differential
  • Normal age-related physiologic calcification
Next Step

No action needed.

3. Pineal gland calcification/cyst

Benign

Physiologic calcification of the pineal gland, extremely common with age, sometimes with an associated small cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Calcification within the pineal gland, typically <1 cm; larger lesions or those without calcification warrant assessment for an underlying pineal cyst or tumor.

Differential
  • Physiologic pineal calcification (most common)
  • Pineal cyst (if a cystic component is present)
Next Step

No action needed for typical small physiologic calcification.

4. Cavum septum pellucidum / cavum vergae

Benign

Normal variant CSF-filled cavity between the leaflets of the septum pellucidum, common in infants and often persisting into adulthood.

Imaging Characteristics

CSF-density, midline cavity between the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles (cavum septum pellucidum) with posterior extension (cavum vergae) in some cases.

Differential
  • Normal anatomic variant (very common, especially in infants)
Next Step

No action needed; recognize as a normal variant, not a pathologic cyst.

5. Empty sella

Variable

Flattened or absent pituitary gland with CSF filling the sella turcica, from an incompetent diaphragma sellae.

Imaging Characteristics

CSF-density material filling the sella turcica with a flattened, thin pituitary gland along the sellar floor.

Differential
  • Primary empty sella (idiopathic, often incidental, associated with obesity/idiopathic intracranial hypertension)
  • Secondary empty sella (post-surgical, post-radiation, post-infarction of a pituitary tumor)
Next Step

Correlate with pituitary function if clinically indicated; usually incidental and asymptomatic.

6. Mega cisterna magna

Benign

Enlarged cisterna magna, a normal variant distinct from Dandy-Walker malformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged CSF space in the posterior fossa below and behind the cerebellum, with a normal cerebellar vermis and fourth ventricle (key distinguishing features from Dandy-Walker malformation).

Differential
  • Mega cisterna magna (normal variant)
  • Dandy-Walker malformation (abnormal vermis, distinguishing feature)
  • Posterior fossa arachnoid cyst
Next Step

No action needed for a classic mega cisterna magna with normal vermis.

7. Developmental venous anomaly

Benign

Congenital variant venous drainage pattern, the most common cerebral vascular malformation, essentially always benign.

Imaging Characteristics

Cluster of small medullary veins converging into a larger collecting vein ('caput medusae' pattern), best seen with contrast.

Differential
  • Developmental venous anomaly (benign variant, essentially never requires treatment in isolation)
  • Associated cavernous malformation (occurs with increased frequency adjacent to a DVA and carries the actual hemorrhage risk)
Next Step

No treatment needed for the DVA itself; note if an adjacent cavernous malformation is also present, as that carries the hemorrhage risk.

8. Persistent trigeminal artery

Benign

Congenital persistence of an embryonic connection between the internal carotid and basilar arteries, the most common persistent fetal carotid-vertebrobasilar anastomosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Direct vascular connection coursing from the cavernous internal carotid artery to the basilar artery, bypassing the normal posterior communicating artery pathway.

Differential
  • Persistent trigeminal artery (normal variant, usually incidental)
Next Step

No action needed for an isolated finding; noted as relevant if planning any skull base or endovascular procedure in the region.

9. Fenestrated cerebral vessel

Benign

Congenital duplication/splitting of a segment of a cerebral vessel, most common at the anterior communicating artery or basilar artery.

Imaging Characteristics

Duplicated, parallel channel of a vessel segment that reunites distally, seen on CT angiography.

Differential
  • Vessel fenestration (normal variant)
  • Association with a higher prevalence of adjacent aneurysm (worth noting, though the fenestration itself is benign)
Next Step

No action needed for the fenestration itself; carefully assess for an associated aneurysm at the fenestration site.

Spine: Trauma - Fractures

12 findings

1. Odontoid (dens) fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the odontoid process of C2, classified by the Anderson-D'Alonzo system based on the fracture level.

Imaging Characteristics

Type I: fracture through the tip of the odontoid (stable, rare). Type II: fracture through the base/waist of the odontoid (most common, unstable, high nonunion risk). Type III: fracture extending into the C2 vertebral body (typically stable, better healing potential).

Differential
  • Type I odontoid fracture
  • Type II odontoid fracture (highest clinical significance)
  • Type III odontoid fracture
  • Os odontoideum (chronic, smooth-margined, key differential from acute fracture)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; management (collar, halo, or surgical fixation) depends on fracture type and displacement.

2. Jefferson fracture (C1 burst fracture)

Concerning

Burst fracture of the C1 (atlas) ring, classically from an axial loading mechanism, often with multiple fracture fragments.

Imaging Characteristics

Fractures through both the anterior and posterior arches of C1, with lateral mass displacement; the combined lateral mass overhang on the odontoid view helps assess transverse ligament integrity.

Differential
  • Jefferson fracture (assess transverse ligament integrity, which determines stability)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; management depends on transverse ligament integrity (often assessed by combined overhang or MRI).

3. Hangman's fracture (C2 traumatic spondylolisthesis)

Concerning

Bilateral fracture through the C2 pars interarticularis, classically from hyperextension (historically judicial hanging, now most often motor vehicle collisions).

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral fracture through the C2 pars interarticularis with variable anterolisthesis of C2 on C3.

Differential
  • Hangman's fracture (graded by degree of displacement/angulation, which guides management)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; management ranges from cervical collar to surgical fixation depending on displacement severity.

4. Cervical facet fracture/dislocation

Concerning

Fracture and/or dislocation of the cervical facet joints, often from flexion-distraction mechanisms, with significant spinal cord injury risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Malalignment of the facet joints with anterolisthesis of the vertebral body above, sometimes with a 'perched' or 'jumped/locked' facet configuration.

Differential
  • Unilateral facet dislocation (often with rotational component)
  • Bilateral facet dislocation (higher instability and cord injury risk)
Next Step

Emergent spine surgery consultation given high spinal cord injury risk; MRI to assess for disc herniation before any reduction maneuver.

5. Cervical burst fracture

Concerning

Comminuted vertebral body fracture from axial loading, with retropulsion of fragments into the spinal canal.

Imaging Characteristics

Comminuted vertebral body fracture with height loss and retropulsed fragments narrowing the spinal canal, often with associated posterior element involvement.

Differential
  • Cervical burst fracture (assess canal compromise and cord signal on MRI if available)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; often requires surgical stabilization given instability and canal compromise.

6. Thoracolumbar compression fracture

Variable

Anterior column height loss without significant posterior element or middle column involvement, generally a stable fracture pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior wedging/height loss of the vertebral body with an intact posterior vertebral body wall and posterior elements.

Differential
  • Traumatic compression fracture (stable pattern)
  • Osteoporotic compression fracture (atraumatic or low-energy mechanism)
  • Pathologic fracture (underlying lesion)
Next Step

Usually managed with bracing/pain control if stable; correlate with mechanism and bone density risk factors.

7. Thoracolumbar burst fracture

Concerning

Comminuted vertebral body fracture involving the middle column with canal retropulsion, a more unstable pattern than simple compression fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Comminuted vertebral body fracture with loss of the posterior vertebral body wall integrity and fragment retropulsion into the spinal canal.

Differential
  • Thoracolumbar burst fracture (higher instability than a simple compression fracture)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; assess neurologic status and canal compromise for surgical decision-making.

8. Chance fracture (flexion-distraction injury)

Concerning

Fracture extending horizontally through the vertebral body and posterior elements, classically from a lap-belt injury in a motor vehicle collision.

Imaging Characteristics

Horizontal fracture line extending through the spinous process, pedicles, and vertebral body, often with an associated intra-abdominal injury given the flexion-distraction mechanism.

Differential
  • Chance fracture (highly unstable, three-column injury)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; carefully evaluate for associated intra-abdominal injury (bowel/mesenteric injury) given the shared mechanism.

9. Transverse process fracture

Variable

Fracture of a vertebral transverse process, generally a stable injury but a marker of higher-energy trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical disruption of one or more transverse processes, often at the lumbar spine, from direct trauma or violent muscular contraction/avulsion.

Differential
  • Isolated transverse process fracture (generally stable)
Next Step

Pain control and mobilization; trauma survey for associated renal or other retroperitoneal injury given the proximity of lumbar transverse processes.

10. Sacral fracture

Concerning

Fracture of the sacrum, from high-energy trauma or, in osteoporotic bone, insufficiency fractures.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertical fracture line through the sacral ala (traumatic or insufficiency), sometimes with a horizontal component forming an 'H-shaped' pattern in bilateral insufficiency fractures.

Differential
  • Traumatic sacral fracture (often associated with pelvic ring injury)
  • Insufficiency fracture (osteoporosis, pelvic radiation)
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine consultation; assess for associated lumbosacral plexus injury and pelvic ring instability.

11. Clay shoveler's fracture

Benign

Avulsion fracture of a lower cervical or upper thoracic spinous process, classically from repetitive flexion strain or direct trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Oblique fracture through the base of a spinous process (most often C7 or T1), typically an isolated and stable injury.

Differential
  • Clay shoveler's fracture (stable avulsion injury)
Next Step

Symptomatic management (collar, analgesia); generally does not require surgical intervention.

12. SCIWORA (spinal cord injury without radiographic abnormality)

Concerning

Spinal cord injury occurring without a visible fracture or malalignment on CT, more common in children given greater spinal elasticity.

Imaging Characteristics

Normal or near-normal CT findings in a patient with clinical signs of spinal cord injury; MRI is required to demonstrate the cord signal abnormality and any associated ligamentous injury.

Differential
  • SCIWORA (classic in pediatric patients)
  • Transient spinal cord concussion
Next Step

MRI is essential given normal CT does not exclude significant cord injury; spine immobilization and neurosurgical/spine consultation.

Spine: Trauma - Ligamentous & Alignment

5 findings

1. Atlantoaxial subluxation/instability

Concerning

Abnormal alignment or increased mobility between C1 and C2, from transverse ligament injury or bony abnormality.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased atlantodental interval (>3 mm in adults, >5 mm in children) suggesting transverse ligament injury or bony abnormality (odontoid fracture, os odontoideum).

Differential
  • Traumatic transverse ligament rupture
  • Inflammatory (rheumatoid arthritis-related)
  • Down syndrome-associated ligamentous laxity
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; flexion-extension imaging (when safe) or MRI to assess ligamentous integrity.

2. Atlanto-occipital dissociation

Concerning

Severe, often fatal ligamentous injury between the skull base and C1, from high-energy hyperextension/distraction trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal widening of the distance between the occipital condyles and C1 lateral masses, or abnormal basion-dens interval, indicating craniocervical ligamentous disruption.

Differential
  • Atlanto-occipital dissociation (highly unstable, life-threatening injury)
Next Step

Immediate rigid immobilization and emergent spine surgery consultation; avoid traction, which can worsen this injury pattern.

3. Traumatic spondylolisthesis, non-C2

Concerning

Vertebral malalignment from traumatic ligamentous or bony injury at a level other than the classic C2 hangman's pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior or posterior displacement of one vertebral body relative to the adjacent level, assessed for degree of translation and associated fracture.

Differential
  • Traumatic facet injury/dislocation
  • Traumatic disc/ligamentous disruption
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; MRI to assess ligamentous and disc integrity.

4. Perched/jumped facet

Concerning

Spectrum of facet joint malalignment from flexion-distraction injury, ranging from subluxation (perched) to complete dislocation (jumped/locked).

Imaging Characteristics

Perched facet: inferior articular facet resting on the tip of the facet below, with abnormal alignment but partial contact. Jumped/locked facet: complete displacement with the inferior facet locked anterior to the facet below.

Differential
  • Unilateral or bilateral facet perching/jumping
Next Step

Emergent spine surgery consultation; MRI before any reduction attempt to assess for a herniated disc that could worsen cord compression during reduction.

5. Widened interspinous distance (ligamentous injury sign)

Concerning

Abnormal widening between adjacent spinous processes, suggesting posterior ligamentous complex disruption.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased distance between adjacent spinous processes compared to levels above and below, or a 'fanning' appearance, suggesting interspinous/supraspinous ligament injury.

Differential
  • Posterior ligamentous complex injury (important stability determinant)
Next Step

MRI to assess ligamentous integrity; spine surgery consultation as this finding affects stability classification and management.

Spine: Degenerative Disease

10 findings

1. Disc herniation, CT

Variable

Displacement of disc material beyond the normal disc space margins, with CT showing this less well than MRI but still able to suggest the finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or broad-based soft-tissue density extending beyond the vertebral body margins into the spinal canal or neural foramen, potentially effacing the thecal sac or compressing a nerve root.

Differential
  • Disc protrusion/extrusion
  • Epidural fat (can mimic on CT, better distinguished on MRI)
Next Step

MRI is the preferred modality for full characterization; correlate with clinical radiculopathy/myelopathy symptoms.

2. Spinal canal stenosis, central

Variable

Narrowing of the central spinal canal, from a combination of disc bulging, facet hypertrophy, and ligamentum flavum thickening.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced central canal diameter/cross-sectional area from combined disc, facet, and ligamentous encroachment, most commonly at the lumbar spine.

Differential
  • Degenerative spinal stenosis (most common)
  • Congenital short pedicle stenosis (predisposes to earlier symptomatic degenerative stenosis)
Next Step

Correlate with neurogenic claudication symptoms; spine surgery/pain management referral for symptomatic cases.

3. Neural foraminal stenosis

Variable

Narrowing of the neural foramen, from disc height loss, facet hypertrophy, and uncovertebral joint hypertrophy (cervical spine).

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced foraminal dimensions from combined disc bulging/herniation, facet joint hypertrophy, and (in the cervical spine) uncovertebral joint osteophyte formation.

Differential
  • Degenerative foraminal stenosis
Next Step

Correlate with radicular symptoms in the corresponding dermatomal distribution; spine surgery/pain management referral if symptomatic.

4. Facet arthropathy

Benign

Degenerative changes of the facet (zygapophyseal) joints, extremely common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Facet joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, and osteophyte formation, which can contribute to foraminal and central canal narrowing.

Differential
  • Age-related degenerative facet arthropathy
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; conservative management (physical therapy, facet injections) for symptomatic cases.

5. Degenerative spondylolisthesis

Variable

Anterior slippage of one vertebral body relative to the one below, from degenerative facet joint incompetence rather than a pars defect.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior displacement of a vertebral body (most often L4 on L5) relative to the level below, with intact posterior elements/pars (distinguishing it from isthmic spondylolisthesis).

Differential
  • Degenerative spondylolisthesis (intact pars, facet-mediated)
  • Isthmic spondylolisthesis (pars defect present, key differential)
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; spine surgery referral if associated with significant stenosis or instability symptoms.

6. Spondylolysis (pars interarticularis defect)

Variable

Stress fracture or defect of the pars interarticularis, most common at L5, a common cause of low back pain in young athletes.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent defect through the pars interarticularis, best seen on sagittal and 3D reconstructions ('Scotty dog with a collar' appearance on oblique views historically described on plain film).

Differential
  • Unilateral or bilateral pars defect
  • Can progress to isthmic spondylolisthesis if bilateral
Next Step

Correlate with activity-related back pain in young athletes; conservative management (activity modification, bracing) is often first-line.

7. Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH)

Variable

Flowing calcification/ossification along the anterolateral aspect of the spine, a noninflammatory condition distinct from ankylosing spondylitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Flowing, 'candle-wax' ossification along the anterolateral vertebral bodies spanning at least four contiguous levels, with preserved disc height and sacroiliac joints (distinguishing it from ankylosing spondylitis).

Differential
  • DISH (preserved disc height and SI joints distinguish from ankylosing spondylitis)
  • Ankylosing spondylitis (disc space and SI joint involvement, key differential)
Next Step

Usually an incidental finding; correlate with dysphagia symptoms if cervical DISH is extensive enough to compress the esophagus.

8. Vacuum disc phenomenon

Benign

Gas (typically nitrogen) within a degenerated intervertebral disc, a reliable sign of disc degeneration.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or cleft-like gas lucency within the disc space, often more apparent on extension views/positioning.

Differential
  • Degenerative disc disease (vacuum phenomenon is a specific sign of degeneration, essentially excludes infection at that level given gas does not typically form in infected discs)
Next Step

No specific action needed; helpful in excluding discitis at a level with vacuum phenomenon.

9. Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament (OPLL)

Concerning

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, most common in the cervical spine, causing spinal canal narrowing.

Imaging Characteristics

Ossified, hyperdense band along the posterior vertebral body margins within the spinal canal, most often cervical, causing variable canal compromise.

Differential
  • OPLL (more common in East Asian populations)
  • Severe degenerative osteophyte formation (differentiate by the continuous ligamentous ossification pattern)
Next Step

Spine surgery referral if causing significant canal stenosis or myelopathic symptoms.

10. Baastrup disease (kissing spines)

Benign

Degenerative approximation and contact of adjacent spinous processes, causing localized back pain.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, closely apposed adjacent spinous processes with sclerosis and sometimes a fluid-filled bursa-like collection between them, most common at the lower lumbar spine.

Differential
  • Baastrup disease (common with lumbar hyperlordosis/degenerative disc height loss)
Next Step

Conservative management (physical therapy, injections); usually not requiring surgical intervention.

Spine: Infection

5 findings

1. Discitis-osteomyelitis

Concerning

Infection of the intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral endplates, from hematogenous spread or direct inoculation.

Imaging Characteristics

Endplate destruction/erosion with disc space narrowing and adjacent vertebral body lucency; CT is less sensitive than MRI for early disease but can show endplate destruction well in more established infection.

Differential
  • Pyogenic discitis-osteomyelitis (most common bacterial cause: Staph aureus)
  • Tuberculous spondylitis (more indolent, larger paraspinal abscess, relative disc preservation early)
Next Step

MRI for definitive characterization and epidural extension assessment; blood cultures and possible image-guided biopsy for organism identification.

2. Spinal epidural abscess

Concerning

Purulent collection in the epidural space, a neurosurgical emergency given spinal cord compression risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Epidural collection, often with rim enhancement, causing variable thecal sac/cord compression; frequently associated with adjacent discitis-osteomyelitis.

Differential
  • Spinal epidural abscess (surgical emergency if neurologic compromise)
  • Epidural hematoma (differentiate by clinical context and imaging characteristics)
  • Epidural malignancy
Next Step

Emergent spine surgery/neurosurgery consultation, particularly with any neurologic deficit; MRI for full extent of cord compression.

3. Paraspinal abscess

Concerning

Infection extending into the paraspinal soft tissues, often from contiguous discitis-osteomyelitis.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing fluid collection within the paraspinal musculature, often contiguous with adjacent vertebral/disc infection.

Differential
  • Paraspinal abscess from contiguous discitis-osteomyelitis
  • Tuberculous (Pott) abscess (classically tracks along the psoas)
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage plus antibiotics; MRI for full extent and epidural involvement assessment.

4. Tuberculous spondylitis (Pott disease)

Concerning

Spinal tuberculosis, classically causing more indolent vertebral destruction with large paraspinal abscesses and relative disc preservation compared to pyogenic infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertebral body destruction with anterior wedging (gibbus deformity in advanced disease), relatively preserved disc space early in the disease course, and often large, sometimes calcified paraspinal/psoas abscesses.

Differential
  • Tuberculous spondylitis (endemic area exposure history important)
  • Pyogenic discitis-osteomyelitis (earlier disc involvement, smaller abscesses)
  • Metastatic disease (can mimic vertebral destruction pattern)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation and multidrug antituberculous therapy.

5. Facet joint septic arthritis

Concerning

Infection of a facet (zygapophyseal) joint, an uncommon but important cause of localized spine infection.

Imaging Characteristics

Facet joint effusion with erosive changes and surrounding soft-tissue/paraspinal inflammatory change, sometimes with an associated epidural abscess.

Differential
  • Facet joint septic arthritis
  • Degenerative facet joint effusion (non-infected, no destructive changes)
Next Step

Antibiotics and possible drainage; correlate with fever and inflammatory markers.

Spine: Tumor & Neoplasm

8 findings

1. Vertebral metastasis

Concerning

Secondary malignant vertebral lesion, the most common spinal tumor overall, most often from breast, lung, prostate, and renal primaries.

Imaging Characteristics

Lytic, sclerotic, or mixed lesion within the vertebral body and/or posterior elements, which can cause pathologic fracture and canal compromise; pedicle involvement is a classically described feature.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common)
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Primary vertebral tumor (less common)
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging; MRI to assess canal/cord compromise, particularly with any neurologic symptoms.

2. Multiple myeloma / plasmacytoma

Concerning

Plasma cell malignancy causing lytic vertebral lesions, either solitary (plasmacytoma) or multifocal (myeloma).

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined lytic lesions ('punched-out' appearance) within the vertebral body, sometimes with an associated soft-tissue mass and pathologic compression fracture.

Differential
  • Multiple myeloma (typically multifocal)
  • Solitary plasmacytoma
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral; skeletal survey/whole-body imaging and laboratory workup (SPEP, free light chains) for staging.

3. Vertebral hemangioma

Benign

Common benign vascular vertebral lesion, often incidental.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertebral body lesion with a characteristic 'corduroy' or 'polka-dot' pattern of coarsened, vertically oriented trabeculae on axial images.

Differential
  • Typical vertebral hemangioma (benign, common incidental finding)
  • Aggressive hemangioma (larger, with soft-tissue extension - less common)
Next Step

No action needed for a typical, asymptomatic vertebral hemangioma.

4. Intradural spinal cord tumor

Concerning

Tumor arising within the dura, either intramedullary (within the cord, e.g., astrocytoma, ependymoma) or extramedullary (e.g., schwannoma, meningioma).

Imaging Characteristics

Best characterized by MRI; CT may show canal widening or bony remodeling from a slow-growing lesion, with the mass itself often subtle on CT alone.

Differential
  • Intramedullary tumor (astrocytoma, ependymoma)
  • Extramedullary intradural tumor (schwannoma, meningioma)
Next Step

MRI for definitive characterization; neurosurgical/spine referral.

5. Chordoma

Concerning

Rare, locally aggressive malignant tumor arising from notochordal remnants, most often at the sacrococcygeal region or clivus.

Imaging Characteristics

Destructive, midline lytic lesion with an associated soft-tissue mass, most often at the sacrum, with characteristic slow but locally aggressive growth.

Differential
  • Chordoma (classic sacrococcygeal or clival location)
  • Chondrosarcoma
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Spine oncology/surgical referral for biopsy and resection planning; notoriously difficult to achieve complete resection given the location and local aggressiveness.

6. Osteoid osteoma (spine)

Benign

Benign bone-forming tumor, classically causing pain worse at night and relieved by NSAIDs, with a predilection for the posterior elements when in the spine.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round lucent nidus (typically <1.5 cm) with surrounding dense reactive sclerosis, most often in a pedicle or lamina; can be associated with painful scoliosis.

Differential
  • Osteoid osteoma (classic NSAID-responsive night pain)
  • Osteoblastoma (larger version of the same tumor spectrum, >1.5-2 cm)
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine referral; radiofrequency ablation or surgical resection for persistent symptoms.

7. Osteoblastoma (spine)

Variable

Benign but locally aggressive bone-forming tumor, larger than osteoid osteoma, with a predilection for the posterior spinal elements.

Imaging Characteristics

Expansile lytic lesion, typically >1.5-2 cm, in the posterior elements, with variable matrix mineralization and surrounding reactive changes.

Differential
  • Osteoblastoma
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst (can coexist)
  • Osteoid osteoma (smaller size distinguishes)
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine oncology referral for resection given local aggressiveness.

8. Aneurysmal bone cyst (spine)

Variable

Benign but locally aggressive, expansile, blood-filled cystic bone lesion, with a predilection for the posterior spinal elements in younger patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Expansile, multiloculated lytic lesion with a thin cortical rim ('blow-out' appearance) and fluid-fluid levels reflecting layered blood products.

Differential
  • Aneurysmal bone cyst (primary or secondary to an underlying lesion such as osteoblastoma)
  • Telangiectatic osteosarcoma (important malignant mimic to exclude)
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine oncology referral; biopsy to exclude an underlying or malignant process before definitive treatment.

Spine: Congenital & Miscellaneous

6 findings

1. Spina bifida occulta

Benign

Incomplete fusion of the posterior vertebral elements without herniation of neural tissue, extremely common and usually asymptomatic.

Imaging Characteristics

Midline bony defect of the posterior elements (typically at S1), without an associated soft-tissue mass or neural tissue herniation.

Differential
  • Isolated spina bifida occulta (common incidental finding, present in a substantial minority of the population)
Next Step

No action needed for an isolated, asymptomatic finding.

2. Tethered spinal cord

Concerning

Abnormally low position of the conus medullaris with a thickened or fatty filum terminale, restricting normal cord movement.

Imaging Characteristics

Conus medullaris positioned below the normal L1-L2 level (age-dependent in children), sometimes with an associated thickened or fatty filum terminale; MRI is the preferred modality for full characterization.

Differential
  • Tethered cord syndrome
  • Associated with spinal dysraphism (lipomyelomeningocele, and others)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral, particularly in the setting of progressive neurologic symptoms, bowel/bladder dysfunction, or orthopedic foot deformity.

3. Transitional vertebra

Benign

Congenital variant where a vertebra at the lumbosacral (or cervicothoracic) junction has features intermediate between the two adjacent regions.

Imaging Characteristics

Lumbarization of S1 or sacralization of L5, often with an enlarged, sometimes pseudo-articulating transverse process; important to correctly number vertebral levels for any planned intervention.

Differential
  • Transitional vertebra (normal variant, common incidental finding)
Next Step

No action needed for the variant itself; ensure correct level identification/counting before any planned spinal procedure or surgery, given documented wrong-level surgery risk with unrecognized transitional anatomy.

4. Scoliosis

Variable

Lateral curvature of the spine, idiopathic or secondary to an underlying structural, neuromuscular, or congenital cause.

Imaging Characteristics

Lateral spinal curvature measured by the Cobb angle, with assessment for an underlying structural cause (hemivertebra, congenital bar) if the pattern is atypical for idiopathic disease.

Differential
  • Idiopathic scoliosis (most common, typically adolescent-onset)
  • Congenital scoliosis (structural vertebral anomaly)
  • Neuromuscular scoliosis
Next Step

Orthopedic/spine referral for curve monitoring and bracing/surgical management based on the Cobb angle and skeletal maturity.

5. Klippel-Feil syndrome

Variable

Congenital fusion of two or more cervical vertebrae, classically associated with a triad of short neck, low posterior hairline, and limited neck motion (though not all features are always present).

Imaging Characteristics

Congenital fusion of cervical vertebral bodies and/or posterior elements, often with an associated 'wasp-waist' disc space appearance at the fused level(s).

Differential
  • Klippel-Feil syndrome (assess for associated anomalies: renal, cardiac, Sprengel deformity)
Next Step

Screen for associated anomalies (renal ultrasound, cardiac evaluation); spine referral given increased risk of adjacent segment degeneration and instability given the fused levels.

6. Hemivertebra

Variable

Congenital malformation with a wedge-shaped, partially formed vertebral body, a common cause of congenital scoliosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped vertebral body with only one (or a partial) ossification center, causing asymmetric growth and progressive congenital scoliosis.

Differential
  • Fully segmented hemivertebra (higher scoliosis progression risk)
  • Semi-segmented or non-segmented hemivertebra (lower progression risk)
Next Step

Pediatric orthopedic/spine referral for curve monitoring given progression risk during growth.

Skull (Plain Film)

12 findings

1. Linear skull fracture, plain film

Variable

Simple fracture line through the calvarium, though CT is now the standard for head trauma evaluation.

Imaging Characteristics

Sharp, lucent line without sclerotic margins, distinguishing it from a normal suture (which has characteristically interdigitated, corticated margins).

Differential
  • Linear skull fracture
  • Normal suture
  • Vascular groove (branching, gradually tapering)
Next Step

CT for evaluation of underlying intracranial injury, which plain film cannot assess.

2. Depressed skull fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture with inward displacement of a bone fragment.

Imaging Characteristics

Increased bone density where a fragment overlaps adjacent bone (tangential view helps show the depth of depression).

Differential
  • Depressed skull fracture
Next Step

CT for full characterization and assessment of underlying brain injury; neurosurgical consultation.

3. Basilar skull fracture, indirect plain film signs

Concerning

Skull base fracture, poorly seen directly on plain film but suggested by secondary signs.

Imaging Characteristics

Opacification or air-fluid level in the sphenoid sinus or mastoid air cells in the setting of trauma.

Differential
  • Basilar skull fracture (indirect sign)
Next Step

CT for direct fracture visualization and assessment of associated complications.

4. Pineal gland shift

Concerning

Displacement of a calcified pineal gland from midline, suggesting a mass lesion or significant asymmetric process.

Imaging Characteristics

Calcified pineal gland (visible in a subset of adults) displaced from the expected midline position.

Differential
  • Mass lesion with midline shift
  • Asymmetric hemispheric volume loss
Next Step

CT or MRI for direct visualization of the underlying cause.

5. Sella turcica changes, plain film

Variable

Abnormal size or contour of the sella turcica, suggesting a pituitary or parasellar process.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged, ballooned sella (pituitary macroadenoma), or erosion/double floor (mass effect); plain film is largely superseded by MRI for this evaluation.

Differential
  • Pituitary macroadenoma
  • Chronically elevated ICP (can cause sellar changes)
  • Empty sella
Next Step

MRI for definitive pituitary/sellar characterization.

6. Lytic skull lesion

Concerning

Focal area of decreased bone density within the calvarium.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined or ill-defined lucent lesion within the skull, with margin characteristics helping suggest benign versus aggressive etiology.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Epidermoid (benign, well-corticated)
  • Langerhans cell histiocytosis (pediatric)
Next Step

CT/MRI for further characterization; correlate with malignancy history.

7. Sclerotic skull lesion

Variable

Focal area of increased bone density within the calvarium.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined area of increased density, often incidental.

Differential
  • Bone island (benign)
  • Osteoblastic metastasis (prostate, breast)
  • Meningioma with hyperostosis
  • Paget disease
Next Step

CT/MRI for further characterization if new or growing; correlate with malignancy history.

8. Sutural diastasis

Concerning

Abnormal widening of a cranial suture, suggesting elevated intracranial pressure (in children) or trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Widened lucency along the course of a cranial suture beyond expected age-related norms.

Differential
  • Elevated intracranial pressure (pediatric)
  • Traumatic sutural diastasis
Next Step

CT for further evaluation of the underlying cause, particularly in a child with suspected elevated ICP.

9. Wormian bones

Benign

Extra, irregular ossicles within cranial sutures, a normal variant that can also be associated with certain syndromes when numerous.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small, irregular bone islands within the sutures, most often the lambdoid suture.

Differential
  • Normal variant (isolated, few in number)
  • Associated with osteogenesis imperfecta, cleidocranial dysplasia, or other syndromes if numerous
Next Step

Usually incidental; correlate with syndromic features if numerous.

10. Hyperostosis frontalis interna

Benign

Benign thickening of the inner table of the frontal bone, common in older women.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric, nodular thickening of the inner table of the frontal bone, typically sparing the midline.

Differential
  • Hyperostosis frontalis interna (benign, common incidental finding)
Next Step

No action needed.

11. Skull foreign body

Variable

Radiopaque foreign material within or adjacent to the skull.

Imaging Characteristics

Discrete radiopaque object overlying or embedded within the calvarium.

Differential
  • Retained foreign body from penetrating trauma
  • External object (jewelry, hair clips - positioning artifact)
Next Step

CT for depth/intracranial involvement assessment if a penetrating injury is suspected.

12. Copper-beaten skull (digital markings)

Concerning

Prominent convolutional (gyral) markings on the inner table of the skull, a sign of chronically elevated intracranial pressure in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse, scalloped thinning of the inner table producing a 'beaten copper' or 'beaten metal' appearance.

Differential
  • Chronically elevated intracranial pressure (craniosynostosis, chronic hydrocephalus)
  • Normal finding in some children under age 10 (can be a normal variant at younger ages)
Next Step

Correlate with age and clinical signs of elevated ICP; CT/MRI for further evaluation if pathologic.

Cervical Spine - Trauma

10 findings

1. Odontoid fracture, plain film

Concerning

Fracture of the C2 odontoid process, best assessed on the open-mouth odontoid view, though CT is now standard for trauma evaluation.

Imaging Characteristics

Lucent fracture line through the base or tip of the odontoid on the open-mouth view; often subtle or missed on plain film.

Differential
  • Odontoid fracture (types I-III)
  • Os odontoideum (chronic, smooth corticated margins)
  • Mach effect (overlap artifact, a common pitfall on this view)
Next Step

CT for definitive characterization given the limited sensitivity of plain film; do not clear the cervical spine based on a negative plain film alone in a high-risk patient.

2. Jefferson fracture, open-mouth view

Concerning

C1 burst fracture, suggested on the open-mouth odontoid view by lateral mass displacement.

Imaging Characteristics

Bilateral lateral overhang of the C1 lateral masses relative to the C2 body on the open-mouth view.

Differential
  • Jefferson fracture (assess combined lateral overhang for transverse ligament integrity)
Next Step

CT for definitive fracture characterization; spine surgery consultation.

3. Hangman's fracture, plain film

Concerning

C2 pars interarticularis fracture, suggested on the lateral view by C2-C3 malalignment.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterolisthesis of C2 on C3 with a fracture line through the C2 pars interarticularis, best seen on the lateral view.

Differential
  • Hangman's fracture (traumatic C2 spondylolisthesis)
Next Step

CT for full characterization; spine surgery consultation.

4. Cervical facet dislocation, plain film

Concerning

Malalignment of the cervical facet joints, suggested on the lateral view by vertebral body malalignment.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterolisthesis of one vertebral body on the one below, sometimes with a 'bow-tie' sign of an overlapping, rotated facet on the lateral view (unilateral dislocation).

Differential
  • Unilateral facet dislocation
  • Bilateral facet dislocation (typically greater degree of translation)
Next Step

CT and likely MRI for full characterization; emergent spine surgery consultation.

5. Cervical compression fracture, plain film

Variable

Anterior height loss of a cervical vertebral body.

Imaging Characteristics

Wedge-shaped anterior height loss of a vertebral body on the lateral view.

Differential
  • Traumatic compression fracture
  • Osteoporotic compression fracture
Next Step

CT for further characterization and to exclude a burst component with canal compromise.

6. Clay shoveler's fracture, plain film

Benign

Avulsion fracture of a lower cervical/upper thoracic spinous process.

Imaging Characteristics

Oblique fracture through the base of a spinous process (classically C7), best seen on the lateral view; the fragment often appears to be doubled ('double spinous process sign') due to slight caudal displacement.

Differential
  • Clay shoveler's fracture (stable avulsion injury)
Next Step

Symptomatic management; generally a stable injury not requiring surgery.

7. Prevertebral soft tissue swelling

Concerning

Widened prevertebral soft tissue shadow on the lateral cervical spine view, a sensitive but nonspecific sign of underlying injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Prevertebral soft tissue thickness exceeding expected norms (roughly >7 mm at C2, >22 mm at C6 in adults, with some variation by reference and patient positioning).

Differential
  • Underlying fracture/ligamentous injury with associated hematoma
  • Retropharyngeal abscess/infection (non-traumatic context)
  • Technical/positioning artifact (common cause of false widening)
Next Step

CT for further evaluation of an underlying fracture or infectious process given this nonspecific but sensitive finding.

8. Widened atlantodental interval, plain film

Concerning

Increased distance between the anterior arch of C1 and the odontoid process, suggesting transverse ligament injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Atlantodental interval >3 mm in adults (>5 mm in children) on the lateral view.

Differential
  • Transverse ligament injury/instability

  • Down syndrome-associated laxity

  • Isolated spinous process avulsion (stable)

Brain: Ischemic Stroke

9 findings

1. Acute infarct, DWI restriction

Concerning

Acute ischemic stroke, most sensitively detected by diffusion-weighted imaging within minutes of onset.

Imaging Characteristics

Restricted diffusion (bright on DWI, dark on ADC map) in a vascular territory distribution, detectable within minutes to hours of ischemic onset, well before conventional T2/FLAIR changes appear.

Differential
  • Acute ischemic infarct
  • Seizure-related transient restricted diffusion (less territory-conforming)
  • Abscess (also restricts, but with a ring-enhancing capsule)
Next Step

Time-critical stroke protocol; determine thrombolysis/thrombectomy eligibility based on time window and clinical severity.

2. Hyperacute vs. established infarct, ADC correlation

Concerning

Distinguishing very early from more established infarction using the apparent diffusion coefficient map.

Imaging Characteristics

ADC values are most severely reduced in the first few days, gradually pseudo-normalizing around 7-10 days and becoming elevated (T2 shine-through predominant) in the chronic phase.

Differential
  • Hyperacute/acute infarct (markedly reduced ADC)
  • Subacute infarct (pseudo-normalizing ADC, a recognized pitfall)
  • Chronic infarct (elevated ADC)
Next Step

Correlate DWI/ADC findings with clinical timeline to accurately stage infarct age.

3. Subacute infarct, pseudo-normalization pitfall

Variable

ADC pseudo-normalization occurring roughly 7-10 days after infarct onset, a recognized diagnostic pitfall.

Imaging Characteristics

DWI may remain bright (T2 shine-through) while the ADC value normalizes or even increases, potentially causing an infarct to be misread as acute or missed entirely if only DWI is reviewed.

Differential
  • Subacute infarct (pseudo-normalization phase)
Next Step

Always correlate DWI with the ADC map, not DWI alone, when staging infarct age.

4. Chronic infarct, MRI

Benign

Remote infarct with established encephalomalacia and gliosis.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-hypointense, T2/FLAIR-hyperintense area of encephalomalacia with volume loss, conforming to a vascular territory, without diffusion restriction.

Differential
  • Remote infarct
Next Step

No acute action; correlate with clinical history.

5. Lacunar infarct, MRI

Variable

Small, deep infarct from penetrating arteriole occlusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Small (<15 mm), T2/FLAIR-hyperintense focus in the deep gray matter or internal capsule/pons, with restricted diffusion if acute.

Differential
  • Acute lacunar infarct
  • Chronic lacunar infarct/prior small vessel ischemic change
  • Dilated perivascular (Virchow-Robin) space (follows CSF signal on all sequences, key differential)
Next Step

Stroke workup per standard protocol; secondary prevention with vascular risk factor control.

6. Watershed (border zone) infarct, MRI

Variable

Infarct at vascular border zones from hypoperfusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or wedge-shaped restricted diffusion along the border zone between two vascular territories, often bilateral if from a global hypoperfusion event.

Differential
  • Hypoperfusion/hypotensive event
  • Severe carotid stenosis/occlusion with impaired collateral flow
Next Step

Identify and correct the underlying hemodynamic cause; evaluate for severe carotid disease.

7. Venous infarct, MRI

Concerning

Infarct from venous outflow obstruction, often with a non-arterial distribution and hemorrhagic component.

Imaging Characteristics

Restricted diffusion and often hemorrhage in a distribution not conforming to an arterial territory, with an associated dural sinus/cortical vein thrombosis identified on MR venography.

Differential
  • Venous infarct secondary to sinus/cortical vein thrombosis
Next Step

MR venography to confirm venous thrombosis; anticoagulation remains indicated despite hemorrhagic component.

8. Posterior circulation/brainstem infarct

Concerning

Ischemic stroke involving the vertebrobasilar territory, which can present with subtle or nonspecific symptoms and is more easily missed on CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Restricted diffusion within the brainstem, cerebellum, thalamus, or occipital lobes; MRI is substantially more sensitive than CT for this territory given less bony artifact.

Differential
  • Posterior circulation ischemic stroke
Next Step

Time-critical stroke protocol; maintain high suspicion given the often subtle/nonspecific presentation (dizziness, ataxia, diplopia).

9. Infarct with hemorrhagic transformation, MRI

Concerning

Hemorrhage within a region of established infarction, best characterized with susceptibility-sensitive sequences.

Imaging Characteristics

Susceptibility signal loss (blooming) within a region of established DWI/T2 abnormality, ranging from petechial to frank hematoma.

Differential
  • Hemorrhagic transformation of ischemic stroke
Next Step

Neurocritical care consultation; may affect antithrombotic therapy decisions.

Brain: Hemorrhage & Trauma

10 findings

1. Hyperacute hemorrhage, MRI signal

Concerning

Blood in the first few hours after hemorrhage, with characteristic evolving MRI signal.

Imaging Characteristics

Hyperacute blood (oxyhemoglobin) is often isointense to mildly hypointense on T1 and hyperintense on T2, with surrounding susceptibility blooming on gradient-echo/SWI sequences.

Differential
  • Hyperacute intracranial hemorrhage
Next Step

Correlate MRI blood product staging with clinical timeline; neurosurgical consultation as appropriate for the underlying hemorrhage type.

2. Acute hemorrhage, MRI signal (deoxyhemoglobin)

Concerning

Blood in the first few days after hemorrhage.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-isointense to hypointense, T2-hypointense signal from deoxyhemoglobin, with pronounced susceptibility blooming.

Differential
  • Acute intracranial hemorrhage (1-3 days)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical timeline; neurosurgical consultation as appropriate.

3. Subacute hemorrhage, MRI signal (methemoglobin)

Concerning

Blood in the first weeks after hemorrhage, transitioning from intracellular to extracellular methemoglobin.

Imaging Characteristics

Early subacute: T1-hyperintense, T2-hypointense (intracellular methemoglobin). Late subacute: T1- and T2-hyperintense (extracellular methemoglobin).

Differential
  • Subacute intracranial hemorrhage (days to weeks)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical timeline.

4. Chronic hemorrhage, MRI signal (hemosiderin)

Variable

Remote hemorrhage with a hemosiderin-lined cavity, persisting indefinitely.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hypointense rim (hemosiderin) surrounding a central area that may be T1/T2 variable depending on the cavity's residual contents, with prominent, persistent susceptibility blooming.

Differential
  • Remote/chronic hemorrhage
Next Step

Correlate with clinical history; no acute action needed for the chronic blood products themselves.

5. Cerebral microhemorrhages

Variable

Tiny foci of chronic hemorrhage, best detected on susceptibility-weighted or gradient-echo sequences, with a distribution pattern that helps suggest etiology.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round foci of susceptibility blooming (typically <10 mm), with distribution informing etiology: lobar/peripheral favors amyloid angiopathy, while deep gray matter/brainstem favors hypertensive small vessel disease.

Differential
  • Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (lobar predominant)
  • Hypertensive microangiopathy (deep/infratentorial predominant)
  • Diffuse axonal injury (if in a post-traumatic context)
  • Cavernous malformations (if larger and more discrete)
Next Step

Correlate distribution pattern with clinical context; relevant to anticoagulation risk-benefit decisions.

6. Cerebral amyloid angiopathy, MRI pattern

Concerning

Vasculopathy from amyloid deposition in small cortical/leptomeningeal vessels, a major cause of lobar hemorrhage in older adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple lobar microhemorrhages and/or lobar macrohemorrhages, with cortical superficial siderosis a supportive additional finding, in a normotensive older patient.

Differential
  • Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (Boston criteria correlate clinical and imaging features)
  • Hypertensive microangiopathy (deep distribution, key differential)
Next Step

Neurology referral; informs future anticoagulation/antiplatelet risk-benefit discussions given elevated hemorrhage risk.

7. Diffuse axonal injury, MRI (SWI)

Concerning

Widespread axonal shearing injury, far more sensitively detected on MRI (particularly susceptibility-weighted imaging) than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple small foci of susceptibility blooming (hemorrhagic DAI) or T2/FLAIR hyperintensity (non-hemorrhagic DAI) at the gray-white junction, corpus callosum, and dorsolateral brainstem.

Differential
  • Diffuse axonal injury (classic locations)
Next Step

MRI is indicated when clinical severity exceeds CT findings; neurocritical care management and prognostic counseling.

8. Cerebral contusion, MRI

Concerning

Traumatic brain bruising, well characterized by MRI with sensitivity for both the hemorrhagic and edematous components.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous T2/FLAIR hyperintensity with foci of susceptibility blooming at the site of impact, most often inferior frontal/temporal.

Differential
  • Cerebral contusion (coup or contrecoup)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/trauma consultation; monitor for progression.

9. Subdural hematoma, MRI staging

Variable

Subdural collection characterized and aged using MRI blood product signal evolution.

Imaging Characteristics

Signal characteristics follow the same hyperacute-to-chronic blood product evolution as parenchymal hemorrhage, allowing more precise age estimation than CT density alone, particularly useful in suspected non-accidental trauma evaluation.

Differential
  • Acute, subacute, or chronic subdural hematoma (staged by signal characteristics)
  • Mixed-age collections (raises concern in the appropriate context, such as suspected abuse)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; MRI blood staging can be valuable in forensic/non-accidental trauma evaluation given its ability to suggest multiple injury timepoints.

10. Traumatic brainstem injury

Concerning

Direct traumatic injury to the brainstem, a high-morbidity finding often associated with severe mechanism.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and/or susceptibility blooming within the brainstem, often at the dorsolateral midbrain/pons in the context of diffuse axonal injury.

Differential
  • Primary traumatic brainstem injury
  • Secondary brainstem injury from herniation (Duret hemorrhage)
Next Step

Neurocritical care management; carries significant prognostic implications given brainstem involvement.

Brain: Primary Tumors

12 findings

1. Glioblastoma, MRI

Concerning

Most common and most aggressive primary malignant brain tumor in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Heterogeneous, irregular ring-enhancing mass with central necrosis, extensive surrounding T2/FLAIR hyperintense edema, restricted diffusion within cellular tumor components, and often crossing the corpus callosum.

Differential
  • Glioblastoma
  • Metastasis (usually multiple)
  • Abscess (thinner, smoother enhancing rim with central restricted diffusion, key differentiator)
  • Demyelinating pseudotumor (can mimic in atypical cases)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral; MR spectroscopy and perfusion can aid characterization prior to biopsy.

2. Low-grade glioma/astrocytoma, MRI

Concerning

Diffusely infiltrating glioma of lower grade than glioblastoma.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, T2/FLAIR-hyperintense, non-enhancing or minimally enhancing mass without significant surrounding edema or restricted diffusion.

Differential
  • Low-grade (diffuse) astrocytoma
  • Focal cortical dysplasia (epilepsy context)
  • Chronic infarct/gliosis
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral; molecular characterization (IDH mutation status) increasingly guides classification and management.

3. Oligodendroglioma

Concerning

Glioma subtype with characteristic calcification, often presenting with seizures, associated with IDH mutation and 1p/19q co-deletion.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortically-based, often calcified mass with heterogeneous T2 signal, typically less enhancing than higher-grade astrocytomas, most often frontal lobe.

Differential
  • Oligodendroglioma (calcification favors this)
  • Low-grade astrocytoma
  • Ganglioglioma (epilepsy context)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neuro-oncology referral; molecular testing (1p/19q co-deletion) guides classification.

4. Pilocytic astrocytoma

Variable

Benign (WHO grade I), often cystic tumor, most common in children, classically in the cerebellum.

Imaging Characteristics

Cystic mass with an enhancing mural nodule, most often cerebellar in children; typically well-circumscribed with a favorable prognosis after resection.

Differential
  • Pilocytic astrocytoma (classic pediatric cerebellar tumor)
  • Hemangioblastoma (similar appearance, consider in adults or with VHL association)
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgery referral; generally excellent prognosis with complete resection.

5. Meningioma, MRI

Variable

Most common benign extra-axial brain tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, extra-axial, dural-based mass, isointense to gray matter on T1/T2, with homogeneous avid enhancement and a characteristic 'dural tail' sign.

Differential
  • Meningioma (dural tail is a helpful but not fully specific sign)
  • Dural metastasis
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for symptomatic/growing lesions; asymptomatic small meningiomas often observed.

6. Vestibular schwannoma, MRI

Variable

Benign tumor of the vestibulocochlear nerve, classically in the cerebellopontine angle/internal auditory canal.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, avidly enhancing mass centered on the internal auditory canal, often with an 'ice cream cone' appearance (intracanalicular plus cerebellopontine angle components).

Differential
  • Vestibular schwannoma (most common CPA mass)
  • Meningioma (broader dural base, less canal-centered)
  • Facial nerve schwannoma (less common, different nerve of origin)
Next Step

ENT/neurosurgical referral; surveillance MRI or treatment (surgery, radiosurgery) based on size and symptoms.

7. Craniopharyngioma, MRI

Variable

Suprasellar tumor from Rathke pouch remnants, bimodal age distribution.

Imaging Characteristics

Suprasellar mass with mixed cystic and solid components; the cystic component often shows high T1 signal from cholesterol/protein content ('machine oil' appearance).

Differential
  • Craniopharyngioma
  • Pituitary macroadenoma
  • Rathke cleft cyst (typically simpler, non-enhancing cyst)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/endocrine referral.

8. Medulloblastoma, MRI

Concerning

Most common malignant pediatric brain tumor, arising in the posterior fossa.

Imaging Characteristics

Restricted diffusion (reflecting high cellularity) within a midline cerebellar mass, often with heterogeneous enhancement and associated hydrocephalus.

Differential
  • Medulloblastoma (restricted diffusion helps distinguish from pilocytic astrocytoma)
  • Ependymoma
  • Atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor (younger children)
Next Step

Emergent pediatric neurosurgical/oncology referral; full neuraxis MRI staging given leptomeningeal spread risk.

9. Ependymoma, MRI

Concerning

Tumor arising from ependymal cells, most common in the fourth ventricle in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Mass centered within or extending from the fourth ventricle, often extruding through the foramina of Luschka/Magendie, with heterogeneous enhancement and sometimes calcification.

Differential
  • Ependymoma ('plastic' extrusion through the fourth ventricle outlets is a classic feature)
  • Medulloblastoma
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgical/oncology referral; spine MRI for staging given CSF dissemination risk.

10. CNS lymphoma, MRI

Concerning

Primary or secondary CNS lymphoma, with a predilection for periventricular white matter.

Imaging Characteristics

Homogeneously enhancing, T2-hypointense to isointense (reflecting high cellularity) periventricular mass with marked restricted diffusion; can dramatically regress with steroids before biopsy ('ghost tumor' phenomenon).

Differential
  • CNS lymphoma (particularly in immunocompromised patients)
  • Toxoplasmosis (HIV/AIDS, key differential, often multiple with peripheral/target enhancement)
  • Glioblastoma
Next Step

Avoid corticosteroids before biopsy if possible; neuro-oncology referral.

11. Dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (DNET)

Benign

Benign, epilepsy-associated cortical tumor, most common in children and young adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, cortically-based, T2-hyperintense, 'bubbly' or multicystic lesion, typically without significant surrounding edema or enhancement, most often in the temporal lobe.

Differential
  • DNET (classic epilepsy-associated tumor)
  • Ganglioglioma
  • Low-grade glioma
Next Step

Epileptology/neurosurgery referral; often curable with resection, particularly when causing drug-resistant epilepsy.

12. Ganglioglioma

Variable

Low-grade, epilepsy-associated tumor composed of neoplastic ganglion and glial cells, most common in the temporal lobe.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortically-based, often cystic mass with a mural nodule, variable enhancement, most common in the temporal lobe of children/young adults with chronic epilepsy.

Differential
  • Ganglioglioma
  • DNET
  • Pleomorphic xanthoastrocytoma
Next Step

Epileptology/neurosurgery referral for resection, particularly in drug-resistant epilepsy.

Brain: Metastatic & Other Mass Lesions

6 findings

1. Brain metastases, MRI

Concerning

Secondary malignant brain lesions, MRI being far more sensitive than CT, particularly for small lesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple (though can be solitary), well-defined lesions at the gray-white junction with ring or nodular enhancement and disproportionate surrounding vasogenic edema.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease
  • Abscess
  • Primary glioma (if solitary)
Next Step

Oncology/neuro-oncology referral for staging and treatment planning (surgery, stereotactic radiosurgery, whole-brain radiation, systemic therapy).

2. Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis, MRI

Concerning

Diffuse malignant leptomeningeal infiltration, better detected on contrast-enhanced MRI than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffuse or nodular leptomeningeal enhancement along the sulci, cranial nerves, and/or spinal nerve roots.

Differential
  • Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis
  • Infectious/inflammatory meningitis (CSF correlation needed)
Next Step

Oncology referral; CSF cytology for confirmation given the poor prognosis and need for accurate diagnosis.

3. Epidermoid cyst, MRI (DWI restriction)

Benign

Benign congenital inclusion cyst, distinguished from an arachnoid cyst by its characteristic diffusion restriction.

Imaging Characteristics

CSF-signal on conventional T1/T2 sequences but, critically, shows restricted diffusion on DWI (unlike a simple arachnoid cyst), most often in the cerebellopontine angle.

Differential
  • Epidermoid cyst (DWI restriction is the key distinguishing feature)
  • Arachnoid cyst (no diffusion restriction)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for symptomatic lesions.

4. Arachnoid cyst, MRI

Benign

Congenital CSF-filled cyst, common incidental finding.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, CSF-signal (on all sequences) extra-axial collection without restricted diffusion or enhancement, most often in the middle cranial fossa.

Differential
  • Arachnoid cyst
  • Epidermoid cyst (restricted diffusion, key differential)
Next Step

No treatment needed for an incidental, asymptomatic cyst; neurosurgical referral if causing mass effect.

5. Colloid cyst, MRI

Variable

Benign cyst at the foramen of Monro with risk of acute obstructive hydrocephalus.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, often T1-hyperintense (proteinaceous content) round lesion at the foramen of Monro/anterosuperior third ventricle.

Differential
  • Colloid cyst (classic location)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; risk of sudden, life-threatening obstructive hydrocephalus warrants prompt evaluation for resection.

6. Choroid plexus tumor, MRI

Variable

Tumor of the choroid plexus, benign (papilloma) or malignant (carcinoma).

Imaging Characteristics

Lobulated, avidly enhancing intraventricular mass, often with a 'cauliflower' or frond-like surface texture; invasive parenchymal extension favors carcinoma over papilloma.

Differential
  • Choroid plexus papilloma (benign)
  • Choroid plexus carcinoma (invasive features)
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgical referral.

Brain: Demyelinating & White Matter Disease

8 findings

1. Multiple sclerosis, typical plaques

Concerning

Chronic demyelinating disease with characteristic periventricular and juxtacortical white matter lesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Ovoid T2/FLAIR-hyperintense lesions oriented perpendicular to the lateral ventricles, involving the periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial, and spinal cord regions per the diagnostic criteria distribution.

Differential
  • Multiple sclerosis
  • Chronic small vessel ischemic disease (typically less periventricular-perpendicular orientation, older patient)
  • Migraine-related white matter changes
Next Step

Neurology referral; McDonald criteria integrate MRI findings with clinical presentation and CSF studies for diagnosis.

2. Dawson fingers

Concerning

Characteristic MS lesion pattern from demyelination extending along periventricular veins.

Imaging Characteristics

Ovoid, periventricular T2/FLAIR-hyperintense lesions oriented perpendicular to the ventricular surface, best appreciated on sagittal FLAIR imaging.

Differential
  • Multiple sclerosis (classic and relatively specific sign)
Next Step

Neurology referral for MS diagnostic workup.

3. Acute enhancing MS plaque

Concerning

Active demyelinating lesion with blood-brain barrier breakdown, indicating recent/active disease activity.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular or incomplete ring ('open ring' or 'horseshoe') enhancement within a T2/FLAIR-hyperintense lesion, with the open portion of the ring typically facing the gray matter.

Differential
  • Active/acute multiple sclerosis plaque (open ring enhancement is relatively specific)
  • Tumefactive demyelinating lesion (larger, can mimic tumor)
Next Step

Neurology referral; enhancing lesions demonstrate disease activity relevant to treatment decisions.

4. Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder

Concerning

Autoimmune demyelinating disease distinct from MS, with a predilection for the optic nerves and spinal cord, associated with aquaporin-4 antibodies.

Imaging Characteristics

Longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions (spanning ≥3 vertebral segments) and optic nerve enhancement/swelling; brain lesions, when present, often involve the area postrema and periependymal regions.

Differential
  • Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (longitudinally extensive cord lesion is a key distinguishing feature from MS)
  • Multiple sclerosis (typically shorter segment cord lesions)
Next Step

Neurology referral; aquaporin-4 antibody testing given important treatment differences from MS.

5. Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM)

Concerning

Monophasic, often post-infectious/post-vaccination demyelinating disease, more common in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Multifocal, often large and asymmetric T2/FLAIR-hyperintense lesions involving both white and deep gray matter (basal ganglia/thalami), typically all appearing similar in age (unlike the temporally dispersed lesions of MS).

Differential
  • ADEM (monophasic, often preceded by infection/vaccination)
  • First presentation of multiple sclerosis (key differential, distinguished partly by clinical course and lesion characteristics)
Next Step

Neurology referral; supportive care and immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG) as indicated.

6. Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)

Concerning

Opportunistic JC virus infection causing demyelination, occurring in immunocompromised patients (HIV/AIDS, certain immunomodulatory therapies).

Imaging Characteristics

Asymmetric, subcortical white matter T2/FLAIR hyperintensity with a characteristic scalloped border extending to involve the subcortical U-fibers, typically without significant mass effect or enhancement (though an inflammatory variant with enhancement can occur, particularly with immune reconstitution).

Differential
  • PML (correlate with immunocompromised status, especially natalizumab or other immunomodulatory therapy)
  • HIV encephalopathy
  • Multiple sclerosis (less U-fiber involvement typically)
Next Step

Infectious disease/neurology referral; discontinue or reduce implicated immunosuppressive therapy if applicable.

7. Osmotic demyelination syndrome

Concerning

Demyelination from rapid correction of severe hyponatremia, classically affecting the central pons.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity in the central pons, sparing the peripheral pons and corticospinal tracts ('trident sign' on axial images), with imaging changes often lagging clinical symptoms by 1-2 weeks.

Differential
  • Central pontine myelinolysis
  • Extrapontine myelinolysis (basal ganglia/thalamic involvement, can co-occur)
Next Step

Neurology referral; emphasize the importance of controlled, gradual sodium correction to prevent this complication.

8. Susac syndrome

Concerning

Rare autoimmune microangiopathy affecting the brain, retina, and inner ear, with a characteristic corpus callosum lesion pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, T2-hyperintense 'snowball' lesions within the central corpus callosum (a relatively specific finding), along with cortical/deep gray matter lesions.

Differential
  • Susac syndrome (correlate with visual and hearing symptoms)
  • Multiple sclerosis (callosal lesions in Susac tend to be more central/snowball-shaped)
Next Step

Neurology/ophthalmology/ENT referral for the complete clinical triad workup (encephalopathy, branch retinal artery occlusions, hearing loss).

Brain: Infection

7 findings

1. Cerebral abscess, MRI (DWI restriction)

Concerning

Localized intracranial infection, with restricted diffusion in the necrotic center being a key distinguishing MRI feature from necrotic tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Ring-enhancing lesion with a thin, smooth capsule and marked central restricted diffusion (reflecting viscous pus), distinguishing it from the typically non-restricting necrotic center of a tumor.

Differential
  • Cerebral abscess (central DWI restriction is a key distinguishing feature)
  • Necrotic glioblastoma or metastasis (central necrosis typically does not restrict)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation for aspiration/drainage and targeted antibiotics; identify the infectious source.

2. HSV encephalitis, MRI

Concerning

Herpes simplex virus brain infection, classically involving the temporal lobes, with MRI more sensitive than CT, especially early in the disease course.

Imaging Characteristics

Asymmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and swelling involving the medial temporal lobes, insula, and inferior frontal lobes, often with restricted diffusion and gyriform enhancement; can show hemorrhagic components.

Differential
  • Herpes simplex encephalitis (medical emergency)
  • Autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (can have a similar distribution, differentiated partly by clinical course and antibody testing)
  • Ischemic infarct (territory-conforming, key differential)
Next Step

Empiric IV acyclovir should not be delayed; MRI is more sensitive than CT, especially in the first 24-48 hours.

3. Ventriculitis, MRI

Concerning

Infection of the ventricular ependymal lining.

Imaging Characteristics

Ependymal enhancement with restricted diffusion of intraventricular debris/pus, often with associated hydrocephalus.

Differential
  • Ventriculitis (often shunt/drain-associated or from ruptured abscess)
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation for shunt/drain management; targeted antibiotics per CSF culture.

4. Subdural/epidural empyema, MRI

Concerning

Purulent collection in the subdural or epidural space, with MRI (particularly DWI) more sensitive than CT for distinguishing infected from sterile collections.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing collection with restricted diffusion (distinguishing infected pus from a sterile effusion/hygroma), often with adjacent sinus or mastoid disease.

Differential
  • Subdural/epidural empyema (surgical emergency)
  • Sterile subdural effusion/hygroma (no restricted diffusion)
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical consultation for drainage plus targeted antibiotics.

5. Neurocysticercosis, MRI

Variable

Parasitic CNS infection with characteristic stage-dependent MRI appearance.

Imaging Characteristics

Vesicular stage: cystic lesion with a visible mural scolex, no surrounding edema. Colloidal vesicular stage: cyst with surrounding edema and enhancement (most symptomatic). Nodular/calcified stage: small calcified nodule, best seen on CT/gradient-echo sequences.

Differential
  • Neurocysticercosis (stage determines management approach)
Next Step

Neurology/infectious disease referral; antiparasitic therapy approach depends on cyst stage and number.

6. Toxoplasmosis, MRI

Concerning

Opportunistic CNS infection in immunocompromised patients (classically HIV/AIDS with low CD4 count).

Imaging Characteristics

Multiple lesions with a characteristic 'target' or eccentric nodular enhancement pattern, most often in the basal ganglia, with surrounding edema.

Differential
  • CNS toxoplasmosis (classic in HIV/AIDS with CD4 <100)
  • CNS lymphoma (key differential; thallium SPECT or response to empiric anti-toxoplasmosis therapy can help distinguish)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; empiric anti-toxoplasmosis therapy with follow-up imaging is a common diagnostic approach given the overlap with lymphoma.

7. Cryptococcal/fungal CNS infection

Concerning

Fungal CNS infection, often in immunocompromised patients, with characteristic gelatinous pseudocyst formation.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated perivascular spaces filled with gelatinous material (cryptococcal pseudocysts), most often in the basal ganglia, sometimes with associated meningeal enhancement.

Differential
  • Cryptococcal meningoencephalitis (classic in HIV/AIDS or other immunocompromise)
  • Other fungal infections (Aspergillus, Mucor, particularly in neutropenic or diabetic ketoacidosis patients)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; CSF cryptococcal antigen testing and antifungal therapy.

Brain: Epilepsy-Related

4 findings

1. Mesial temporal sclerosis

Concerning

Hippocampal atrophy and gliosis, the most common cause of temporal lobe epilepsy amenable to surgical treatment.

Imaging Characteristics

Hippocampal volume loss with T2/FLAIR hyperintensity and loss of normal internal architecture, often with associated ipsilateral fornix atrophy and temporal horn dilation.

Differential
  • Mesial temporal sclerosis (often asymmetric/unilateral)
  • Bilateral hippocampal atrophy from another cause (e.g., early Alzheimer disease, key differential in older patients)
Next Step

Epileptology referral; dedicated epilepsy-protocol MRI and video-EEG monitoring for surgical candidacy evaluation.

2. Focal cortical dysplasia, MRI

Concerning

Localized abnormality of cortical development, an important surgically treatable cause of drug-resistant focal epilepsy.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal cortical thickening, blurring of the gray-white junction, and sometimes a T2/FLAIR-hyperintense 'transmantle sign' extending from the cortex to the ventricle, best seen on dedicated epilepsy-protocol MRI.

Differential
  • Focal cortical dysplasia (subtle, often requires dedicated protocol and expert review)
  • Low-grade glioma
  • Tuberous sclerosis-related cortical tuber
Next Step

Epileptology/neurosurgery referral for dedicated imaging and surgical evaluation.

3. Hypothalamic hamartoma

Variable

Congenital, non-neoplastic mass of the hypothalamus/tuber cinereum, classically causing gelastic (laughing) seizures and precocious puberty.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined mass at the tuber cinereum/hypothalamus, isointense to gray matter without enhancement or growth over time.

Differential
  • Hypothalamic hamartoma (classic association with gelastic seizures)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology/endocrine/neurosurgery referral for the associated precocious puberty and refractory epilepsy.

4. Rasmussen encephalitis

Concerning

Rare, progressive autoimmune/inflammatory condition causing unihemispheric atrophy and drug-resistant epilepsy, most common in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Progressive unilateral cerebral hemispheric atrophy with T2/FLAIR hyperintensity, best appreciated on serial imaging showing progression over time.

Differential
  • Rasmussen encephalitis (progressive unihemispheric pattern is characteristic)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology/epileptology referral; hemispherectomy is a definitive treatment option for refractory cases.

Brain: Vascular

7 findings

1. Cerebral aneurysm, MRA

Variable

Focal vessel outpouching, characterized non-invasively by MR angiography.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, rounded or saccular outpouching from a cerebral artery, most often at branch points of the circle of Willis.

Differential
  • Unruptured saccular aneurysm
  • Infundibulum (normal variant, key benign mimic)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurointerventional referral for risk-benefit discussion based on size, location, and patient factors.

2. Arteriovenous malformation, MRI

Concerning

Congenital tangle of abnormal vessels with direct arteriovenous shunting.

Imaging Characteristics

Tangle of flow voids ('bag of worms') on conventional MRI sequences, with feeding arteries and early draining veins better characterized on MRA/catheter angiography.

Differential
  • Brain AVM
Next Step

Neurosurgical/neurointerventional referral for risk assessment and treatment planning.

3. Cavernous malformation, MRI

Variable

Vascular malformation with abnormal, low-flow vascular channels, far more sensitively detected on MRI (especially susceptibility-weighted imaging) than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

'Popcorn ball' appearance with mixed signal core (reflecting blood products of varying ages) surrounded by a complete hemosiderin rim, best seen on gradient-echo/SWI sequences.

Differential
  • Cavernous malformation (classic 'popcorn' appearance)
  • Developmental venous anomaly with an associated cavernoma (common co-occurrence)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral if symptomatic (seizures, hemorrhage); asymptomatic incidental lesions often observed.

4. Dural arteriovenous fistula, MRI/MRA

Concerning

Acquired abnormal connection between a dural arterial branch and a venous sinus or cortical vein.

Imaging Characteristics

Prominent flow voids along the expected course of a dural venous sinus, with abnormal early venous filling on dynamic MRA sequences.

Differential
  • Dural AV fistula (cortical venous reflux indicates higher hemorrhage risk)
Next Step

Neurointerventional referral; catheter angiography remains the gold standard for full characterization.

5. Moyamoya disease

Concerning

Progressive stenosis of the distal internal carotid arteries with formation of an abnormal collateral vascular network.

Imaging Characteristics

Progressive narrowing of the distal ICAs and proximal circle of Willis vessels with an extensive network of small, flow-void collateral vessels at the base of the brain ('puff of smoke' appearance on angiography).

Differential
  • Moyamoya disease (idiopathic, more common in East Asian populations and children)
  • Moyamoya syndrome (associated with sickle cell disease, neurofibromatosis, prior radiation, and other conditions)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for consideration of revascularization surgery given progressive stroke risk.

6. Vertebral artery dissection

Concerning

Tear in the vertebral artery wall, from trauma or spontaneous, with stroke risk from thromboembolism or occlusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic intramural hematoma (T1-hyperintense on fat-saturated sequences) narrowing the true vessel lumen, best seen on axial fat-saturated T1 imaging through the neck.

Differential
  • Traumatic vertebral artery dissection
  • Spontaneous dissection (consider underlying connective tissue disorder if young/atraumatic)
Next Step

Neurology/vascular neurology referral; antithrombotic therapy (antiplatelet or anticoagulation per current evidence) to reduce stroke risk.

7. Carotid artery dissection

Concerning

Tear in the carotid artery wall, a significant cause of stroke in younger patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Crescentic intramural hematoma narrowing the true lumen of the internal carotid artery, best seen on axial fat-saturated T1 imaging, often extending from just above the carotid bifurcation.

Differential
  • Traumatic carotid dissection
  • Spontaneous dissection (correlate with connective tissue disorder history, cervical manipulation, or minor trauma)
Next Step

Neurology/vascular referral; antithrombotic therapy to reduce embolic stroke risk.

Brain: Hydrocephalus & CSF Disorders

4 findings

1. Obstructive hydrocephalus, MRI

Concerning

Ventricular dilation from a mechanical CSF flow obstruction, with MRI often better identifying the causative lesion than CT.

Imaging Characteristics

Ventricular dilation proximal to the level of obstruction, with periventricular T2/FLAIR hyperintensity from transependymal CSF flow in acute/decompensated cases; MRI can directly visualize the causative lesion (tumor, aqueductal stenosis).

Differential
  • Aqueductal stenosis
  • Mass lesion obstructing CSF pathways
  • Intraventricular hemorrhage with clot obstruction
Next Step

Neurosurgical consultation; may require urgent CSF diversion if acute and symptomatic.

2. Normal pressure hydrocephalus, MRI (DESH pattern)

Variable

Chronic communicating hydrocephalus in older adults with the classic clinical triad.

Imaging Characteristics

Ventriculomegaly disproportionate to sulcal atrophy, callosal angle <90 degrees, and disproportionately enlarged Sylvian fissures relative to high-convexity sulci (DESH pattern).

Differential
  • Normal pressure hydrocephalus
  • Age-related ventriculomegaly/atrophy (proportionate enlargement)
Next Step

Neurology/neurosurgery referral; large-volume lumbar puncture as a diagnostic/prognostic test before shunt consideration.

3. Chiari I malformation with syrinx

Concerning

Tonsillar herniation associated with a spinal cord syrinx, indicating impaired CSF flow dynamics at the craniocervical junction.

Imaging Characteristics

Cerebellar tonsils ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum with an associated fluid-filled cavity (syrinx) within the cervical or thoracic spinal cord.

Differential
  • Chiari I malformation with syrinx (higher likelihood of symptoms/need for intervention than Chiari I alone)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; posterior fossa decompression is often considered given the presence of a syrinx.

4. Idiopathic intracranial hypertension, MRI signs

Variable

Elevated intracranial pressure without a structural cause, with several supportive but nonspecific MRI findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Flattening of the posterior globes, an empty or partially empty sella, distension of the optic nerve sheaths, and sometimes transverse sinus stenosis.

Differential
  • Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumor cerebri)
  • Dural venous sinus thrombosis (important to exclude with MR venography)
Next Step

MR venography to exclude venous sinus thrombosis; ophthalmology and neurology referral.

Brain: Neurodegenerative Disease

8 findings

1. Alzheimer disease, MRI pattern

Variable

Most common cause of neurodegenerative dementia, with characteristic medial temporal predominant atrophy.

Imaging Characteristics

Disproportionate hippocampal and medial temporal lobe atrophy relative to overall brain volume loss, with associated temporal horn dilation.

Differential
  • Alzheimer disease
  • Normal age-related atrophy (less medial temporal-predominant)
Next Step

Neurology referral for cognitive workup; MRI supports but does not solely establish the diagnosis.

2. Frontotemporal dementia, MRI pattern

Concerning

Neurodegenerative disease with predominant frontal and/or temporal lobe atrophy, often with an asymmetric pattern.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or asymmetric frontal and/or anterior temporal lobe atrophy, often more pronounced than the medial temporal pattern of Alzheimer disease.

Differential
  • Frontotemporal dementia (behavioral or language variants have somewhat different atrophy patterns)
  • Alzheimer disease (more medial temporal/parietal predominant)
Next Step

Neurology referral for further cognitive and genetic workup.

3. Parkinson disease, MRI signs

Variable

Neurodegenerative movement disorder, with conventional MRI often normal or nonspecific; newer sequences can show substantia nigra changes.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal 'swallow tail' sign (dorsolateral nigral hyperintensity) on high-resolution susceptibility imaging of the substantia nigra; conventional MRI is often unremarkable and primarily used to exclude mimics.

Differential
  • Idiopathic Parkinson disease
  • Atypical parkinsonian syndromes (multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy - different imaging patterns)
Next Step

Neurology referral; MRI is primarily used to exclude structural mimics and atypical parkinsonian syndromes.

4. Progressive supranuclear palsy, MRI (hummingbird sign)

Concerning

Atypical parkinsonian syndrome with characteristic midbrain atrophy.

Imaging Characteristics

Midbrain atrophy with a preserved pons, creating the 'hummingbird sign' (or 'penguin sign') on midsagittal imaging, along with a reduced midbrain-to-pons ratio.

Differential
  • Progressive supranuclear palsy (relatively specific imaging sign when present)
Next Step

Neurology referral for movement disorder specialist evaluation.

5. Multiple system atrophy, MRI (hot cross bun sign)

Concerning

Atypical parkinsonian syndrome with cerebellar and/or autonomic features, associated with characteristic pontine signal change.

Imaging Characteristics

Cruciform T2-hyperintense signal within the pons ('hot cross bun sign') from selective degeneration of pontocerebellar fibers, often with cerebellar and middle cerebellar peduncle atrophy.

Differential
  • Multiple system atrophy (relatively specific sign when present, though not always seen)
Next Step

Neurology referral for movement disorder/autonomic specialist evaluation.

6. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, MRI

Concerning

Motor neuron disease with occasionally supportive but nonspecific MRI findings.

Imaging Characteristics

T2/FLAIR hyperintensity along the corticospinal tracts (internal capsule, brainstem) in some patients, along with precentral gyrus atrophy in more advanced disease; MRI is often normal early in the disease.

Differential
  • Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (imaging findings support but do not establish diagnosis, which remains primarily clinical/electrophysiologic)
Next Step

Neurology referral; MRI is primarily used to exclude structural mimics of motor neuron disease.

7. Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease

Concerning

Rapidly progressive prion disease with characteristic diffusion-weighted imaging findings.

Imaging Characteristics

Cortical ribboning (gyriform restricted diffusion) and/or basal ganglia restricted diffusion on DWI, often the most sensitive sequence, out of proportion to changes on conventional T2/FLAIR.

Differential
  • Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (DWI cortical ribboning is a highly suggestive finding in the appropriate clinical context)
  • Autoimmune/limbic encephalitis (can have overlapping features)
  • Hypoxic-ischemic injury (bilateral, more symmetric, clinical context differs)
Next Step

Neurology referral; CSF studies (14-3-3 protein, RT-QuIC) and EEG support the diagnosis given the rapidly progressive, ultimately fatal course.

8. Wernicke encephalopathy

Concerning

Thiamine deficiency-related encephalopathy, classically in alcohol use disorder or malnutrition, a reversible condition if treated promptly.

Imaging Characteristics

Symmetric T2/FLAIR hyperintensity involving the mammillary bodies, periaqueductal gray matter, medial thalami, and tectal plate, sometimes with associated enhancement.

Differential
  • Wernicke encephalopathy (classic locations, though imaging can be normal, especially early)
Next Step

Emergent thiamine repletion should not be delayed for imaging confirmation given the reversibility of early disease and risk of progression to irreversible Korsakoff syndrome.

Brain: Congenital & Pediatric

8 findings

1. Agenesis of the corpus callosum, MRI

Variable

Congenital absence of the corpus callosum, complete or partial.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent or partial corpus callosum with parallel, widely separated lateral ventricles ('racing car sign' on axial images), colpocephaly, and often an associated interhemispheric cyst or lipoma.

Differential
  • Complete or partial callosal agenesis
  • Associated syndromic conditions
Next Step

Neurology/genetics referral; correlate with developmental status and screen for associated anomalies.

2. Chiari II malformation

Concerning

Complex hindbrain malformation almost universally associated with myelomeningocele, distinct from and more severe than Chiari I.

Imaging Characteristics

Small posterior fossa with downward displacement of the cerebellar vermis, brainstem, and fourth ventricle through the foramen magnum, along with characteristic supratentorial findings (beaked tectum, callosal dysgenesis, and others).

Differential
  • Chiari II malformation (essentially always associated with myelomeningocele)
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgery referral; associated with the underlying spinal dysraphism requiring its own management.

3. Dandy-Walker malformation, MRI

Concerning

Posterior fossa malformation with vermian hypoplasia/agenesis and cystic fourth ventricle dilation.

Imaging Characteristics

Large posterior fossa cyst continuous with a dilated fourth ventricle, cerebellar vermian hypoplasia/agenesis, and an enlarged posterior fossa with elevated torcular herophili.

Differential
  • Dandy-Walker malformation (classic triad)
  • Mega cisterna magna (normal vermis, key differential)
  • Blake pouch cyst
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgery/neurology referral; associated hydrocephalus is common.

4. Lissencephaly

Concerning

Severe malformation of cortical development with absent or reduced gyral folding.

Imaging Characteristics

Smooth brain surface with absent (agyria) or reduced/broadened (pachygyria) gyri, a thickened cortex, and a characteristic 'figure-8' or 'hourglass' axial brain contour in the most severe forms.

Differential
  • Lissencephaly (genetic causes include LIS1 and DCX mutations)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology/genetics referral; associated with severe developmental impairment and epilepsy.

5. Polymicrogyria

Concerning

Malformation of cortical development with excessive, small, irregular gyri.

Imaging Characteristics

Irregular, small, excessive gyral pattern with an irregular gray-white junction, which can be focal or diffuse and unilateral or bilateral.

Differential
  • Polymicrogyria (various genetic and acquired/vascular causes)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology referral; correlate with developmental status and seizure history.

6. Gray matter heterotopia

Concerning

Malformation of cortical development where neurons fail to migrate fully to the cortex, resulting in ectopic gray matter.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular or band-like foci of gray matter signal (isointense to normal cortex on all sequences) located along the ventricular margins (subependymal) or within the white matter (subcortical), an important cause of epilepsy.

Differential
  • Subependymal (periventricular nodular) heterotopia
  • Subcortical band heterotopia
Next Step

Pediatric neurology/epileptology referral; associated with drug-resistant epilepsy in many cases.

7. Schizencephaly

Concerning

Congenital cleft extending from the ventricle to the pial surface, lined by gray matter (polymicrogyric cortex).

Imaging Characteristics

Gray-matter-lined cleft extending from the ependymal (ventricular) surface to the pial surface; 'closed-lip' clefts have apposed walls, while 'open-lip' clefts have a CSF-filled gap.

Differential
  • Closed-lip schizencephaly
  • Open-lip schizencephaly (more severe, larger CSF-filled defect)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology referral; correlate with developmental status and seizure history.

8. Holoprosencephaly

Concerning

Severe malformation from incomplete cleavage of the embryonic forebrain, ranging from alobar (most severe) to lobar (least severe).

Imaging Characteristics

Alobar: single, fused ventricular cavity with fused thalami and absent falx/interhemispheric fissure. Lobar (least severe): near-normal hemispheric separation with more subtle midline fusion abnormalities.

Differential
  • Alobar holoprosencephaly (most severe)
  • Semilobar holoprosencephaly
  • Lobar holoprosencephaly (least severe)
Next Step

Pediatric neurology/genetics referral; often associated with midline facial anomalies and chromosomal abnormalities.

Brain: Pituitary & Sellar

5 findings

1. Pituitary microadenoma

Variable

Small (<1 cm) pituitary tumor, often functional (hormone-secreting), best detected with dedicated dynamic contrast-enhanced pituitary MRI.

Imaging Characteristics

Small focus of relatively delayed/reduced enhancement compared to the normal pituitary gland on dynamic post-contrast imaging, sometimes with associated gland asymmetry or infundibular deviation.

Differential
  • Functional microadenoma (prolactinoma most common)
  • Non-functioning microadenoma
  • Normal pituitary gland variant/artifact
Next Step

Endocrine referral for hormonal workup; dedicated dynamic pituitary MRI protocol for optimal detection.

2. Pituitary macroadenoma

Concerning

Pituitary tumor ≥1 cm, with risk of mass effect on the optic chiasm and cavernous sinus invasion.

Imaging Characteristics

Sellar mass ≥1 cm, often with suprasellar extension causing optic chiasm compression ('figure-8' or 'snowman' configuration as it extends through the diaphragma sellae) and variable cavernous sinus invasion.

Differential
  • Pituitary macroadenoma (most common sellar mass in adults)
  • Craniopharyngioma
  • Meningioma (suprasellar/planum sphenoidale)
Next Step

Endocrine and neurosurgical referral; formal visual field testing given optic chiasm compression risk.

3. Pituitary apoplexy

Concerning

Acute hemorrhage and/or infarction of the pituitary gland, often within a pre-existing adenoma, a neuroendocrine emergency.

Imaging Characteristics

Acute hemorrhage (T1-hyperintense) within an enlarged pituitary gland/adenoma, often with associated sellar expansion and optic chiasm compression.

Differential
  • Pituitary apoplexy (classic presentation: sudden severe headache, visual changes, ophthalmoplegia)
Next Step

Emergent endocrine/neurosurgical consultation; assess for acute adrenal insufficiency requiring urgent stress-dose steroids.

4. Rathke cleft cyst, MRI

Benign

Benign, congenital sellar cyst.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, non-enhancing cystic sellar/suprasellar lesion with variable T1/T2 signal depending on internal proteinaceous content, typically without the calcification of craniopharyngioma.

Differential
  • Rathke cleft cyst
  • Craniopharyngioma (calcification and more heterogeneous solid components favor this)
Next Step

Endocrine/neurosurgical referral if symptomatic.

5. Lymphocytic hypophysitis

Concerning

Autoimmune inflammation of the pituitary gland, classically in peripartum women, increasingly recognized with immune checkpoint inhibitor cancer therapy.

Imaging Characteristics

Diffusely enlarged, symmetrically and homogeneously enhancing pituitary gland with thickened infundibulum, without the more focal/heterogeneous appearance of an adenoma.

Differential
  • Lymphocytic hypophysitis (peripartum or checkpoint inhibitor-associated)
  • Pituitary macroadenoma (more heterogeneous, less symmetric)
Next Step

Endocrine referral; correlate with immunotherapy history and assess for anterior/posterior pituitary hormone deficiencies.

Brain: Incidental & Miscellaneous

6 findings

1. Developmental venous anomaly, MRI

Benign

Congenital variant venous drainage pattern, the most common cerebral vascular malformation.

Imaging Characteristics

Cluster of small medullary veins converging into a larger collecting vein ('caput medusae' pattern) on post-contrast imaging or susceptibility sequences.

Differential
  • Developmental venous anomaly (benign in isolation)
  • Associated cavernous malformation (the actual hemorrhage risk, worth specifically screening for)
Next Step

No treatment needed for the DVA itself; note if an adjacent cavernous malformation is present.

2. Perivascular (Virchow-Robin) spaces

Benign

Normal, fluid-filled spaces surrounding penetrating blood vessels, common and usually incidental, though can be prominent.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, round or linear foci following CSF signal on all sequences (including FLAIR suppression), typically in the basal ganglia or centrum semiovale, following the expected course of penetrating vessels.

Differential
  • Normal perivascular spaces (common, especially with age)
  • Lacunar infarct (does not fully suppress on FLAIR and may show diffusion restriction if acute, key differential)
Next Step

No action needed for typical, small perivascular spaces.

3. Nonspecific white matter hyperintensities

Variable

Small foci of T2/FLAIR signal change in the white matter without a specific diagnostic pattern, extremely common with age.

Imaging Characteristics

Punctate or patchy T2/FLAIR hyperintense foci in the subcortical or deep white matter, without the specific distribution pattern of MS, ADEM, or other defined entities.

Differential
  • Age-related chronic small vessel ischemic change (most common in older adults)
  • Migraine-related white matter changes
  • Early demyelinating disease (correlate with distribution and clinical context)
Next Step

Correlate with age, vascular risk factors, and clinical context; further workup only if the pattern or clinical presentation suggests a specific underlying process.

4. Pineal cyst, MRI

Benign

Common benign cystic lesion of the pineal gland.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, T2-hyperintense cystic lesion, typically <1-1.5 cm, sometimes with a thin rim of enhancement.

Differential
  • Pineal cyst (common incidental finding)
  • Cystic pineal tumor (larger or atypical features warrant further characterization)
Next Step

No follow-up needed for a small, classic pineal cyst.

5. Empty sella, MRI

Variable

Flattened pituitary gland with CSF filling the sella, from an incompetent diaphragma sellae.

Imaging Characteristics

CSF signal filling the sella turcica with a thin, flattened pituitary gland along the sellar floor.

Differential
  • Primary empty sella (idiopathic, often incidental)
  • Secondary empty sella (post-surgical, post-radiation, post-infarction of a tumor)
Next Step

Correlate with pituitary function if clinically indicated; usually incidental.

6. Central pontine incidental signal change

Variable

Nonspecific signal change within the pons identified incidentally, requiring correlation to distinguish benign from pathologic causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse T2/FLAIR signal change within the pons, without a clearly diagnostic pattern.

Differential
  • Chronic small vessel ischemic change
  • Incidental perivascular space
  • Early/subtle demyelinating or osmotic demyelination change (correlate with clinical history)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context (sodium correction history, vascular risk factors); further workup only if the pattern or symptoms suggest a specific process.

Spine: Degenerative Disease

10 findings

1. Disc herniation, MRI (protrusion/extrusion/sequestration)

Variable

Displacement of disc material beyond the normal disc space margins, with MRI as the definitive modality for characterization.

Imaging Characteristics

Protrusion: base wider than the herniated portion. Extrusion: herniated portion wider than the base, or discontinuous from the parent disc. Sequestration: free fragment fully separated from the parent disc, potentially migrating within the canal.

Differential
  • Disc protrusion (broad-based, less likely to require surgery)
  • Disc extrusion
  • Sequestered/free fragment (may migrate and cause symptoms at a different level than expected)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical radiculopathy/myelopathy; spine surgery/pain management referral for symptomatic cases.

2. Central spinal canal stenosis, MRI

Variable

Narrowing of the central spinal canal, from combined disc, facet, and ligamentous factors.

Imaging Characteristics

Reduced canal cross-sectional area with effacement of the surrounding CSF signal around the thecal sac, graded qualitatively (mild/moderate/severe) based on CSF effacement and cord/cauda equina crowding.

Differential
  • Degenerative spinal stenosis (most common)
  • Congenital short pedicle stenosis (predisposes to earlier symptomatic degenerative stenosis)
Next Step

Correlate with neurogenic claudication or myelopathic symptoms; spine surgery/pain management referral if symptomatic.

3. Neural foraminal stenosis, MRI

Variable

Narrowing of the neural foramen, well characterized on sagittal MRI sequences.

Imaging Characteristics

Loss of the normal perineural fat signal surrounding the exiting nerve root, from combined disc, facet, and uncovertebral (cervical) hypertrophy.

Differential
  • Degenerative foraminal stenosis
Next Step

Correlate with radicular symptoms in the corresponding dermatomal distribution.

4. Facet arthropathy, MRI

Benign

Degenerative facet joint change, well seen on MRI including any associated synovial reaction/effusion.

Imaging Characteristics

Facet joint space narrowing, T2-hyperintense effusion, and osteophyte formation, which can contribute to foraminal and central canal narrowing.

Differential
  • Age-related degenerative facet arthropathy
Next Step

Correlate with symptoms; conservative management (physical therapy, facet injections) for symptomatic cases.

5. Modic endplate changes

Variable

Vertebral endplate signal changes associated with degenerative disc disease, classified into three types reflecting different underlying pathology.

Imaging Characteristics

Type 1: T1-hypointense, T2-hyperintense (edema/inflammation, often the most symptomatic and can mimic infection). Type 2: T1-hyperintense, T2-isointense/hyperintense (fatty replacement, more chronic/stable). Type 3: T1- and T2-hypointense (subchondral sclerosis).

Differential
  • Modic type 1 (inflammatory, can be difficult to distinguish from early discitis without correlating clinical/lab findings)
  • Modic type 2 (fatty, chronic degenerative)
  • Modic type 3 (sclerotic, chronic degenerative)
Next Step

Correlate Modic type 1 changes with infectious symptoms/inflammatory markers if there is any diagnostic uncertainty with discitis.

6. Synovial cyst, spine

Variable

Cystic outpouching from a degenerated facet joint, which can cause canal or foraminal compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense cystic lesion adjacent to and communicating with a degenerated facet joint, most often at L4-L5, sometimes causing significant canal narrowing.

Differential
  • Facet synovial cyst (degenerative)
  • Ganglion cyst (similar appearance, may not directly communicate with the joint)
Next Step

Correlate with radicular/stenosis symptoms; pain management (aspiration/injection) or surgical referral if symptomatic.

7. Ligamentum flavum hypertrophy

Variable

Thickening of the ligamentum flavum, a major contributor to degenerative central canal stenosis.

Imaging Characteristics

Thickened (>4-5 mm), often T2-hypointense ligamentum flavum contributing to posterior canal encroachment, particularly at the lumbar spine.

Differential
  • Degenerative ligamentum flavum hypertrophy
Next Step

Correlate with stenosis symptoms; contributes to overall stenosis severity assessment.

8. Degenerative spondylolisthesis, MRI

Variable

Anterior vertebral slippage from facet incompetence, with MRI assessing the associated canal/foraminal compromise.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior displacement of a vertebral body (most often L4 on L5) with intact pars interarticularis, often associated with facet degeneration and canal/foraminal narrowing at that level.

Differential
  • Degenerative spondylolisthesis (facet-mediated)
  • Isthmic spondylolisthesis (pars defect, key differential)
Next Step

Correlate with stenosis symptoms; spine surgery referral if significant stenosis or instability symptoms present.

9. OPLL, MRI

Concerning

Ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament causing spinal canal narrowing, most common in the cervical spine.

Imaging Characteristics

T1/T2-hypointense ossified band along the posterior vertebral body margins, causing variable canal compromise and sometimes cord signal change if severe.

Differential
  • OPLL
Next Step

Spine surgery referral if causing significant canal stenosis or myelopathic symptoms (cord T2 signal change).

10. Baastrup disease, MRI

Benign

Degenerative approximation of adjacent spinous processes with an interspinous bursa-like reaction.

Imaging Characteristics

Closely apposed, sclerotic adjacent spinous processes with a T2-hyperintense fluid collection (bursa) between them, most common at the lower lumbar spine.

Differential
  • Baastrup disease
Next Step

Conservative management (physical therapy, injections); usually not requiring surgery.

Spine: Infection

5 findings

1. Discitis-osteomyelitis, MRI

Concerning

Infection of the intervertebral disc and adjacent vertebral endplates, with MRI as the most sensitive and specific imaging modality.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense disc signal with endplate destruction, adjacent vertebral body T1-hypointense/T2-hyperintense marrow edema, and often associated paraspinal or epidural extension with enhancement.

Differential
  • Pyogenic discitis-osteomyelitis (most common bacterial cause: Staph aureus)
  • Tuberculous spondylitis (more indolent course, larger paraspinal abscess, relative disc preservation early)
  • Modic type 1 endplate change (inflammatory but non-infectious, key differential)
Next Step

Blood cultures and possible image-guided biopsy for organism identification; MRI for epidural extension assessment.

2. Spinal epidural abscess, MRI

Concerning

Purulent collection in the epidural space, a neurosurgical emergency given spinal cord/cauda equina compression risk.

Imaging Characteristics

Epidural collection with rim enhancement causing variable thecal sac/cord compression, often contiguous with adjacent discitis-osteomyelitis; assess the full craniocaudal extent given the propensity to track along the epidural space.

Differential
  • Spinal epidural abscess (surgical emergency with any neurologic deficit)
  • Epidural hematoma (differentiate by clinical context and signal characteristics)
  • Epidural malignancy
Next Step

Emergent spine surgery/neurosurgery consultation, particularly with any neurologic deficit.

3. Paraspinal abscess, MRI

Concerning

Infection extending into the paraspinal soft tissues.

Imaging Characteristics

Rim-enhancing, T2-hyperintense fluid collection within the paraspinal musculature, often contiguous with adjacent vertebral/disc infection.

Differential
  • Paraspinal abscess from contiguous discitis-osteomyelitis
  • Tuberculous (Pott) abscess (classically tracks along the psoas)
Next Step

IR/surgical drainage plus antibiotics.

4. Tuberculous spondylitis (Pott disease), MRI

Concerning

Spinal tuberculosis with characteristic indolent vertebral destruction and large paraspinal abscesses.

Imaging Characteristics

Vertebral body destruction with anterior wedging, relatively preserved disc space early in the disease course, and large, often multi-level, paraspinal/psoas abscess collections with peripheral enhancement.

Differential
  • Tuberculous spondylitis (endemic area exposure history important)
  • Pyogenic discitis-osteomyelitis (earlier disc involvement, smaller abscesses)
  • Metastatic disease (can mimic destructive pattern)
Next Step

Infectious disease referral; AFB testing/biopsy for confirmation and multidrug antituberculous therapy.

5. Facet joint septic arthritis, MRI

Concerning

Infection of a facet joint, well characterized by MRI including any associated epidural extension.

Imaging Characteristics

Facet joint effusion with erosive changes, surrounding soft-tissue enhancement, and sometimes an associated epidural abscess extending from the infected joint.

Differential
  • Facet joint septic arthritis
  • Degenerative facet effusion (non-infected, no surrounding enhancement/destructive change)
Next Step

Antibiotics and possible drainage; correlate with fever and inflammatory markers.

Spine: Tumor & Neoplasm

10 findings

1. Vertebral metastasis, MRI

Concerning

Secondary malignant vertebral lesion, with MRI as the most sensitive modality for marrow involvement and cord compression assessment.

Imaging Characteristics

T1-hypointense, variable T2 signal replacing normal fatty marrow signal within the vertebral body and/or posterior elements, with possible epidural extension and cord/thecal sac compression.

Differential
  • Metastatic disease (most common)
  • Multiple myeloma/plasmacytoma
  • Primary vertebral tumor
Next Step

Oncology referral for staging; urgent MRI and radiation/surgical oncology consultation with any cord compression or neurologic symptoms.

2. Multiple myeloma / plasmacytoma, MRI

Concerning

Plasma cell malignancy causing marrow-replacing vertebral lesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal or diffuse T1-hypointense marrow replacement, with patterns ranging from focal lesions to diffuse infiltration to a 'salt and pepper' heterogeneous appearance.

Differential
  • Multiple myeloma (typically multifocal/diffuse)
  • Solitary plasmacytoma
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Hematology/oncology referral; whole-body MRI or PET-CT and laboratory workup for staging.

3. Vertebral hemangioma, MRI

Benign

Common benign vascular vertebral lesion.

Imaging Characteristics

T1- and T2-hyperintense vertebral body lesion (reflecting fat and vascular content) with a characteristic 'polka-dot' pattern on axial images from coarsened trabeculae.

Differential
  • Typical vertebral hemangioma (benign)
  • Aggressive hemangioma (larger, extraosseous soft-tissue extension, less common)
Next Step

No action needed for a typical, asymptomatic vertebral hemangioma.

4. Intramedullary astrocytoma

Concerning

Primary spinal cord tumor arising from astrocytes, most common intramedullary tumor in children.

Imaging Characteristics

Ill-defined, expansile intramedullary mass with variable, often patchy enhancement and associated cord edema/syrinx.

Differential
  • Intramedullary astrocytoma
  • Ependymoma (more common in adults, tends to be better circumscribed)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for biopsy/resection.

5. Intramedullary ependymoma

Concerning

Most common intramedullary spinal cord tumor in adults.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-circumscribed, centrally located intramedullary mass with homogeneous enhancement, often with a 'cap sign' (hemosiderin at the tumor poles) and associated syrinx.

Differential
  • Intramedullary ependymoma (most common adult intramedullary tumor)
  • Astrocytoma
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; generally more amenable to complete resection than astrocytoma given its well-circumscribed nature.

6. Extramedullary intradural schwannoma

Variable

Benign nerve sheath tumor, the most common extramedullary intradural spinal tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, avidly enhancing extramedullary intradural mass, often with a 'dumbbell' shape extending through the neural foramen.

Differential
  • Schwannoma (most common)
  • Neurofibroma (less well-encapsulated, associated with neurofibromatosis if multiple)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for resection if symptomatic or growing.

7. Extramedullary intradural meningioma

Variable

Benign tumor arising from the spinal meninges, most common in the thoracic spine of older women.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, dural-based, avidly enhancing extramedullary intradural mass, often with a dural tail sign similar to intracranial meningiomas.

Differential
  • Spinal meningioma (most common thoracic extramedullary intradural tumor)
  • Schwannoma
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for resection if symptomatic or causing cord compression.

8. Chordoma, MRI

Concerning

Rare, locally aggressive malignant tumor from notochordal remnants, most often sacrococcygeal.

Imaging Characteristics

Destructive, midline mass, typically markedly T2-hyperintense, with an associated soft-tissue component, most often at the sacrum.

Differential
  • Chordoma (classic sacrococcygeal location)
  • Chondrosarcoma
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Spine oncology/surgical referral for biopsy and resection planning.

9. Drop metastases (leptomeningeal spinal seeding)

Concerning

Spinal leptomeningeal metastases from a primary CNS tumor seeding via CSF pathways, or from a systemic malignancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Nodular or diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement along the spinal cord surface and nerve roots ('sugar coating' appearance in extensive disease).

Differential
  • Drop metastases from a primary CNS tumor (medulloblastoma, ependymoma, high-grade glioma)
  • Leptomeningeal carcinomatosis from a systemic primary
Next Step

Oncology referral; full neuraxis staging and treatment planning.

10. Sacral chordoma vs. other sacral tumor

Concerning

Differentiating sacral chordoma from other primary and secondary sacral tumors given overlapping imaging appearances.

Imaging Characteristics

Chordoma is typically midline and markedly T2-hyperintense; chondrosarcoma may show characteristic chondroid matrix; metastases and giant cell tumor have differing signal and enhancement characteristics.

Differential
  • Chordoma
  • Chondrosarcoma
  • Giant cell tumor of bone
  • Metastatic disease
Next Step

Spine oncology referral; biopsy is generally needed for definitive tissue diagnosis given overlapping imaging features.

Spine: Trauma & Cord Injury

8 findings

1. Spinal cord contusion/edema

Concerning

Traumatic spinal cord injury without frank transection, with MRI as the key modality for prognostication.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense signal within the spinal cord at the level of injury, with the craniocaudal length of signal change correlating with injury severity and prognosis; associated susceptibility signal indicates hemorrhagic contusion (worse prognosis).

Differential
  • Spinal cord contusion/edema (non-hemorrhagic, better prognosis)
  • Hemorrhagic cord contusion (worse prognosis)
Next Step

Neurosurgical/spine consultation; MRI findings inform prognosis and surgical timing decisions.

2. Spinal cord transection

Concerning

Complete traumatic disruption of the spinal cord, a devastating injury with permanent neurologic consequences.

Imaging Characteristics

Complete discontinuity of the spinal cord at the level of injury, often with associated severe fracture-dislocation and cord/canal disruption.

Differential
  • Complete traumatic spinal cord transection
Next Step

Neurosurgical/spine consultation for stabilization; prognosis for neurologic recovery below the level of complete transection is poor.

3. Traumatic disc herniation, MRI

Concerning

Acute disc herniation from trauma, which can cause or worsen cord/nerve root compression, particularly relevant before any planned reduction of a fracture-dislocation.

Imaging Characteristics

Acute herniated disc material at the level of injury, assessed particularly to exclude cord compression before any closed reduction maneuver of an associated fracture-dislocation.

Differential
  • Traumatic disc herniation (important to identify before reduction attempts)
Next Step

MRI before any closed reduction of a cervical fracture-dislocation to assess for a herniated disc that could worsen cord compression during the maneuver.

4. Posterior ligamentous complex injury, MRI

Concerning

Disruption of the posterior spinal ligaments (supraspinous, interspinous, ligamentum flavum, facet capsules), a key determinant of spinal stability after trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense signal (edema/disruption) within the expected course of the posterior ligamentous complex, sometimes with frank discontinuity.

Differential
  • Posterior ligamentous complex injury (affects stability classification, e.g., TLICS scoring for thoracolumbar injuries)
Next Step

Spine surgery consultation; MRI ligamentous assessment is a key input into surgical versus non-surgical management decisions.

5. Epidural hematoma, spine

Concerning

Bleeding into the spinal epidural space, from trauma, anticoagulation, or post-procedural (post-epidural/spinal anesthesia) causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Epidural collection, often biconvex, with signal characteristics evolving similarly to intracranial hemorrhage, causing variable thecal sac/cord compression.

Differential
  • Traumatic spinal epidural hematoma
  • Spontaneous (anticoagulation-related)
  • Post-procedural (post-epidural anesthesia/lumbar puncture)
Next Step

Emergent neurosurgical/spine consultation, particularly with any neurologic deficit, given the time-sensitivity of decompression for neurologic recovery.

6. SCIWORA, MRI findings

Concerning

Spinal cord injury without radiographic (CT) abnormality, with MRI as the essential modality to demonstrate the cord injury.

Imaging Characteristics

Cord T2 signal change (edema, or hemorrhage on susceptibility sequences) and/or ligamentous injury in a patient with a normal CT, more common in children given greater spinal elasticity.

Differential
  • SCIWORA (classic in pediatric patients)
  • Transient spinal cord concussion (milder, may show no persistent signal change)
Next Step

MRI is essential given normal CT does not exclude significant cord injury; spine immobilization and neurosurgical/spine consultation.

7. Cord compression from fracture fragment

Concerning

Direct spinal cord compression from a retropulsed bone fragment after a burst fracture.

Imaging Characteristics

Retropulsed vertebral body fragment causing direct cord compression and abnormal cord T2 signal at the level of impingement.

Differential
  • Burst fracture with cord compression (surgical emergency with neurologic deficit)
Next Step

Emergent spine surgery consultation for decompression given the direct mechanical compression.

8. Vertebral artery injury, cervical trauma

Concerning

Injury to the vertebral artery associated with cervical spine trauma, particularly fractures involving the transverse foramen.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal vessel caliber, intramural hematoma, or occlusion of the vertebral artery on MRA, particularly relevant with fractures extending through the transverse foramen (increased injury risk).

Differential
  • Blunt cerebrovascular (vertebral artery) injury
Next Step

Vascular/neurology consultation; antithrombotic therapy per current blunt cerebrovascular injury management guidelines to reduce stroke risk.

Spine: Congenital & Developmental

6 findings

1. Tethered spinal cord, MRI

Concerning

Abnormally low conus medullaris position with a thickened or fatty filum terminale, restricting normal cord movement.

Imaging Characteristics

Conus medullaris positioned below the normal L1-L2 level (age-dependent in children), often with an associated thickened (>2 mm) or fatty filum terminale.

Differential
  • Tethered cord syndrome
  • Associated with spinal dysraphism
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral, particularly with progressive neurologic symptoms, bowel/bladder dysfunction, or orthopedic foot deformity.

2. Syringomyelia

Concerning

Fluid-filled cavity within the spinal cord, from a range of causes including Chiari malformation, trauma, and tumor.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, CSF-signal cavity within the spinal cord parenchyma, which can be focal or extend over multiple levels.

Differential
  • Chiari-associated syrinx (most common)
  • Post-traumatic syrinx
  • Tumor-associated syrinx
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral; identify and address the underlying cause (e.g., posterior fossa decompression for Chiari-associated syrinx).

3. Diastematomyelia (split cord malformation)

Concerning

Congenital splitting of the spinal cord into two hemicords, often with an intervening bony or fibrous septum.

Imaging Characteristics

Sagittal splitting of the spinal cord into two hemicords, each with its own central canal, sometimes with a bony or cartilaginous spur dividing the two.

Differential
  • Type I (single dural sac, bony/cartilaginous septum)
  • Type II (two dural sacs, fibrous septum)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral, particularly if associated with tethering or a bony spur causing symptoms.

4. Lipomyelomeningocele

Concerning

Congenital spinal dysraphism with a lipoma extending through a posterior spinal defect and attaching to a low-lying, tethered spinal cord.

Imaging Characteristics

Fat signal (T1-hyperintense) extending from the subcutaneous tissue through a posterior spinal defect and attaching to the spinal cord, which is typically low-lying/tethered.

Differential
  • Lipomyelomeningocele (closed spinal dysraphism)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral for evaluation of prophylactic or symptomatic untethering surgery.

5. Spinal dysraphism spectrum, MRI

Concerning

General term for congenital defects of neural tube closure, ranging from open (myelomeningocele) to closed (spina bifida occulta with various associated anomalies) forms.

Imaging Characteristics

Findings range from an overt open neural placode (myelomeningocele) to more subtle closed dysraphism findings (dermal sinus tract, lipoma, tethered cord) depending on the specific subtype.

Differential
  • Open spinal dysraphism (myelomeningocele)
  • Closed spinal dysraphism (various subtypes)
Next Step

Pediatric neurosurgery referral for comprehensive evaluation and management planning.

6. Filum terminale lipoma

Variable

Fatty infiltration of the filum terminale, which can be an isolated incidental finding or associated with cord tethering.

Imaging Characteristics

Fat signal (T1-hyperintense, suppressing on fat-saturated sequences) within the filum terminale, with or without an associated low-lying conus.

Differential
  • Isolated filum lipoma (often incidental if the conus position is normal)
  • Filum lipoma with tethered cord (if the conus is low-lying)
Next Step

Neurosurgical referral if associated with a low conus or tethered cord symptoms; isolated incidental findings without tethering are often observed.

Spine: Inflammatory & Demyelinating

5 findings

1. Transverse myelitis

Concerning

Inflammatory spinal cord disorder, idiopathic or associated with an underlying systemic/autoimmune condition.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense signal within the spinal cord, typically spanning less than 3 vertebral segments in classic (non-NMOSD) transverse myelitis, with variable enhancement and cord swelling in the acute phase.

Differential
  • Idiopathic transverse myelitis
  • Multiple sclerosis-associated (shorter segment)
  • Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder-associated (longitudinally extensive, ≥3 segments)
  • Post-infectious/para-infectious
Next Step

Neurology referral; MRI brain and serologic testing (aquaporin-4, MOG antibodies) to help classify the underlying etiology.

2. MS spinal cord plaque

Concerning

Demyelinating lesion within the spinal cord from multiple sclerosis, typically shorter segment and more peripheral than NMOSD lesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal, typically <2 vertebral segments in length, peripherally located T2-hyperintense lesion within the spinal cord, most often cervical.

Differential
  • Multiple sclerosis spinal cord plaque
  • Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (longer segment, more central, key differential)
Next Step

Neurology referral as part of overall MS diagnostic workup.

3. Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, spinal involvement

Concerning

Autoimmune demyelinating disease with characteristic longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions.

Imaging Characteristics

Longitudinally extensive T2-hyperintense lesion spanning ≥3 vertebral segments, often centrally located within the cord, with associated cord swelling in the acute phase.

Differential
  • Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (longitudinally extensive lesion is a key diagnostic feature)
  • Acute transverse myelitis of other etiology
Next Step

Neurology referral; aquaporin-4 antibody testing given important treatment differences from MS.

4. Sarcoid myelopathy

Concerning

Spinal cord involvement by neurosarcoidosis, an uncommon but important cause of myelopathy.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense cord signal change, often with a characteristic 'trident sign' on axial imaging and leptomeningeal/nodular enhancement.

Differential
  • Neurosarcoidosis (correlate with systemic sarcoidosis and chest imaging)
  • Other inflammatory/demyelinating myelopathy
Next Step

Neurology/rheumatology referral; correlate with chest imaging and systemic sarcoidosis workup.

5. Spinal cord infarct

Concerning

Ischemic injury to the spinal cord, uncommon but important, classically affecting the anterior spinal artery territory.

Imaging Characteristics

T2-hyperintense signal within the cord, often affecting the anterior two-thirds (anterior spinal artery territory) with a characteristic 'owl eyes' or 'snake eyes' appearance on axial images from bilateral anterior horn involvement, with restricted diffusion in the acute phase.

Differential
  • Spinal cord infarct (aortic surgery/dissection is a classic risk factor)
  • Acute transverse myelitis (differentiate by distribution pattern and clinical context)
Next Step

Neurology/vascular surgery referral; correlate with aortic disease/surgery history and cardiovascular risk factors.

5. Periventricular leukomalacia

Concerning

Ischemic injury to the periventricular white matter in preterm infants, a major cause of cerebral palsy.

Imaging Characteristics

Early: increased periventricular echogenicity (echodensity), which can be subtle and easily mistaken for normal periventricular halo. Late: periventricular cystic changes (cystic PVL) developing over 2-4 weeks.

Differential
  • Periventricular leukomalacia (classic in preterm infants)
  • Normal periventricular halo/'blush' (bilaterally symmetric, less intense, key differential from early PVL)
Next Step

Serial cranial ultrasound over several weeks to assess for cystic evolution; neurodevelopmental follow-up given cerebral palsy risk.

6. Hydrocephalus, neonatal ultrasound

Concerning

Ventricular dilation in the neonate, from post-hemorrhagic, congenital, or other causes.

Imaging Characteristics

Dilated lateral ventricles with a ventricular index or anterior horn width exceeding normal values for gestational/postnatal age, sometimes with a rounded (rather than normal slit-like) anterior horn contour.

Differential
  • Post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus (following IVH)
  • Congenital hydrocephalus (aqueductal stenosis and others)
Next Step

Serial measurements to monitor progression; neurosurgical consultation for symptomatic or progressive ventriculomegaly.

7. Choroid plexus cyst, neonatal

Benign

Common benign cystic finding within the choroid plexus, often detected prenatally.

Imaging Characteristics

Well-defined, anechoic cystic structure within the choroid plexus.

Differential
  • Isolated choroid plexus cyst (typically resolves and is of no clinical significance in isolation)
Next Step

No action needed for an isolated finding without other soft markers; correlate with prenatal genetic screening context if applicable.

8. Cavum septum pellucidum, neonatal (normal)

Benign

Normal midline CSF-filled cavity, present in essentially all neonates and often persisting into infancy.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, midline cavity between the frontal horns of the lateral ventricles.

Differential
  • Normal finding in neonates and young infants
Next Step

No action needed.

9. Subependymal cyst, neonatal

Benign

Small cyst along the ventricular margin, often from resolved germinal matrix hemorrhage or a benign developmental cause.

Imaging Characteristics

Small, anechoic cystic structure along the caudothalamic groove or ventricular margin.

Differential
  • Resolved germinal matrix hemorrhage (most common in this location)
  • Congenital/infectious (correlate with TORCH screening if multiple or atypical)
Next Step

Usually incidental and benign; correlate with clinical context if multiple or associated with other findings.

10. Ventriculomegaly, neonatal (non-hydrocephalic)

Variable

Enlarged ventricles without the clinical/pressure features of true hydrocephalus, sometimes from volume loss.

Imaging Characteristics

Enlarged ventricles, which can reflect either active hydrocephalus or ex vacuo dilation from parenchymal volume loss/injury.

Differential
  • Ex vacuo ventriculomegaly (from parenchymal injury/volume loss)
  • Early or compensated hydrocephalus
Next Step

Correlate with serial measurements and head circumference trend; neurology/neurosurgery referral if progressive.

11. Hypoxic-ischemic injury pattern, neonatal ultrasound

Concerning

Brain injury from perinatal hypoxia-ischemia, with a pattern that differs between term and preterm infants.

Imaging Characteristics

Term infants: increased echogenicity of the basal ganglia/thalami and cortex in severe injury. Preterm infants: predominantly periventricular white matter involvement (overlapping with PVL).

Differential
  • Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (term pattern)
  • Periventricular injury pattern (preterm)
Next Step

Neurology referral; MRI (including diffusion-weighted imaging) provides more sensitive and prognostically useful characterization than ultrasound alone.

12. Neonatal cerebral infarct, ultrasound

Concerning

Focal ischemic injury in the neonate, most often in the MCA territory.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal area of increased echogenicity in a vascular territory distribution, which can be subtle on ultrasound compared to MRI.

Differential
  • Neonatal arterial ischemic stroke (often MCA territory)
Next Step

MRI (including diffusion-weighted imaging) for more sensitive and definitive characterization; neurology referral.

13. Vein of Galen malformation

Concerning

Congenital vascular malformation causing high-flow arteriovenous shunting into a dilated median prosencephalic vein, presenting with high-output heart failure in neonates.

Imaging Characteristics

Large, anechoic midline structure posterior to the thalami with turbulent, high-velocity flow on color/spectral Doppler.

Differential
  • Vein of Galen malformation (classic association with neonatal high-output cardiac failure)
Next Step

Emergent neurointerventional and cardiology consultation given the risk of high-output heart failure.

14. Lenticulostriate vasculopathy

Variable

Increased echogenicity along the course of the lenticulostriate vessels in the basal ganglia, a nonspecific finding with a broad differential.

Imaging Characteristics

Linear or branching echogenic foci within the basal ganglia/thalami following the expected course of the lenticulostriate arteries.

Differential
  • Congenital infection (TORCH)
  • Chromosomal abnormalities
  • Perinatal hypoxic-ischemic injury
  • Idiopathic/incidental (a substantial proportion of cases have no identifiable cause and a benign course)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context and consider TORCH/genetic workup if other risk factors or findings are present.

Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter / ICP Assessment

4 findings

1. Elevated optic nerve sheath diameter

Concerning

Point-of-care ultrasound surrogate marker for elevated intracranial pressure.

Imaging Characteristics

Optic nerve sheath diameter measured 3 mm posterior to the globe, with a value exceeding the commonly cited threshold (approximately 5-6 mm in adults, with some variation by population and technique) suggesting elevated ICP.

Differential
  • Elevated intracranial pressure (mass lesion, hemorrhage, hydrocephalus, idiopathic intracranial hypertension)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical presentation; further neuroimaging (CT/MRI) to identify the underlying cause of elevated ICP if suspected.

2. Normal optic nerve sheath diameter

Benign

Optic nerve sheath diameter within the expected normal range, an appropriately reassuring finding in the correct clinical context.

Imaging Characteristics

Optic nerve sheath diameter below the threshold suggestive of elevated ICP, measured 3 mm posterior to the globe.

Differential
  • Normal ICP (though a normal ONSD does not entirely exclude mild or early elevation)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical picture; a normal measurement is reassuring but should be interpreted alongside the overall clinical assessment.

3. Papilledema, ultrasound correlate

Concerning

Sonographic assessment of the optic disc, which can show elevation/bulging corresponding to clinical papilledema.

Imaging Characteristics

Anterior bulging of the optic disc into the posterior globe, sometimes with an associated '30-degree test' positive result (reduction in ONSD with lateral gaze suggesting true papilledema-associated distension rather than a fixed anatomic variant).

Differential
  • Papilledema from elevated ICP
Next Step

Correlate with formal fundoscopic examination and further neuroimaging as clinically indicated.

4. Optic nerve sheath measurement technique pitfalls

Variable

Recognized sources of error in ONSD measurement that can affect clinical interpretation.

Imaging Characteristics

Common pitfalls include measuring too close to or too far from the globe (not exactly at the 3 mm mark), oblique rather than perpendicular imaging planes, and excessive probe pressure altering the measurement.

Differential
  • Technical measurement error rather than a true abnormality
Next Step

Ensure standardized technique (3 mm posterior to the globe, perpendicular imaging plane, minimal probe pressure) before clinical interpretation.

Carotid & Transcranial Doppler

8 findings

1. Carotid stenosis, grading by duplex criteria

Concerning

Narrowing of the internal carotid artery, graded by peak systolic velocity and other duplex Doppler criteria, relevant to stroke risk stratification.

Imaging Characteristics

Elevated peak systolic velocity (and, at higher grades, elevated end-diastolic velocity and ICA/CCA ratio) correlating with percent stenosis per validated duplex criteria (e.g., >70% stenosis criteria).

Differential
  • Mild stenosis (<50%, typically managed medically)
  • Moderate stenosis (50-69%)
  • Severe stenosis (≥70%, may warrant revascularization per symptomatic status and surgical risk)
Next Step

Vascular surgery/neurology referral for revascularization discussion (endarterectomy or stenting) based on the degree of stenosis and symptom status.

2. Carotid plaque characterization

Variable

Assessment of carotid plaque morphology, which can carry information about stroke risk beyond stenosis degree alone.

Imaging Characteristics

Plaque echogenicity (echolucent/hypoechoic plaques are considered less stable) and surface characteristics (irregular or ulcerated surface suggests higher embolic risk).

Differential
  • Stable, homogeneous, echogenic plaque
  • Unstable, heterogeneous, echolucent, or ulcerated plaque (higher embolic risk)
Next Step

Correlate with degree of stenosis and symptom status for overall stroke risk stratification.

3. Carotid occlusion, ultrasound

Concerning

Complete occlusion of the internal carotid artery, with absent flow on duplex Doppler.

Imaging Characteristics

Absent color and spectral Doppler flow within the ICA lumen, often with a 'thump' or minimal-flow pattern in the proximal stump on the common carotid artery.

Differential
  • Chronic carotid occlusion
  • Acute carotid occlusion (correlate with acute stroke symptoms)
Next Step

Correlate with acuity and stroke symptoms; CT/MR angiography for confirmation and collateral assessment.

4. Vertebral artery flow reversal (subclavian steal)

Variable

Reversed vertebral artery flow direction from proximal subclavian artery stenosis/occlusion, causing the vertebral artery to supply the arm at the expense of posterior circulation flow.

Imaging Characteristics

Reversed (retrograde) flow direction in the vertebral artery on spectral Doppler, sometimes provoked or accentuated with arm exercise.

Differential
  • Subclavian steal physiology (symptomatic if causing vertebrobasilar insufficiency symptoms, particularly with arm use)
Next Step

Vascular surgery referral if symptomatic; often an incidental finding if asymptomatic.

5. Carotid dissection, Doppler signs

Concerning

Carotid artery wall tear, with duplex ultrasound findings that can be subtle and less definitive than MRI/CTA.

Imaging Characteristics

Abnormal, often high-resistance or dampened waveform, sometimes with a visible intimal flap or tapered narrowing of the lumen; ultrasound has lower sensitivity than CTA/MRA for this diagnosis.

Differential
  • Carotid artery dissection
Next Step

CTA or MRA for definitive characterization given limited ultrasound sensitivity; neurology/vascular referral.

6. Vasospasm monitoring, transcranial Doppler

Concerning

Elevated intracranial arterial flow velocities on transcranial Doppler, used for serial monitoring of vasospasm risk after aneurysmal SAH.

Imaging Characteristics

Elevated mean flow velocity (commonly >120 cm/s as a general threshold, with trends and the Lindegaard ratio adding specificity) within the MCA or other major intracranial vessels.

Differential
  • Vasospasm following aneurysmal SAH (classic timing days 3-14)
  • Hyperemia (differentiated using the Lindegaard ratio comparing intracranial to extracranial velocities)
Next Step

Neurocritical care correlation; serial monitoring is a common adjunct to clinical assessment and CT angiography in SAH management.

7. Microembolic signal detection, transcranial Doppler

Variable

Detection of transient high-intensity signals on TCD representing microemboli passing through an insonated vessel, used in stroke and perioperative monitoring.

Imaging Characteristics

Brief, high-intensity transient signals within the Doppler spectral waveform, distinct from the background flow signal, representing passing microemboli.

Differential
  • Embolic source (cardiac, carotid, or other) warranting further workup
  • Air microemboli (in a procedural/surgical monitoring context)
Next Step

Correlate with clinical context; further embolic source workup (echocardiography, carotid imaging) if clinically indicated.

8. Carotid web

Concerning

Shelf-like intimal projection at the carotid bulb, an underrecognized cause of cryptogenic stroke, particularly in younger patients.

Imaging Characteristics

Thin, shelf-like linear projection into the carotid bulb lumen on ultrasound, though CTA is generally preferred for definitive characterization given its subtlety.

Differential
  • Carotid web (associated with fibromuscular dysplasia spectrum)
  • Atherosclerotic plaque (differentiate by morphology and patient age/risk factor profile)
Next Step

CTA for definitive characterization if suspected; vascular surgery/neurology referral, particularly in a younger patient with cryptogenic stroke.

Pediatric Spinal Ultrasound

5 findings

1. Spinal dysraphism screening, infant spine ultrasound

Concerning

Screening ultrasound for occult spinal dysraphism in infants with cutaneous stigmata (dimple, hairy patch, hemangioma), performed while the posterior elements remain incompletely ossified.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of conus medullaris position, filum terminale thickness, and any associated mass (lipoma) or tract communicating with the skin surface.

Differential
  • Normal spinal anatomy
  • Tethered cord/occult spinal dysraphism (if abnormal conus position or filum thickening identified)
Next Step

MRI for further characterization if ultrasound is abnormal or equivocal, or if the ultrasound window becomes inadequate (typically after 4-6 months of age as ossification progresses).

2. Tethered cord screening, ultrasound

Concerning

Ultrasound assessment of conus medullaris position and movement to screen for cord tethering in young infants.

Imaging Characteristics

Low-lying conus position and/or reduced normal cord pulsatility/movement with cardiac and respiratory motion on real-time imaging.

Differential
  • Tethered spinal cord
Next Step

MRI for definitive characterization; neurosurgical referral if tethering is confirmed or strongly suspected.

3. Sacral dimple evaluation, ultrasound

Variable

Ultrasound assessment of a sacral dimple to screen for an underlying occult spinal dysraphism, with risk stratified by dimple characteristics.

Imaging Characteristics

Assessment of whether the dimple's base can be visualized (a low-risk, simple dimple) versus features suggesting a deeper tract, associated mass, or abnormal cord position (higher-risk features).

Differential
  • Simple sacral dimple (low risk, base visualized, typically no further workup needed)
  • Atypical dimple with concerning features (deep, associated with other cutaneous stigmata, or with an abnormal cord/filum)
Next Step

Reassurance for a simple, low-risk dimple; MRI referral for atypical or high-risk features.

4. Filum terminale assessment, pediatric ultrasound

Variable

Ultrasound evaluation of filum terminale thickness as part of tethered cord screening.

Imaging Characteristics

Filum terminale thickness measured, with thickening (or fatty change) raising concern for a fatty filum contributing to cord tethering.

Differential
  • Normal filum terminale
  • Thickened/fatty filum terminale (associated with tethered cord)
Next Step

MRI for further characterization if abnormal.

5. Syrinx, pediatric spinal ultrasound

Concerning

Fluid-filled cavity within the spinal cord, occasionally identified on screening infant spine ultrasound.

Imaging Characteristics

Anechoic, tubular or fusiform cavity within the spinal cord on longitudinal imaging.

Differential
  • Syringohydromyelia (correlate with Chiari malformation or other underlying cause)
Next Step

MRI for definitive characterization and to identify the underlying cause; neurosurgical referral.

Miscellaneous

4 findings

1. Cephalohematoma vs. caput succedaneum, ultrasound

Benign

Distinguishing two common birth-related scalp/skull findings using ultrasound, though the diagnosis is often clinical.

Imaging Characteristics

Cephalohematoma: subperiosteal fluid collection confined by suture lines, does not cross sutures. Caput succedaneum: diffuse subcutaneous edema that does cross suture lines and is typically present at birth (resolving within days) rather than appearing after.

Differential
  • Cephalohematoma (subperiosteal, suture-limited)
  • Caput succedaneum (subcutaneous, crosses sutures, resolves faster)
Next Step

Usually managed conservatively; large or expanding cephalohematomas warrant monitoring for underlying skull fracture or, rarely, significant blood loss.

2. Infant skull fracture, ultrasound

Concerning

Skull fracture detection using point-of-care ultrasound in infants, an emerging alternative to reduce radiation in low-risk head trauma.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic linear discontinuity in the normally continuous hyperechoic cortical bone surface, distinguished from a suture by lack of the expected anatomic location and serrated suture pattern.

Differential
  • Linear skull fracture
  • Normal suture (correlate with expected anatomic location)
Next Step

CT remains the gold standard for definitive characterization and assessment of underlying intracranial injury if there is clinical concern.

3. Temporal artery ultrasound (giant cell arteritis)

Concerning

Ultrasound assessment of the temporal artery for the 'halo sign,' supporting a diagnosis of giant cell arteritis.

Imaging Characteristics

Hypoechoic, concentric wall thickening ('halo sign') surrounding the temporal artery lumen, which may partially or fully resolve with treatment, and vessel compressibility assessment.

Differential
  • Giant cell arteritis (halo sign is supportive but temporal artery biopsy remains an important complementary diagnostic tool)
  • Atherosclerotic wall thickening (typically less concentric/uniform)
Next Step

Rheumatology/ophthalmology urgent referral; do not delay corticosteroid treatment for definitive diagnostic testing given the risk of irreversible vision loss.

4. Peripheral nerve ultrasound (entrapment neuropathy)

Variable

Ultrasound assessment of peripheral nerves for entrapment, most commonly the median nerve at the carpal tunnel.

Imaging Characteristics

Focal nerve enlargement (increased cross-sectional area) proximal to or at the site of entrapment, sometimes with flattening of the nerve within a tight anatomic tunnel.

Differential
  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve at the wrist, most common)
  • Cubital tunnel syndrome (ulnar nerve at the elbow)
  • Other focal entrapment neuropathies
Next Step

Correlate with clinical exam and electrodiagnostic studies (EMG/NCS); neurology or hand surgery referral for confirmed entrapment.
